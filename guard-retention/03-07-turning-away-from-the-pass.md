# Turning Away From The Pass

## Overview

Every retention method covered so far involves turning **toward** the opponent as he passes. This lesson covers the exception: situations where turning toward him isn't realistic (he's pressuring forward hard enough that turning in gets you flattened), and you instead have to turn **away** and manage the resulting back-exposure risk.

---

## The general rule and its exception

- Fundamental rule across combat sports: never turn your back on your opponent. Turning toward him is always the preferred, safer option when it's available.
- Exception: if he's pressuring forward hard enough that turning into him gets you flattened/pinned, you may have to go **with** the direction of his passing force instead of against it — turning away.
- The real risk: turning away exposes your back, and a good opponent can capitalize by taking hooks and finishing a back take instead. This happens even at world championship level.

## The risk/reward decision

- **Points competition, short match, can't afford to concede pass points:** turning away to prevent the pass may be worth the back-exposure risk.
- **Submission-only or no-points format:** it usually doesn't make sense to turn away — better to just accept being passed and pinned, then escape the pin and return to guard, rather than risk giving up your back.
- This is a deliberate risk assessment, not a default technique — decide based on the ruleset and match situation before committing to it.

## How to turn away safely (minimize back exposure)

1. Build an initial frame — **cross frame**, forearm across his collarbones.
2. As he comes in, **elbow prop away** from him (building height/space).
3. The fastest way to reduce back exposure is to **put your back on the ground** — this creates distance rather than staying in a vulnerable turned-and-rising position.
4. Once distance is created, **roll into the gap** — bring your body underneath him and back through into guard.

### Variation depending on his approach
- If he comes in and you can get your hips underneath him after propping up, do that directly to reset guard.
- If he's actively chasing your movement (more realistic, competition-like scenario): frame, come up to an elbow, and when he tries to stick to you and take your back, use that same walking/framing motion to bring your legs back around into guard rather than letting him settle on your back.

## General movement pattern

Frame → gain height (elbow or hand prop) → create space by putting your back down or walking away → push into the space you created → guard re-established.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Turning toward the opponent is always preferred; turning away is a **deliberate, situational choice**, not a default.
- Match format matters: worth the back-exposure risk in short points matches where conceding a pass is costly; usually not worth it in submission-only/no-points formats.
- If you do turn away: cross frame → elbow prop away → back to the ground (fastest way to cut back exposure) → roll into the created gap.
- The instructor personally still prefers shoulder-rolling **into** the opponent over turning away and rolling out — turning away is the fallback when turning in isn't realistic.
