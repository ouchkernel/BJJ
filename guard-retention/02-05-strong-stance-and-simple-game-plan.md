# Getting A Solid Start To Guard Retention With A Strong Stance And A Simple Game Plan

## Overview

Builds directly on the earlier stance lesson, adding the mechanical trick for *holding* that stance under pressure (self-framing) and a simple, opening game plan (two-on-one gripping) for the first few seconds of engagement.

---

## The 4-5 elements of stance (recap + extension)

1. **Compact** — the more extended, the worse. Analogy: a good boxer stays compact in front of an opponent.
2. **Rounded spine** — shape of a rocking chair / banana, whether seated or supine. A rounded spine lets you carry an opponent's weight efficiently (the rocking chair is called "one of the greatest weight-carrying devices in human history") and turn/pivot/spin on your shoulders with minimal mat friction. A flat spine creates so much friction you can't move at all.
3. **Knees close to chest.**
4. **Elbows close to knees.**
5. **Elbows outside the knees** (in supine position, almost always; in seated position it's acceptable to go inside sometimes). Exception: when reaching across the body, one elbow will naturally go inside a knee — that's fine, it's the resting position that matters.

Why elbow/knee connection matters for weight-bearing: with knee-elbow connection, an opponent's weight gets distributed onto the frame instead of purely onto your chest — demonstrated by being able to bear an opponent's weight easily with connection, versus getting flattened immediately without it.

---

## Self-framing — the mechanism that makes stance holdable under pressure

Two methods:

### Pulling method
- Hands on your own shins, or wrists hooked inside your own knees, pulling the knees to the chest.
- Preference: use the **wrists** rather than gripping the shins directly — this keeps the hands free to grip the opponent when needed.
- Effect: if the opponent grabs your feet and pulls, self-framing prevents your legs from extending and your knees from leaving your chest. If he instead pulls your feet toward the floor, the pulling frame actually helps you sit up.
- Use it **surreptitiously/intelligently** — grip when he's attacking your stance, release when he's not. Don't hold on rigidly regardless of what's happening.

### Pushing method
- Elbows push off the mat, hands push gently into the outside of your own legs/knees.
- Effect: massively widens your base. Base width without this = just the width of your hips (easy to throw your legs to a side). Base width with this = the width of your two elbows — much wider, putting a hard limit on how far an opponent can throw your legs before you can recover.

### Floor framing (a third category)
- Instead of framing your own body, you frame the floor directly (no self-contact) — used when you need to reorient with **no contact** to the opponent at all.
- Example use: if your knees end up pointing the wrong way relative to the opponent, frame the floor and kick to reorient your knees back toward him, then move into your counters.

## The simple opening game plan: two-on-one gripping

- Key fact this is built on: **almost every effective guard pass in no-gi requires the use of two hands.** Passing with one hand is technically possible but difficult and low-percentage.
- Therefore: get a **two-on-one grip** on one of the opponent's arms as your opening move. This nullifies one of his hands — he literally cannot begin a real pass attempt until he breaks the grip.
- **Which two-on-one to use depends on his hand height:**
  - Hands **high** (above your chest) → two-on-one **wrist**, thumbs pointing **up**.
  - Hands **low** (down near your legs) → two-on-one **wrist**, thumbs pointing **down**, or two-on-one **elbow**.
  - Either wrist grip can be upgraded into a two-on-one **elbow** grip, described as inherently superior.
- Effect: while you hold the two-on-one, the opponent is forced to react to *you* — this is called being in an **offensive cycle** (he reacts to you) instead of a **defensive cycle** (you react to him). As he fights to free his arm, that opening is your chance to go into offense from bottom.
- Applies whether you're seated or supine, but seated is where you can most reliably initiate it first (you can sit up and get to grips before he gets to grips with you).

## The mobility problem when you go from seated to supine

- Seated: you retain meaningful mobility even against a standing opponent.
- Supine (flattened to your back): the opponent — especially if standing — now has a **major** mobility advantage over you.
- **The #1 mistake this creates in developing students:** reaching to compensate for the mobility gap. Trying to chase/grab a fast-moving opponent causes you to overextend and abandon your stance (the exact compactness principles above).
- Analogy given: an untrained fighter in a street fight instinctively reaches for punching hands and gets knocked out; a trained boxer stays compact, lets the punches come to him, and counters from a solid base. Same principle applies here.
- **Rule: never sacrifice your stance to chase mobility. Let him come to you.** Stay compact, keep elbow-knee connection, and use self-framing so that even when the opponent throws your legs to a side, movement is bounded and recoverable rather than a full loss of position.

---

## Summary Game Plan

- **Seated:** start with two-on-one gripping immediately (wrist if hands are high/low accordingly, or elbow). Forces the opponent into a reactive, defensive posture before he can even begin passing.
- **If put supine:** stance comes first, everything else second. Self-frame (pulling or pushing method), stay compact, don't reach — let the opponent come to you and respond from a solid, bounded position rather than chasing him.

## Key Takeaways

- Stance = compact, rounded spine, knees to chest, elbows to knees, elbows outside knees (supine).
- Self-framing (pulling with the wrists/hands, or pushing with the elbows+hands) is what makes that stance survivable under an opponent's pressure — pulling prevents extension, pushing widens your effective base.
- Floor framing reorients your body with zero contact to the opponent, useful when your knees end up pointing the wrong way.
- Two-on-one gripping is the default opening move: almost no effective pass works with only one free hand, so denying him a second hand puts him in a defensive, reactive cycle immediately.
- Going supine costs you a major mobility advantage relative to a standing opponent — the fix is never to chase/reach for him, but to stay in stance and let him come to you, the same way a boxer counters rather than lunges.
