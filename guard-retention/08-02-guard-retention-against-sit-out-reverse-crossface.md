# Guard Retention Against A Sit-Out Reverse Crossface

## Overview

Second sit-out variant. The reverse crossface is presented as a stronger, more preferred no-gi head-control grip than the standard crossface covered previously (in a gi context, standard crossface + lapel would be stronger, but no-gi favors the reverse crossface). Same golden rule applies (control hip on two knees/prone, control head on sit-out), but the escape mechanics differ because of how the grip locks the head.

---

## What makes the reverse crossface strong

- From half guard, the opponent takes his arm over your head and sits out, using the full strength of his lat muscle to pull his elbow back — this compresses and locks your head in place.
- Once locked, you can't move your head backward at all; from there it's easy for him to work your legs and pass in any direction.
- No-gi, this is considered a stronger, more commonly preferred grip among skilled half guard passers than the standard crossface.

## The escape: the "backhand frame"

Same underlying philosophy as the crossface lesson — free the head — but a different technical detail because the reverse crossface locks the head differently.

1. Take your free hand and place the **back of your hand** on the opponent's chest, close to his armpit, fingers poking through the gap.
2. Keep your elbow **high** — don't let him pin your elbow down (if he sits/leans on it, the frame is neutralized). You want the top of your elbow still visible from the front.
3. With the grip locked tight, start a small **kipping** motion with both feet, driving off a knee placed in his hip.
4. Simultaneously bring your chin to your chest — reduces how much purchase he has on the back of your head.
5. As you kip, his grip slips off "like magic."
6. Immediately put your free hand/forearm in front of his elbow to take inside position, preventing him from re-grabbing your head — he'd have to come back up to his knees to try again.
7. The moment he comes up to his knees, put a foot on his hip and shimmy your knee in front — guard restored.

## The "tight sweater" analogy

To execute the kip/chin-tuck correctly: think of taking off a tight sweater — chin down to chest, shrug, and it comes off. Same motion here to slip the reverse crossface grip.

---

## Common Mistakes

- Letting your elbow drop low enough that the opponent can sit or lean his weight on it — neutralizes the backhand frame entirely.
- Trying to power through the reverse crossface with strength instead of the kip + chin-tuck combination.
- Not re-establishing inside position (forearm in front of his elbow) immediately after slipping the grip — lets him simply reset the same crossface a second time.

## Decision Points

- **Opponent takes a reverse crossface (arm over head, sits out, pulls with lat strength)** → backhand frame: back of hand on his chest near armpit, elbow high.
- **Grip is locked tight** → kip off a knee in his hip + tuck chin to chest simultaneously to slip the grip.
- **Grip slips off** → immediately take inside position with your forearm before he can re-grab.
- **He comes back up to his knees to try again** → foot to his hip, knee shimmies in front, guard restored.

## Terminology Introduced

- **Reverse crossface** — an arm-over-the-head crossface variant that uses lat strength to compress and lock the head; considered the strongest no-gi head control of the sit-out family (stronger than a plain crossface without a gi).
- **Backhand frame** — placing the back of the hand on the opponent's chest near the armpit with a high elbow, used to create a lever to kip the reverse crossface off.
