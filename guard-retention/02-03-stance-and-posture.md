# Stance And Posture For Guard Retention

## Overview

First concrete, actionable piece of the course: how to hold your body *before* contact even happens, to make passing harder from the start.

## The core rule: compactness

- General principle: the more compact your body, the better your retention.
- Beginner mistake #1: extended body, straight spine — knees drifting away from the chest, and (once the opponent gets to North-South) elbows drifting away from the knees.
- **Two foundational posture rules:**
  1. Elbows and knees as close together as possible.
  2. Knees as close to the chest as possible.
- The further elbows/knees separate, the more open space you present for the opponent to insert a knee or get chest-to-chest. The further your knees drift from your chest, the more vulnerable you are to simple throw-bys that misdirect your legs.

## Foot position

- Presenting symmetrical feet (soles pointing straight down, both feet the same height/distance) makes it trivially easy for an opponent to snatch both legs at once and get a strong start on the pass.
- Fix: point the soles of your feet **at** the opponent, and keep the feet **asymmetrical** — if he grabs one, he only gets one, not both together.

## Elbow position relative to knees

- General rule: elbows **outside** the knees. Elbows inside the knees is a structurally weak position to defend guard from.

## Body orientation relative to the opponent

- Facing an opponent flat/squared-on gives him the choice of passing either left or right — you don't know which side to defend.
- Presenting **one side** of your body at roughly a 45° oblique angle removes that ambiguity: he can now only reasonably pass on the side you're presenting (running all the way to the far side is too far to be efficient for him).
- This lets you "load up" all your defenses — frames, mobility — on the one side you know he has to attack, instead of splitting attention across both sides.

## Why the rounded spine matters mechanically

- A straight spine creates more friction with the mat and less capacity for movement.
- A rounded spine (described as the shape of a rocking chair or a banana) reduces mat friction and allows faster, freer movement.

## Key Takeaways

- Two non-negotiable posture rules: **elbows to knees, knees to chest.**
- Keep feet asymmetrical and pointed at the opponent — never present two symmetrical, grabbable feet.
- Elbows should be outside your knees, not inside.
- Present one side of your body at an oblique angle so you always know which side the opponent has to commit to passing on, and stack your defenses there.
- Spine should stay rounded (rocking-chair/banana shape) — a straight spine increases mat friction and kills mobility.
