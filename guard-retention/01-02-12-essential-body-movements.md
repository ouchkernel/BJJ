# The 12 Essential Body Movements For Guard Retention

## Overview

The prerequisite movement vocabulary for the whole course. These 12 solo-drillable movements underpin every technique taught later — meant to be drilled daily as a warm-up (described as far more relevant than generic warm-ups like burpees/jumping jacks, since they directly mimic the guard retention game). None require special athleticism; the goal is to be able to perform all 12 flawlessly, on demand, without thought before moving on to live application.

---

## Distance-creation movements

### 1. Pommeling (the legs)
- Knees stay close to the chest; circular movement of the lower legs combined with linear side-to-side movement of the hips, done in concert.
- Toes point outward (not up in the air) — needed later for hooking into an opponent.
- The further the knees drift from the chest, the less realistic/useful the movement becomes.
- Applied: pommel a foot to the inside of an opponent's arm/bicep if they place a hand on your knee, using the motion to move your body from one side to the other.
- Practice both directions.

### 2. Shrimping
- From knees-close-to-chest, plant one foot to the mat (foot placed **outside** the knee — planting inside the knee limits how far you can move), form a knee-elbow frame on that side, then shrimp the hips out and away to create a V-shape with the framing forearm.
- Result: hips and shoulders move further from the opponent — distance and angle.
- **Built-in weakness:** shrimping breaks knee-elbow connection on the side you drop a foot to the ground. Be aware of this and re-establish the connection afterward.

### 3. Scooting
- The seated version of shrimping. Frame up, bring feet in close to the buttocks, then push the body backward, alternating side to side.
- Same purpose as shrimping (create distance between your hips and the opponent) but used from a more seated posture.

---

## Realignment movements

### 4. Hip kipping
- Problem it solves: if an opponent pushes your knees to point the wrong way, it becomes very difficult to defend without either shrimping (which risks exposing your stomach in the time it takes) or otherwise repositioning.
- Method: use an upward kipping action of both feet to make the hips momentarily light off the mat, then rotate the knees back to face the opponent — without planting a foot or fully shrimping.
- Key detail: power for this comes from **bracing the elbows into the mat**, not from the hips alone — without the elbow brace the kip doesn't work.
- Value: lets you correct knee direction fast, in the exact window where a slower repositioning (shrimp) would let the opponent complete the pass.

### 5. Crossovers
- Definition: the feet cross over one another (e.g. left foot crosses outside the right knee).
- Powered mainly by the elbows braced on the mat — without that brace, crossing over cleanly is difficult.
- **When to use it:** while the opponent is still in front of your legs and has *not yet passed your hip line* but is starting to angle to a side. Cross one leg over the other to pull your body around and keep facing him, repeating every time he threatens a side.
- Keeps the opponent in front of you so he never gets a significant angle to complete the pass.

---

## The hip line and shoulder line — key demarcations

- **Hip line:** as long as the opponent stays in front of it, you're in a position of relative control and can attack off distance/connection. The moment he passes your hip line, you shift from offense to a **defensive cycle** and must react.
- **Shoulder line:** worse than passing the hip line — once the opponent passes the shoulder line, the movement required to recover guard is significantly bigger.

## Movements for when the opponent has passed your hip line

### 6. High leg
- Used when the opponent is standing over you (or has gotten to your hip line). Take the outside leg high over him and make contact with the sole of your foot anywhere on his body — shoulders, hips, or legs, exact spot doesn't matter.
- Use that foot contact to pull/claw your body around and re-face him, resetting to guard.
- Solo drill version: mimic an opponent moving to an angle, frame with the hand, bring the opposite foot up and touch the outside of your own wrist — training the coordination of joining hand and foot the way you would against a real passer.

### 7. Inside leg (the hook)
- Alternative to high leg: bring the *inside* leg through and hook it onto the opponent's lead-leg quadriceps.
- That hook lets you pull your body all the way around to re-face him, same end result as the high leg (put him back in guard) but via a different leg/contact point.
- Solo drill: frame up, reach out and touch the floor (standing in for the opponent's hip/thigh), then pull your body back the other way using that point of contact.

### 8. Inverted spin
- For when the opponent has passed not just the hip line but the **shoulder line** — the bigger jeopardy situation.
- Method: extend the legs asymmetrically (one leg more extended than the other), reach/invert to touch the floor with one foot, then rotate the body up and over through the inversion to re-face the opponent on the opposite side, finishing with elbows and knees pulled in close together.
- Considered one of the most important guard retention movements overall.
- Solo drill: mimic asymmetric leg extension, reach over the top to touch the floor (standing in for the opponent), rotate through, and finish facing your imaginary partner with elbows and knees tight.

## Anti-pin movements (defeating the guard-pass criteria directly)

**The pass criteria:** to officially pass your guard, an opponent must simultaneously pin **one hip and one shoulder** to the mat for **3 continuous seconds**. Both movements below work by making sure that condition is never met, even if the opponent lands across your side.

### 9. Elbow prop
- From getting passed across the side, come up onto an elbow. This keeps your shoulder off the mat even if your hip is pinned — breaking the "hip + shoulder pinned" requirement.
- With the shoulder free, you can scoot and combine with other movements to create space and reset guard.
- Works, but relies on upper-body strength against the opponent's whole-body driving force.

### 10. Knee prop (preferred over elbow prop)
- Considered mechanically superior to the elbow prop: uses leg strength (always stronger than arm strength) instead of upper-body strength, so it favors a smaller/weaker person against a bigger opponent. Trade-off: requires more flexibility/dexterity, which can be harder for older or less mobile athletes.
- Method: dig the toes of the bent leg into the mat (same foot position as a *tsume geri*-style sweep setup), prop up onto that knee — resulting posture looks like "a dog urinating on a tree." Shoulder is still on the mat, but the hip is lifted off — breaking the pass criteria the same way the elbow prop does, just via the hip instead of the shoulder.
- **Critical detail for it to hold up under pressure:** the propping knee must end up **outside** your own ankle (inside of the knee touching the mat). If the knee ends up inside the ankle (outside of the knee on the mat), you get pushed straight over the first time the opponent drives into you. Demonstrated live: knee-outside-ankle = stable under a push; knee-inside-ankle = collapses immediately.
- Solo drill: foot cocks into the mat, push up into the framed knee-prop posture, repeat both sides ("push up and frame" x N).
- Stated preference: **given a choice between elbow prop and knee prop, always take the knee prop** — make it part of daily warm-up drilling, since it will be used constantly, especially against bigger/stronger opponents.

---

## Combination / transition movements

### 11. Shoulder roll
- Direct continuation of the knee prop: from the knee-prop position, trace the foot along an outline on the mat and roll the body over/through, ending back facing the opponent in guard.
- Considered one of the single best guard retention methods in the sport — the course returns to it repeatedly.

### 12. Leg scissor
- Takes you from supine (facing up) to prone (facing down). Pull everything in tight (elbow close to the body), then scissor the legs to come up through the knees into a prone position.
- Sets up further guard retention options that require being prone rather than supine.

---

## Key Takeaways

- These 12 movements are the physical prerequisites for the whole course — drill them daily until they're automatic before trying to apply the technical content later in the series.
- **Hip line and shoulder line are the two critical demarcations.** In front of the hip line = you're in relative control. Past the hip line = defensive cycle. Past the shoulder line = a much bigger recovery movement is required.
- Crossovers work *before* the hip line is passed; high leg, inside leg, and inverted spin work *after* it's passed (with inverted spin specifically for when the shoulder line is also gone).
- The guard-pass criteria is hip **and** shoulder pinned simultaneously for 3 seconds — elbow prop and knee prop both work by permanently denying one half of that pair.
- **Knee prop > elbow prop** whenever physically possible — legs are stronger than arms, and it works better for smaller/weaker athletes against bigger opponents. The one non-negotiable detail: knee must land outside your own ankle, or you get pushed over.
- Shrimping and scooting both create hip distance but shrimping temporarily costs you knee-elbow connection on one side — know that trade-off going in.
