# Guard Retention Vs. Hips-Centered Passing — Negating Body Lock Guard Passing

## Overview

Introduces the second major category of guard pass (after toreando/leg-control passes): hips-centered passes, where the opponent locks their arms around your waist to control your hips directly rather than your legs. Covers why body locking is so effective and the three prerequisites the opponent needs before it can happen at all — the basis for negating it before it's even applied.

---

## Guard pass categories (context)

1. **Toreando/leg-control passes** — control focused on the feet and knees. Occurs both in front and at north-south.
2. **Hips-centered passes** (this lesson) — control focused on the hips and waist, e.g. body locking, where the passer's arms lock around your waist.

## Why body locking works so well

Your effectiveness from guard is largely bound up in your ability to move your hips: forward/backward movement, hip escapes (heisting), and shrimping side to side. Body locking directly attacks all of it — once locked around the hips, none of those hip movements are available to you, while the passer advances at their own pace, stepping over your legs and working toward a full pass.

## The two-phase framework

1. **Before the lock is applied** — the priority phase, since preventing the lock is far better than escaping it. This lesson focuses here.
2. **After the lock is applied** — covered separately.

## The three prerequisites for a body lock

For an opponent to successfully body lock you, they must satisfy all three:

1. **Connected hands** — their hands must lock together (typically via a figure-four or similar grip) around your waist.
2. **Elbows controlling the outside of your hips** — the elbows are what actually pin the hips once hands are connected.
3. **Head below your shoulder line** — critically, the lock is only dangerous once the opponent's head drops below your shoulders. If their chin stays on top of your shoulder, the body lock is always beatable — you can counter with a sumigaeshi-style sweep regardless of the arm lock.

**Corollary:** if your own elbows are inside theirs when they attempt the lock, it becomes very difficult for them to be effective with the lock over both your arms (barring a large size/strength mismatch).

## Negation methods (stopping the prerequisites)

Framing and hand control target these three prerequisites directly.

### Method 1 — Hand control → knee post
1. Take an inside hand grip on one of the opponent's hands (one hand is enough — don't try to control both).
2. Hold it only long enough to plant a knee in front of their shoulder — this knee post is what actually kills the body lock.
3. Once the knee is in place, release the hand grip; the knee position alone prevents them from ever locking their hands.
4. Variations for the hand grip itself: palm-to-palm, inside-wrist (thumb inside the opponent's wrist), or feeding a wrist deliberately to bait a two-on-one from wrist to elbow.

### Method 2 — Elbow post / thumb post
- Thumb post inside the opponent's elbow. Looks minor but is highly effective — it lets you stay open (like a scarecrow) without exposing yourself to the lock, since the opponent has to beat the thumb post first.
- The only way to beat a thumb post is to back away from it — and that retreat is your opportunity to off-balance them.
- Variation: hold underneath the opponent's elbows (rather than posting the elbow from inside) — kills the lock outright, functions the same way it does in a standing wrestling tie.

### Method 3 — Feet on the hips
- If hand/elbow control keeps getting stripped, put a foot on the opponent's hip as an alternative barrier.
- Denies the lock outright and sets up the knee-in-front-of-shoulder finish.
- A variant is putting a foot on the opponent's head to force them to remove it, which typically gives up wrist control in exchange — a trade you want.

### Method 4 — Cross shoulder post
- Rated as possibly the single most effective method if you could only keep one.
- One hand controls both of the opponent's shoulders and their head simultaneously (compact position, knees in close).
- This frees your other hand entirely for thumb posts, hand control, or counter-offense.

### Method 5 — Side neck post
- A V-grip on the side of the opponent's neck.
- Prevents them from bringing their head down below your shoulder (the key prerequisite) without fully getting underneath their head the way the cross-shoulder post does.
- Easier to get to than the cross-shoulder post, but not as strong. Useful when you can't get underneath the opponent's head directly (e.g. their ear is already tight to your chest).
- Also usable from supine when initiating a sit-up.

## The core stance principle

Before any of these specific grips are needed, the general defensive posture is: elbows close to your own sides, head positioned in front of the opponent's head. This alone prevents a "free" body lock — the opponent has to work past your head and elbows first, and in doing so they present wrists for you to control.

## Combining methods

None of these individually stops a determined body-lock passer. It's the amalgamation — cycling through hand control → thumb posts → feet on hips → cross shoulder → side neck as the opponent adapts — that shuts it down. The most dangerous moment is the transition from supine to seated (sitting up into range), since that's when the opponent has full head/elbow position available to flatten you immediately — always come up with a frame (hand across, cross frame, etc.) already in place, never bare.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Body locking attacks your hip mobility directly — the single most important resource you have from guard.
- Three prerequisites the opponent needs: connected hands, elbows outside your hips, head below your shoulder line. Deny any one and the lock fails.
- Base stance: elbows in close to your sides, head in front of the opponent's head — prevents a free lock and forces them to expose wrists.
- Five negation tools: hand-control-to-knee-post, elbow/thumb post, feet on the hips, cross-shoulder post (single most effective if you only get one), side-neck post.
- Never come up from supine to seated without a frame already in place — that transition is the highest-risk moment for getting body locked.
- No single method stops a good body locker alone; use them in combination as the opponent adapts.
