# Understanding How An Opponent Passes Your Guard: The 5 Steps Of Guard Passing

## Overview

Core conceptual framework for the whole course: every guard pass, regardless of style, requires an opponent to satisfy **5 critical steps**. The order they're satisfied in varies by pass type, but all 5 must be present before the guard is officially passed. If you can deny even one of the five, the pass cannot complete. This reframes guard retention from "react to whatever he's doing" into "identify which of the 5 steps he's missing and deny it."

---

## The 5 steps (demonstrated via a Toreando-style distance pass)

### 1. Contact
The opponent must make effective physical contact with your body. Without it, he can't pass by "magic" — if he doesn't touch you, you can simply follow/track him and stay in front. Contact is what removes your ability to freely follow his movement.

### 2. Distance + angle (as a combined pair, not separately)
Neither distance alone nor angle alone is dangerous — only the *combination*. An opponent who advances straight down your centerline (no angle) walks right into your legs and gets checked. An opponent who circles out to an angle from too far away gives you time to simply reset your foot back to guard. It's only when he has **both** distance closed and an angle established that he starts threatening your hip line and becomes dangerous.

### 3. Clearing your legs
Even with contact, distance, and angle, the opponent hasn't passed anything if part of your leg is still connected to his legs or body. He must fully free himself from any leg contact you have on him — as long as any part of your legs remains in contact with his legs/upper body, there is no guard pass under consideration.

### 4. Level change
The opponent must lower his level to close the gap for pinning. Two main methods, in increasing order of danger:
- **Knee level change** — more conservative, less committal, but also less stable for him.
- **Chest-to-chest level change** — bigger commitment, much closer to actually completing the pass.

### 5. Final pin (head/shoulders OR hips)
The last step is shifting from passing motion into an actual pin — and this is where the official pass condition applies: **control one hip and one shoulder simultaneously on the mat for 3 seconds.**
- **Pinning head and shoulders** — the more common finish from side-passing positions. Makes it hard for you to get your hips or shoulders off the mat.
- **Pinning the hips** — typically the finish from North-South. His hips over your shoulders pins your shoulders down while his hip control denies your hips.
- **Rule of thumb:** North-South passes tend to finish at the hips; side passes tend to finish at the head and shoulders.

---

## The steps don't always happen in this order

The Toreando example above follows: contact → distance+angle → clear legs → level change → pin. But other pass styles satisfy the same 5 requirements in a **different sequence**.

**Half guard passing example:**
1. Contact
2. Distance + angle
3. Pin head and shoulders (this is step 5 in the Toreando sequence, but happens *before* clearing the legs here)
4. Clear the legs (freeing the trapped foot)

All 5 elements still occur — contact, distance+angle, clearing the legs, level change, and pinning — just reordered. **There are no exceptions to needing all 5**; only the order changes based on pass type.

---

## How to use this framework

- The more of the 5 elements an opponent accumulates, the more dangerous your situation — 1-2 elements isn't close to a pass; 4 elements is very close.
- Your job as the guard player is to identify, in real time, which of the 5 elements the opponent is currently missing, and fight specifically to deny that one. You don't need to stop everything — denying any single element is sufficient to prevent the pass entirely.
- This gives a concrete diagnostic instead of vague "defend the pass" — e.g. "he has contact and distance+angle but hasn't cleared my legs yet" tells you exactly what to fight for.

## Key Takeaways

- Every guard pass requires exactly 5 things: **contact, distance+angle (as one combined step), clearing your legs, level change, and a final hip+shoulder pin held 3 seconds.**
- Distance and angle only matter together — either alone is not dangerous.
- Denying any single one of the 5 steps means the pass cannot complete, no matter how far along the other 4 are.
- The sequence of the 5 steps varies by pass type (e.g. half guard passes often pin before clearing the legs), but all 5 are always required.
- North-South finishes tend to pin the hips; side-passing finishes tend to pin the head and shoulders.
