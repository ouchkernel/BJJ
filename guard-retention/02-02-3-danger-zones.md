# The 3 Most Important Danger Zones Of Guard Passing

## Overview

Addresses a common beginner problem: not knowing when to attack from guard vs. when to switch fully into defense. Attacking at the wrong moment gets you passed easily; being overly defensive when you should be attacking produces a weak, purely negative guard. This lesson defines 3 specific situations where you should be **100% defensive**, with no offensive intent, until retention is re-established.

---

## Zone 1 — The hip line (the "J-point" / jeopardy point)

- Trigger: opponent gets past the line of your hips and no longer has any contact with his legs or upper body from your legs.
- This is the **first clear signal** you're in jeopardy — hence "J-point."
- Required response: throw up **upper body frames** — your lower body has already been beaten, so you have to fight him with the upper body now.
- Only after establishing that upper-body defensive frame can you start working on realigning your body and putting him back in guard.

## Zone 2 — The shoulder line (worse than the hip line)

- Trigger: opponent passes the line of your shoulders and starts moving toward North-South.
- Described as **more damaging** than merely reaching the J-point.
- Required response: put up effective frames on the opponent and try to "ride" the developing roll/transition back into guard, re-establishing a connection that lets you return to counter-attacks.

## Zone 3 — Chest-to-chest with an underhook past your knee (the most dangerous of the three)

- Trigger: opponent is past your knee, has established chest-to-chest contact, **and** has some form of underhook (near-side, far-side, or double underhooks).
- Described as the single most dangerous of the three zones — if you don't know what you're doing here, your guard gets passed very easily.
- At this point there should be **zero offensive thinking** — the only question is how to escape the situation. This is pure crisis management, not an attacking opportunity.

---

## Why this framework matters

- These three triggers give you an unambiguous signal for "stop thinking offense, start thinking pure retention." Without this clarity, people either try to attack from bad positions (get passed) or play overly passive/defensive guard even when they have room to attack (weak, negative guard).
- Offense should only re-enter your thinking **after** active retention has re-established a safe connection — not before.

## Key Takeaways

- **3 defensive triggers, escalating in severity:** hip line passed (J-point) → shoulder line passed → chest-to-chest with an underhook past the knee (worst case).
- Each trigger has a different first response: hip line → upper body frames; shoulder line → frame + ride the roll back to guard; chest-to-chest+underhook → pure escape mode, no offense.
- Knowing these 3 triggers precisely removes the guesswork of "should I be attacking or defending right now" — a major source of beginner mistakes in guard play.
- Offense is only appropriate once you've re-established retention — not as a response to one of these three triggers.
