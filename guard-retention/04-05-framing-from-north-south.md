# Framing From North-South

## Overview

Covers what to do once an opponent has already succeeded in reaching north-south position with reverse toreando grips — a situation where the natural instinct (frame the body) is actively wrong and will get you passed. Teaches the correct target (the elbows, not the torso) via three specific framing methods.

---

## The critical rule: never frame the body from north-south

When an opponent is in a conventional position in front of your hips, framing their body/hips is the correct, trained response. From north-south, it's the opposite — reaching for the body is one of the most common ways people actually get passed from this position.

**Why reaching for the body fails:** the entire guard retention system in this course is built on maintaining elbow-knee connection. Reaching for the opponent's torso pulls your elbow away from your knee. Since the opponent already controls your feet (easy for them to push away), any additional loss of elbow-knee integrity is exploited immediately — your elbows spread apart, their head drives into the center, and the pass completes.

**What the opponent actually needs to complete the pass:** their elbows inside yours, and their head below your hip line and inside your elbows. The pin is completed between their elbows, with the head as the entry point.

## The correct target: frame the elbows, not the body

Three methods, covering both a standing opponent and one who has level-changed down.

### Method 1 — Post on both elbows (stalling grip)
- Post directly on the opponent's two elbows as they circle around toward north-south.
- Prevents them completing the level change (their head can't come down) but doesn't actively put you back in guard — it's a frustration/stalling tool.
- Keep your own elbows and knees close together throughout so you don't lose your own connection while doing this.
- As the opponent gets frustrated and starts moving to find another angle, that movement is your opportunity to reconnect your legs to theirs and put them back in guard.

### Method 2 — Scoop from outside + inside knee post
- Take one hand and catch/scoop from outside the opponent's arm.
- Opposite hand goes inside their knee.
- Denies the head-down/pass-completion attempt and opens up counter-attacks from this angle.

### Method 3 — Elbows inside knees (against a level-changing opponent)
- Note the inversion from normal posture: when an opponent is in front of you, your elbows are normally **outside** your knees. From north-south, put your elbows **inside** your knees instead, since the positional relationship has flipped.
- As the opponent brings weight down, they run into the elbow-knee connection.
- From here you can spin in any direction to put the opponent back in guard, or bring a knee to their collarbone and turn on them.

## The underlying principle

All three methods share the same logic: controlling the opponent's elbows and blocking their ability to bring the head down creates a delay. During that delay, they're forced to move — and any movement is your opportunity to re-establish leg connection and return to an offensive cycle instead of a defensive one.

---

## Key Takeaways

- From north-south, never frame the body — it destroys your elbow-knee connection and lets the opponent's head drive to center.
- The pass completes when the opponent's elbows control the inside and their head goes below your hip/shoulder line — so attack the elbows, not the torso.
- Three elbow-framing methods: post both elbows (stalling), scoop-outside + inside-knee-post, and elbows-inside-knees (inverted from normal posture) against a level-changing opponent.
- Any of these buys a delay; opponent movement during that delay is your window to reconnect to their legs and flip back to offense.
