# Guard Retention Vs. North-South Toreando Passing — Leg Frames To Prevent The Transition

## Overview

Explains why north-south is a categorically more dangerous position for a toreando (feet/knee-control) passer to reach than any position in front of the hips, and gives the specific framing method to stop an opponent from ever getting there in the first place.

---

## Why north-south is special (the biomechanics)

Compares the passer's workload at three heights, using a weightlifting analogy:

| Passer's position | Analogous lift | Passer's effort |
|---|---|---|
| In front of hips, low | Decline bench press | Easiest — holding your weight is simple, low core demand |
| By your knee line | Regular bench press | Moderate — has to "bench press" your leg weight |
| North-south (up by shoulders) | Shoulder press | Hardest — people always lift less weight overhead than on a bench |

Additional reasons north-south favors the top player:
- Maintaining elbow-to-knee connection from north-south taxes the passer's core/abdominals far more than from a low position — you can hold the low position "and watch TV," but north-south requires constant work.
- Distance the passer has to travel to reach your legs is much greater from north-south — their whole upper body becomes an intermediary between their hands and your legs, so they have to reach and expose themselves to get back down.
- As the bottom player, your workload correspondingly **doubles** when the opponent reaches north-south — it becomes a fatigue battle that favors the passer over time.

**Conclusion:** it's a significant advantage for a passer to reach north-south rather than stay down by the hips, so the priority is stopping the transition before it happens, not just defending once they're there.

## The defense — leg + arm framing to block the transition

**Principle:** people don't teleport to north-south, they walk there in stages. Danger increases as the opponent moves from directly in front of you (low danger) to an angle (danger starting) to fully around at your shoulder line (high danger).

**Method:**
1. As the opponent starts to walk around toward north-south, frame on their **lead leg**, below the knee, with a solid grip.
2. This framing hand is normally responsible for monitoring the opponent's lead arm (since that's what eventually controls your head) — using it on the leg means that job is uncovered.
3. Compensate: bring your other arm in as a **thumb post at the opponent's elbow**, covering the arm you gave up.
4. Result: connected to the opponent's leg (blocking the walk to north-south) and their elbow (blocking a level-change head-control attempt) simultaneously.

If the opponent abandons the attempt and drops to their knees instead, get your hands in place and re-establish inside position with your top knee and feet up front, ready to continue.

## The mindset

Your opponent will eventually get to north-south sometimes if they're skilled — that's acceptable. The goal is making sure they earn it through real skill rather than being given it for free by your inattention.

---

## Key Takeaways

- North-south dramatically increases a passer's workload (core demand, reach distance) while doubling yours — like the difference between a bench press and a shoulder press.
- People transition to north-south in observable stages: front → angle → fully around. Intervene early.
- Frame the lead leg below the knee as the opponent starts walking around, and compensate by thumb-posting the opponent's elbow with your other hand (since your leg-framing hand can no longer monitor the arm).
- If the opponent bails and drops to their knees, reset into standard inside position (top knee, feet up front).
- Goal: make north-south something the opponent has to earn, not something you give away for free.
