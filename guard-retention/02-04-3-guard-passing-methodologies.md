# The 3 Most Important Guard Passing Methodologies

## Overview

An alternative organizing framework to "here's one specific pass, here's the counter." Instead, this breaks passing down by **which part of your body the opponent is trying to control** — a broader lens that groups many specific passes into just 3 categories. Understanding which category an opponent is working in tells you which general defensive toolkit applies, regardless of the exact pass variant he's using.

## The 3 categories, by target of control

### 1. Lower legs (knee to ankle)
- The opponent controls the area between your knees and ankles/feet — e.g. switching grips to knee-and-ankle control.
- Common in Toreando-style (distance) passing, from both conventional positions and North-South.
- This is the passing category the rest of the course will spend the most time on.

### 2. Hips
- The opponent controls your hips directly — e.g. a tight waist or a body lock.
- **Knee cuts are classified here too** (a deliberately non-obvious choice): even though a knee cut is technically thrown with the knee, when it cuts through it controls your **inside hip**, which is what actually makes it hard to put him back in guard. So mechanically it belongs with the hip-control category, not the leg-control category.

### 3. Head and shoulders
- The opponent is over one of your legs and controls your head and shoulders — the usual mechanism of half guard passing.

## Why this framework matters

- These 3 categories are exhaustive — every guard pass an opponent throws at you across your entire career will fall into one of them.
- Rather than memorizing a long list of named passes and their individual counters, you build 3 general defensive skill sets (one per category) that transfer across many specific pass variants within that category.
- Framed as a career-long requirement: to call yourself a master of guard retention, you must be effective against all 3 categories, not just your favorites.

## Key Takeaways

- Every guard pass targets one of exactly 3 areas: **lower legs (knee-to-ankle), hips, or head-and-shoulders.**
- Knee cuts are classified as hip-control passes, not leg-control passes, because the actual mechanism that stops your realignment is inside-hip control.
- This category-first approach (vs. memorizing individual pass counters) is the organizing structure the rest of the course builds on.
