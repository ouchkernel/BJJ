# Outside Shoulder Roll (Late Defense vs. Body Lock + Passed Guard)

## Overview

An even later, more critical scenario than the chin-post-and-shrimp lesson: the opponent doesn't just have a body lock — he's already passed your legs entirely, so you have zero leg connection to work with. Normally 90% of pin escapes rely on moving your hips, but the body lock removes that option. The counter exploits a structural requirement of finishing the pass: the opponent must eventually release the body lock and shift control to your head/shoulders (the "cranial shift") to actually pin you.

---

## The core insight

- A body lock controls your **hips**. To finish the pass into an actual pin, the opponent's pins are ultimately at the **head and shoulders** — so at some point he has to let go of the hip control and transition ("cranial shift").
- If he never makes that shift and just holds the body lock statically, he **cannot finalize the pass**. This gives you a window.

## The sequence

1. **Thumb-post inside the opponent's elbow** immediately once you realize you're in this situation. This lets you control whether/when he's able to release the body lock and start his cranial shift.
2. Using that elbow control, start coming up to your base — get your shoulder off the floor.
3. Transition into the **knee prop**: bring a knee inside, propped against the opponent's hip. Once your hip comes off the floor, the guard pass cannot be finalized (a guard pass requires both shoulder and hip pinned).
4. The opponent's only way to put your hip back down is to control your head — when he reaches to do that, slide your free hand underneath his armpit.
5. From the knee prop + armpit frame, execute the **shoulder roll**: push off, turn around the corner, and land back in guard with your knee in front of his shoulder — the position that holds him off going forward.

## Why it works structurally

- Both shoulder AND hip must be pinned simultaneously (the standard "3-second pin" requirement) — controlling just one, or controlling them at different times, doesn't finish the pass.
- By owning the elbow (blocking the cranial shift) and then owning hip position (knee prop), you deny the opponent the ability to ever get both control points at once.

---

## Key Takeaways

- This builds directly on the **knee prop + shoulder roll** — described as one of the most important recurring guard retention movements in the whole course.
- The finish requires the opponent to transition from hip control to head/shoulder control (cranial shift) — thumb-posting his elbow lets you gate that transition on your terms.
- Sequence: thumb-post elbow → base up → knee prop (hip off floor) → armpit frame when he reaches for your head → shoulder roll back to guard.
- End position: knee in front of opponent's shoulder, guard re-established.
