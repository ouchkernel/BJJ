# Sit-Up Recovery

## Overview

Follow-up to the leg-framing defense against north-south transitions. Covers what to do once an opponent learns to avoid your leg frames by staying at a distance and circling without touching your legs — using distance itself as the recovery tool.

---

## The problem this solves

Framing on the opponent's legs (from the previous lesson) works — but a smart opponent will eventually recognize that touching your legs while circling lets you stay stuck to them. Their counter-adjustment: stop touching your legs, stay out at a distance, and circle to north-south without giving you anything to frame on. Once they're doing that, leg-framing no longer applies, since there's nothing to grab.

## The solution — the sit-up method

**Core idea:** use the very distance the opponent is exploiting against you, the same way a boxer uses angle-stepping — as an opponent circles to create an angle, you don't square back up to face them, you step into the space they just vacated. Applying this on the ground:

1. As the opponent moves around you at a distance, sit up into the space they just vacated (not toward where they currently are).
2. Quickly put your back down on the ground and push off the floor.
3. This puts the opponent back in front of you, back in guard.

**Why not just turn to face them directly?** Turning toward an opponent who's flanking you is a classic error — it exposes you to a front headlock and a spin to their back, since they simply meet your turn and go around. Going to the space they vacated instead avoids ever offering your back to where the danger actually is.

## Combining with the previous lesson's framing

The two methods cover the full spectrum of opponent behavior:
- **If the opponent makes contact with your legs** (close enough to reach) → use leg framing from the previous lesson; stay stuck to them as they try to walk around.
- **If the opponent stays out at a distance** (too smart to give you contact) → use the sit-up method; sit into the space they vacated, put your back down, come back to guard.

Either way, the objective is the same: re-establish some form of connection to the opponent's legs and resume guard retention work from there.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Opponents who get wise to leg-framing will stop touching your legs and circle at a distance instead — the sit-up method is the counter.
- Sit up into the space the opponent just vacated, not toward their current position — mirrors angle-stepping in striking, avoiding a turn-in that exposes your back to a front headlock/take-the-back sequence.
- After sitting up, quickly put your back down and push off the floor to reset into guard.
- Two-mode defense: contact → leg frame and stick to them; distance → sit-up into vacated space. Both aim to re-establish leg connection.
