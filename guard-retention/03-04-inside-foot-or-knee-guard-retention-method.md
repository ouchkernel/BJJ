# Inside Foot Or Knee Guard Retention Method

## Overview

The instructor's personally preferred guard retention method (explicitly flagged as personal opinion, not orthodox consensus): basing retention on **inside position** of your limbs relative to the opponent's — elbow inside his elbow, knee (or foot) inside his shoulder — rather than the high leg or inside leg methods. Introduced as "the king of guard retention methods" and the cornerstone concept the rest of the course keeps returning to.

---

## The core principle: inside position

- Whoever has their limbs **inside** the other person's limbs has the control advantage, in any grappling context — standing or on the ground, top or bottom.
- Standing example: if your elbows are inside your opponent's arms wrapped around your body, he can't dominate your torso even with a size/skill advantage. If his hand is inside your arm, you can't even reach his head.
- Same principle when pinned: someone controlling you with head, hands, elbows, and shoulders **inside** yours pins you very securely. Conversely, from bottom position, if your feet/knees/elbows/head are inside the top player's limbs, you can move and manipulate them even under their weight.
- Personal belief: **the single most reliable way to stop a guard pass** is to consistently fight to get your hands, elbows, head, and feet inside the opponent's body at the key contact points — inside the knees, inside the hips, inside the armpits, inside the elbows.

## Why high leg / inside leg get shut down

- If the opponent achieves inside position on your leg (hand or head inside your knee), you physically cannot bring that leg over the top for a high leg, even if you're framing successfully everywhere else.
- If his knee is inside your knee, you can't get the inside leg across either.
- Both classic methods have committed to a single leg option, i.entirely dependent on that leg being free — inside positioning as a concept doesn't have this single point of failure.

## Building inside position — foot or knee

- Regardless of how strong the opponent's outside control is, you can always rotate your foot inward toward his armpit and get **inside position with your foot** on his shoulder — this can't be shut out the way the high-leg option can.
- Once your foot has inside position, push back, create space, and you're back in business.
- **Preferred variant: inside knee.** When he level-changes down (the most likely scenario in real passing), place your knee in front of his shoulder — inside position with the knee. Even if he sends your knee through and tries to pin you, there's so much inside position established that creating space and returning to guard is easy.

### Foot vs. knee — when to use which
- Opponent standing / further away → **foot** is often the better option (more reach).
- Opponent level-changed down / close → **knee** is the better option (less flexibility required, works at close range). Since most real passing situations end up close, the knee is used more often as a default.

## The full position: inside knee + inside elbow (opposite sides)

- The complete version combines **inside knee on the far/outside side** with **inside elbow on the near side** — switching from thumb-posting to full elbow-to-elbow control.
- Elbow inside his elbow means he can never reach your head to pin you. Knee inside his shoulder means he can't level-change into you or complete the pass.
- Stated as a near-guarantee: with elbow inside his elbow and knee inside his shoulder, he cannot pass your guard until he first breaks that connection — which usually means physically moving away and restarting the entire pass.

## Why this beats high leg / inside leg specifically

- The strongest weapon a standing/mobile opponent has is switching direction side-to-side — baiting a defensive reaction on one side, then passing the other.
- High leg and inside leg are both vulnerable to this: committing your leg one way lets him time the switch and get you facing the wrong direction, or clear both legs and pass to the opposite side entirely.
- Inside elbow + inside knee is **not** vulnerable to the side-switch, because you're not committed to a single leg action — you're occupying space on both the near and far side simultaneously. When he tries to switch sides, the connection holds regardless.
- Works against most guard-passing styles: half guard passing, body locking, Toreando — all get shut down by this because the only way to actually pass is to fully disengage and start over.

## Drilled sequence

1. Opponent comes around to establish an angle; maintain stance (elbows and knees together, knees close to chest, one side presented).
2. As he starts level-changing down, get **elbow inside his elbow** (switching from a thumb post) on the near side.
3. Simultaneously pommel your **knee inside his shoulder** on the outside/far side.
4. With both connections established, he cannot advance the pass without breaking the grip first — even if he gets a leg through, a knee shield remains in place.
5. From here, transition freely into counter-offense.

## Why maintaining stance matters here specifically

- The whole method depends on **never extending** — elbows outside your knees, knees close to your chest, one side of your body presented at a time.
- The moment you extend, he can go side-to-side and fake you out; inside position alone doesn't protect you if your base stance breaks down.
- With stance + inside elbow + inside knee combined: he can't put weight on you (goes straight into your hip power), can't reach your head, can't body-lock, can't tight-waist you, and even an advanced knee-cut attempt still leaves a knee shield in place.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Inside position (limbs inside the opponent's limbs) is the single most reliable retention principle** — more reliable than high leg or inside leg specifically because it isn't dependent on one leg staying free.
- **Knee inside shoulder + elbow inside elbow (opposite sides)** is the complete version — combine near-side elbow control with far-side knee control.
- Use **foot** when the opponent is further away/standing; use **knee** by default once he's closed distance (which is the more common real scenario).
- The method's key advantage over high leg / inside leg: it isn't beaten by side-to-side switching, which is how good passers usually beat single-leg-based retention methods.
- Requires disciplined stance (elbows outside knees, knees to chest, one side presented) — extending out of stance undermines the whole method.
