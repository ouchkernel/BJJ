# Volume 3 — The Keystone: Arumi Ashi Garami, Breaking to a Hip & The Four-Step Heel Hook

> The most important volume in the series. It introduces the **primary entry** (Arumi ashi garami), teaches **Skill 3 (breaking the opponent down to a hip)**, and then assembles **the complete four-step heel-hook sequence**. It also delivers the single most important principle in the whole system: **your ashi garami must push in the *opposite* direction to your break.**

---

## Overview

Three things happen here, in order:

1. **Arumi ashi garami** — the "standard" ashi garami, named *arumi* ("to enter"). It's the universal **entry and connection** position; every subsystem begins from it.
2. **Skill 3 — breaking down to a hip** — a *contingent* skill used only from bottom: get the standing opponent's hips to the floor where real breaking mechanics become available.
3. **The four-step finishing sequence** — entry → heel exposure → negation → break, mapped to four specific ashi garami positions, and unified by the opposing-forces principle.

Corrected terminology: **ashi garami**, **Arumi ashi garami** ("a Rumi/Arimi" in the audio), **sankaku**, **single-leg X**, **reverse X-guard**.

---

## Arumi Ashi Garami — The Primary Entry

*Arumi* = "to enter." This is the position most people simply call "standard ashi garami," and it's the king of forming an **initial entry and connection** into the opponent's legs. Devote the bulk of early training to it.

### Building the position
- **Inside leg + outside leg.** The **outside leg governs distance**: toes point outside, the foot hooks on top of the opponent's hip. If they lean in, they meet the sole of your foot.
- **Don't push into the ribcage** — that pushes them *out* of the ashi garami. Instead **back heel**: draw your heel toward your own buttocks/hamstring so your foot **pulls on their quadricep**.
- **Shoelace (top-of-foot) placement** of the second foot — two choices:
  - **Straight buttock** (your left foot to their *right* buttock): lets you **close knee over ankle** into a **complete closed circle** around their leg/hip — a **reinforced, closed wedge**. **Preferred for solid connection.**
  - **Cross buttock** (your left foot to their *left* buttock, across the centerline): leaves an **open space between knee and ankle** the opponent can exploit by pushing the foot down — but it's needed for several specific moves (breakdowns, reverse X entries).
- **Close knee and ankle together**, then put **body weight over the shin**. That's the strong connection.

### Connection tests (no hands)
- **Dragging test** — partner posts hands and walks backward on hands/buttocks; your ashi garami holds their knee inside your knee line.
- **Rolling test** — partner rolls; you stay connected through the roll. Trusting the **back heel + pull** (never push) keeps you attached **hands-free**, which matters because **your hands need to be free to attack the ankle** while your legs hold the hip.

### Entering when the opponent crosses their ankles: head over hips
A recurring theme. If their hips are merely over *your* hips and they **cross their ankles**, entry is hard. Switch from a **pulling** grip to a **pushing** grip and drive their **hips directly over your head**. Now no amount of ankle-crossing stops you from taking a **90° (perpendicular) angle** and entering the Arumi ashi garami with their knee inside your knee line.

## Skill 3 — Breaking the Opponent Down to a Hip

**Contingent skill — bottom position only.** (On top, skip it.) You *can* break a leg while the opponent stands over you, but it's low-percentage against skilled opposition. Break them to a **hip** (or at least a **knee**) where controlled breaking mechanics live.

### Why a standing opponent resists
With a good **posture** — head centered on their **centerline**, level lowered, taking **back steps** with the free leg — they absorb your pressure and you'll *never* knock them down. You can't reach their head to stop it, **but you can interfere with the back-stepping leg.**

### The governing principle: directionality of force
**Apply your breakdown force across / perpendicular to the opponent's hips — never into their base of support.** Pushing into the base is the #1 breakdown mistake. Push *across* the hips and they sit straight down.

### Method 1 — Single-leg (foot interferes with the back-step)
Switch to a **straight buttock** foot position and **touch knee to ankle**. First force is **up**; as they bring the head down, switch force **out**. Now your foot **trips/interferes** with their free-leg back-step — they take one-two-three steps and **drop**. This works because the ashi garami controls **both** legs through the hips; control only one and the free leg saves their base.

### Method 2 — Double-leg (Danaher's favorite)
Like a takedown, **both legs** beats gymnastic single-leg defense. When the opponent fights the ashi garami by **splitting your legs** (a hand on knee, a hand on ankle — the most common defense), switch to **cross buttock** (right foot to right buttock), keep the **ankle grip**, post the left foot and **push their hands to the mat**. That brings their **second foot into range** — re-throw the ashi garami **gripping the leg** so they **can't hop** to recover base, and a small force puts them down. (If a foot is ever in range, just grab it; only use cross-buttock to bring a far foot into range.)

### Method 3 — X-guard → reverse X-guard (with the power curve)
When they split the ashi garami, take an **over-wrap** or **scoot grip** on the leg and drop into **X-guard** (foot behind the knee, second foot in front of the hip), using your knee behind the hamstring to **tilt their hands to the mat** (loads weight onto the hands → hips go light). Then switch to **reverse X-guard** (right foot cross-buttock, left foot halfway down the quad). To bring the hips over your head, **don't lift in a straight line** (the calf feels heavy) — bring **knees to chest first**, then lift in a **curve out and around (the "power curve")** so the far leg comes in and the hips arrive over your head. Walk the figure-four, knee inside knee line, then **push your knee across their hips** (not into the base) to sit them down to a hip.

## The Four-Step Heel-Hook Sequence

This is the spine of every finish in the system. Four steps, four specific ashi garami positions, executable in seconds on a resisting opponent.

| Step | Goal | Position used |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Entry & connection** | Attach to the leg, knee inside knee line | **Arumi ashi garami** |
| **2. Heel exposure** | Get the heel out where you can attack it | **Inside ashi garami** (foot crosses the centerline) |
| **3. Negation of escape** | Shut down all escape routes | **Far-hip ashi garami** (a **sankaku** with feet over the far hip) |
| **4. Breaking** | Apply the heel hook | **Outside ashi garami** |

### Step 2 — Heel exposure (inside ashi garami)
From the Arumi entry, take the inside foot **across the opponent's centerline** to **expose the heel**, then secure a wrist around the heel and lock the hands.

### Step 3 — Negation (far-hip ashi garami)
Lock a **sankaku (triangle) with your feet over the opponent's *far* hip** — this welds your hips to theirs. From here the opponent has only **three escape routes, all closed**:
- **Separate your legs / break the ashi garami** → impossible: the **triangle is locked around the hips**.
- **Rotate faster than you** → impossible: **point your knee at the floor** and you rotate at the same speed.
- **Create distance (knee outside your knee line)** → impossible: the **reinforced wedge** (the sankaku) holds it. With the knee pointed down you can let them **run while carrying your full body weight** and the ashi garami stays fixed.

> 📷 **Reference photos:** the far-hip lock is the triangled control in the [inside sankaku / 411 guide — Evolve MMA](https://evolve-mma.com/blog/ultimate-guide-to-the-inside-sankaku-411-in-bjj/) — note the "collect the far leg, knee pointing toward the ground" detail (same mechanic as negating the escape here).

### Step 4 — Breaking (outside ashi garami) — and the central principle

> **The single most important idea in the system:** when it's time to break, **the force of your ashi garami must oppose the force of your break.** Distinguish the **mechanism of control** (ashi garami) from the **mechanism of breaking** (the heel hook).

**Why heel hooks historically failed or maimed people:** people made *no* distinction — they'd run the ashi garami and the heel hook in the **same** direction. Result: the opponent simply **spins out**, *or* the ashi garami literally **pushes them out of the lock** so there's never enough pressure, forcing people to **crank harder** → either no break or a sudden **catastrophic** one. That's how heel hooks earned their bad reputation.

**How to read direction:** **your knees betray the direction of the ashi garami's force** — wherever the knees point, that's where the ashi garami pushes. For a right-leg heel hook the break goes right-to-left; you want your **knees pointing the opposite way (to the outside).**

**Why outside ashi garami is the best breaking position:** it's a **reinforced wedge** — your feet lock and reinforce each other (top-over-bottom or bottom-over-top), so when the opponent tries to push your feet apart it's **shockingly difficult**, and your **knees point outward**, opposite the heel hook. With knees out, **even a minor lift on the heel creates breaking pressure**. (You *can* heel hook from inside an Arumi ashi garami with feet pointing out, and Danaher does against less-skilled opponents — but it's an **unreinforced** wedge the opponent can break by pushing the foot down.)

To finish: **both legs back-heel (pull, never push)**, top knee comes over the top → strong, controlled breaking position.

## The Six Skills (recap) — the chain

Mastery of the system = mastery of all **six essential skills**, in a largely fixed sequence. *Each step is built on the success of the previous one; a chain is only as strong as its weakest link.* Strong ashi garami + strong heel hook is useless if your method of getting **inside position** fails — you never reach the lock.

1. **Grip and inside position** *(treated as one skill)*
2. **Distance and connection** *(treated as one skill)*
3. **Breaking down to a hip** *(contingent — bottom only)*
4. **Heel exposure**
5. **Negation** (of the opponent's defenses)
6. **Breaking**

Steps 1–3 are taught as a **general system**; steps 4–6 are taught inside the **three subsystems** (straight, cross, reverse ashi garami) because the exposure/negation/break details differ by family. The teaching now pivots to **hands-on work**, beginning with the **straight ashi garami system**.

### Straight ashi garami — definition
The opponent's leg goes **over your hip and stays on one side of your centerline** (does **not** cross it). Contrast with **cross ashi garami**, where their leg **crosses** your centerline. The two are mechanically so different that Danaher treats the leg-lock system as really **three systems** (straight, cross, reverse) entwined into one.

## Common Mistakes

- **Pushing into the ribcage / quad** with the outside foot instead of **back-heeling** (pushes the opponent out of the position).
- Using **cross buttock** when you want a solid hold (leaves an **open wedge**) — use **straight buttock** to close the circle.
- Trying to **break a standing opponent** instead of first putting them **down to a hip**.
- Applying breakdown force **into the base of support** instead of **across the hips**.
- Controlling **only one leg** in a breakdown (the free leg back-steps and saves their base).
- Lifting in a **straight line** out of reverse X-guard (heavy) instead of the **power curve**.
- **The cardinal error:** running the **ashi garami and the heel hook in the same direction** — the opponent spins out or you push them out of the lock; then over-cranking causes a bad break.
- Breaking from an **unreinforced wedge** (feet not locked) when an **outside ashi garami** is available.
- Pushing (not pulling/back-heeling) with the legs during the break.

## Decision Points

- **Opponent crosses ankles to deny entry** → switch to a **pushing** grip, drive **hips over your head**, take the **90° angle**.
- **You need a rock-solid connection** → **straight buttock**, close knee-to-ankle (closed wedge). **You need a breakdown or reverse-X entry** → **cross buttock**.
- **Standing opponent won't go down (good posture, back-stepping)** → stop reaching for the head; **interfere with the back-step leg** (straight-buttock single-leg, or double-leg).
- **Opponent splits your ashi garami** → over-wrap/scoot grip → **X-guard → reverse X-guard**, or convert to the **double-leg** breakdown.
- **Time to break** → get to a **reinforced wedge** (**outside ashi garami**) with **knees pointing opposite** the heel-hook direction; then a minor heel lift finishes.

## Terminology Introduced

- **Arumi ashi garami** — the "enter" ashi garami; the primary entry/connection position.
- **Straight buttock / cross buttock** — shoelace placements (closed reinforced wedge vs. open wedge).
- **Back heel** — pulling the heel toward your own hamstring to draw the opponent's quad in.
- **Inside ashi garami** — foot across the centerline to expose the heel (Step 2).
- **Far-hip ashi garami** — a sankaku over the opponent's far hip; the negation position (Step 3).
- **Outside ashi garami** — both feet outside the opponent's legs; the reinforced breaking wedge (Step 4).
- **Single-leg X / X-guard / reverse X-guard** — breakdown positions.
- **Power curve** — lifting out and around (not straight) to bring the hips over your head.
- **Opposing forces principle** — ashi garami force must oppose break force.
