# Volume 8 — Inversions, the Double-Seated Position, Dilemmas & Head Position

> The capstone. More entries (inversion-based, knee shield, deep half variations), the **double-seated** position you'll meet constantly as you climb, and the two ideas that tie the whole system together: **great players create dilemmas**, and in any heel-hook chase, **head position is the single most important thing.**

---

## Overview

This volume finishes the entry catalog with **inversion** entries (a different family from the elevation entries of Vol 7), the **knee shield** and **deep half** conversions, and the **double-seated** position (both players seated, hands on each other's ankles). It closes by elevating two principles above all the technical detail: **the dilemma** (forcing the opponent into a position where every choice is bad for them) and the **primacy of head position** in finishing.

Corrected terminology: **ashi garami**, **sankaku**, **reverse De La Riva**, **knee shield**, **kani basami**, **kuzushi**.

## Inversion Entries (a second family, alongside elevation)

Vol 7's entries were **elevation**-based (get under the center of gravity, lift the knees off the mat). The other family is **inversion** — put your weight up onto the **points of your shoulders** and fire your legs up into position.

### From reverse De La Riva guard
With the opponent on top in a **split squat** (one knee low, other leg wide) and a **reverse De La Riva** hook in (your shoelaces on his quad, leg controlling the knee from inside):
- Hand to his **ankle**, feed the **back of your wrist** through in front of the ankle/shoelaces.
- **Extend both legs** to load his weight onto the **far leg** and move him off you.
- Begin the **inversion**, feet close together, pushing his weight forward; then **switch legs** around the corner so your **knee is inside his hip**, second leg through → **drop** him into the control position.
- **Far-leg vs. near-leg:** attack the far leg by switching around the corner; or **post the far leg and switch under** to attack the **near** leg, locking a **sankaku**.
- If he drops his **knee to the floor**, **over-rotate** all the way around into a **reverse X-guard**, catch the knee, lift, and drop.

## Knee Shield Half Guard Entry

The **knee shield** stops the top player from getting **chest-to-chest** and flattening you.
- **No-gi: keep the knee shallow, in the pocket of the *near* hip** — feet locked or close together. **Don't** bring the knee across to the **far** hip (you get squashed and passed). Especially important against leg-lock-savvy opponents.
- Pair it with a **cross frame** (hand on his shoulder, forearm/elbow on the near shoulder) so he can't drive weight in.
- Then use a **scissor-sweep-like** action of the legs (the BJJ-101 scissor sweep, repurposed) to enter the legs.

## Deep Half Guard Variations (the dilemma in action)

Extending Vol 7's deep half entry:
- **Far-leg route:** transfer the arm from the near leg **across to the far leg**, hook your foot under his ankle and elevate his leg **out and over** (not up), then whip up behind him. Build **wedges on both sides of your own lower leg** (knee behind his ankle, elbow/wrist in front of his calf) plus a **wedge over the hip** so he **can't yank his leg out**; drop to an elbow, pass your foot through and around, roll → inside heel hook.
- **The back-or-leg dilemma:** as your head comes out the "back door," you threaten the **back**. If the opponent **doesn't** move his upper body away → you take the **back**. If he **does** move it away → the **lower body stays** and you take the **leg**. He can hide one, not both. (Bernardo Faria's "Turkish get-up" sweep plugs straight into this — same motion, but instead of finishing the sweep/taking the back, take the heel hook.)

> **Great players create dilemmas.** "Good players create problems; great players create dilemmas." The whole submission game becomes constructing positions where **every** option is bad for the opponent.

## The Double-Seated Position

Both players seated, each with hands on the other's ankles — increasingly common as your leg-lock game develops (closely related to the 50-50 family). The goal: **double inside position** + correct **distance**.

### Holding and pommeling
- **Don't grip the opponent's foot with your thumb** — the hand isn't strong enough; he kicks/pommels free. Instead **hug his ankle to your thigh with an open hand** and use the **strength of your extending leg** to hold it.
- To get inside, **strip his grip and pommel your foot in** — and **always pommel to CROSS buttock** (right foot to his right buttock). Straight buttock leaves **so much space between your knees "you could drive a truck through it,"** and he easily re-pommels back inside. **Cross buttock closes your knees** so you can **clear his re-pommel**.

### Inside position is not enough — you still need distance
Two prerequisites for *any* ashi garami: **inside position AND distance.** Sitting where you are, you're **outside his knee line**. Take a **two-on-one grip** and **use your feet to pull your hips up to his knee line** (breach it), *then* enter the ashi garami with his knee inside your knee line, expose the heel, and roll through.

### When you're losing the pommel battle: change levels
You're not required to stay seated. Against a great pommeler:
- After getting a foot inside (cross buttock), **pull that foot back underneath your *own* leg** — one foot forward, one back — so there's **no weapon for him to pommel against** and no inside space to exploit.
- As he comes forward hand-fighting, do a quick **hip heist / pop up to your knee**, drive forward, and **catch the leg** — either gripping it directly (stronger **kani basami** control, but you don't control the secondary leg) or going **under your own knee with a hand assist** (controls differently). You only ever needed **one** foot inside to get the entry.

## Finishing Review: Head Position Is the #1 Thing

A detailed finishing recap (with Bernardo) that crystallizes the whole inside-heel-hook battle:

- **Make the opponent's knee face you.** Shift your hips so **your knees point at his belly button/chest.** The more your knees point **out**, the more you're aligned with **his escape** (and even assisting it). Point your knee in his direction so it functions as a **brake** on his turn; extend your foot into the **bar of the hamstring** to slow him further.
- **The turnout problem is the fundamental problem** (as in cross ashi garami). Catch the **secondary leg** to stop the turnout → creates the **release problem** when you must let go to finish.
- **But the absolute critical detail is head position — not the secondary-leg control.** When asked directly whether securing the leg is *the* key, Danaher says no: catching the leg is a "useful expedient to stop the guy moving, but it's not enough at the highest level." **Everything is in service of keeping your head inside his knee.**
  - With his knee turned away, **you simply cannot reach the heel.**
  - So drive your **head inside his knee**; as he continues to turn, your **head becomes a post on the floor** that **stops his roll**.
  - When his rotation stops, **you catch up to the heel**, lock it, and **roll through** to the winning position.

> **The hierarchy of finishing priorities:** head position (inside the knee, then a floor post) **>** secondary-leg control **>** everything else.

## Common Mistakes

- Relying only on **elevation** entries and ignoring the **inversion** family (e.g., from reverse De La Riva).
- Bringing the **knee shield across to the far hip** instead of keeping it **shallow on the near hip** (you get squashed/passed).
- In the double-seated position, **gripping the foot with the thumb** instead of **hugging the ankle to the thigh**.
- Pommeling to **straight buttock** in the double-seated position (huge knee gap → easy re-pommel) instead of **cross buttock**.
- Assuming **inside position alone** is enough — forgetting you must also **breach the knee line** for distance.
- Staying **stuck seated** and losing the pommel war instead of **changing levels** (foot under your own leg → hip heist).
- Letting your **knees point out** in the finish (aligns with the escape) instead of **toward his chest**.
- Over-prioritizing **secondary-leg control** over **head position** — the leg grip is an expedient; **head position is the critical detail**.

## Decision Points

- **Opponent passing against your reverse De La Riva** → **inversion** entry (load the far leg, invert, switch to near/far leg or over-rotate to reverse X-guard).
- **In half guard, opponent seeking chest-to-chest** → **knee shield** on the **near** hip + cross frame → scissor-style entry.
- **Head out the back door from deep half** → play the **back-or-leg dilemma** (he hides one, you take the other).
- **Double-seated, can't win the pommel** → **change levels** (foot under your own leg, hip heist) — one inside foot is enough.
- **Heel hook on, opponent turning out** → forget chasing the leg first; **get your head inside his knee, then post it on the floor** to stop the roll, then take the heel.

## Terminology Introduced

- **Inversion entries** — entering via the points of the shoulders (vs. elevation); e.g., from reverse De La Riva.
- **Reverse De La Riva** — the guard used for the inversion entries here.
- **Knee shield (near-hip)** — the shallow shield that blocks chest-to-chest and feeds the entry.
- **Back-or-leg dilemma** — threatening the back so the opponent surrenders the leg (or vice versa).
- **Double-seated position** — both players seated, hands on ankles; a 50-50-adjacent battle for inside position + distance.
- **Kani basami (level-change)** — the hip-heist entry when the pommel war stalls.
- **Head-position primacy** — the head inside the knee (then a floor post) is the single most important finishing detail.
