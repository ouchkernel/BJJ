# Volume 4 — Breaking Mechanics: Finishing the Straight Ashi Garami Heel Hook

> The deepest mechanics in the series. How to actually *break* — the general theory of breaking, the **two** pairs of opposing forces, the lever of the foot, the grip progression, **inside-leg dominance**, hip weight, and the **shared-spiral problem** that ruins most people's heel hooks.

---

## Overview

This volume finishes the **straight ashi garami subsystem** by going deep on **Step 4 (breaking)**. The four-step sequence (Arumi → inside → far-hip → outside ashi garami) is assumed; everything here is about generating a real, controlled break from the **outside ashi garami** finishing position. Two through-lines: a **general theory of breaking** that applies to all joints, and the **mechanics specific to the heel hook**.

---

## The General Theory of Breaking (three elements)

Applies to any joint break in jiu-jitsu.

### 1. Opposing forces
You must apply forces in **opposing directions** on the joint. **Analogy: tearing paper** — trivial because your hands move *opposite* ways. Most people apply the break in **one** direction, and tragically in the **same** direction as their ashi garami — so the break is weak *and* it shoves the opponent toward their escape. As a rule: **ashi garami one way, heel hook the other.**

### 2. Follow-through
Get to the breaking point **and keep going through it.** A boxer punches *through* the target; a golfer who stopped at contact would hit the ball a few feet. **You must never be at full extension when you reach the tapping point** — you need range left to drive through. At elite levels people tolerate enormous pain for medals/money; without a credible *follow-through threat* they won't tap.
- **Trained by the one-handed drill** — finish the heel hook with a **single hand** so the legs and a forearm do the work, forcing you to develop follow-through rather than relying on a two-hand crank.

### 3. Tension / slack removal
**Analogy: a rope.** A slack rope needs a fast whipping cut; a rope **stretched tight like piano wire** cuts with a single easy stroke. Joints are identical — **build tension in the ligaments/tendons first**, then the break takes a tiny movement. Create tension via, in order: **back-heel the legs → align the head so your body is a straight line (a bent body relieves tension) → bring both elbows to the ribcage** so head and hands move together. You'll feel the opponent's tissues "vibrating," their musculature tighten protectively — *then* a small force finishes. Never attack the heel in a **relaxed** state with a speed-based jump (hit-or-miss → uncontrolled break when it hits). The goal: **break in minimal distance** — small movements the opponent can't stop.

## The Heel-Hook Lever: Two Pairs of Opposing Forces

There are **two** opposing-force relationships, not one:

1. **Heel hook vs. ashi garami** (the macro pair, above).
2. **Push the toes vs. pull the heel** — the **foot is a lever with two ends**: the **toe line** (knuckle of big toe to little toe) and the **heel** (below the ankle). You must **push the toes and pull the heel simultaneously**.

### Setup of the lever (do these every time)
- Put the opponent's **toe line into your bicep/armpit**.
- **Line your forearm up with the FOOT, never the shin.** Forearm-on-shin = the opponent points the toes, slips the heel, and escapes **every time**.
- Keep your **wrist BELOW the ankle.** Above the ankle, they point the toes and slip the heel inside your wrist.
- **Finish on your ELBOW, not your shoulder.** Connect the **elbow to your ribs** (creates the toe-push), then **lift the heel** (your head rises).

### The rule of head and hands
As you apply the heel hook, **your head and hands must move together — the distance between them never changes.** If the head moves away from the hands, **power bleeds out** of the lock. Contour your body over the opponent's and move head + hands together in a curve up and away from the mat.

## The Grip Progression (circumference shrinks → tighter lock)

As you change grips, the circle of your arms gets smaller and the lock tighter (same principle as strangles):

| Grip | Property |
|---|---|
| **Ten-finger** | Widest circumference (loosest) |
| **Palm-to-palm** | **Most versatile**; the wide circle lets you use your **top elbow as a wedge on top of the shin** — but only works if you can get the elbow there |
| **Wrist-to-wrist** | Tighter; use when you **can't** get the elbow on the shin (e.g., opponent posts a hand inside your tricep) |
| **Figure-four** | Tighter still; chest becomes a wedge on the shoelaces and there's **nothing to grab** (precludes hand-fighting) |
| **Reverse figure-four** | **Strongest** — "wrong" wrist inside/under the heel; lets you **point the elbow at the opponent's buttocks** and pull the heel; works even from weak ashi garami / when the opponent has turned over |

Start palm-to-palm; **read the resistance** and tighten as needed. The more the opponent hand-fights, the further down the progression you go.

## Leg Mechanics: Inside-Leg Dominance (the big insight)

**The overwhelming majority of an outside heel hook's breaking power comes from the INSIDE leg** — long misunderstood. You have an inside leg (between their legs) and an outside leg, and you must **cycle which one is dominant by phase**:

- **Arumi ashi garami** → inside/outside **neutral**.
- **Heel exposure** → **outside-leg dominant** (this is what exposes the heel).
- **Breaking** → **inside-leg dominant**. The inside leg **pushes down on the opponent's knee** (driving the upper leg one way) while your arms twist the **ankle the other way** — producing a true **rotating** force from knee to ankle.

> **The classic catastrophic error:** staying **outside-leg dominant during the break.** The outside leg then pushes the opponent in the **same** direction as the heel hook — *assisting their escape* and *relieving pressure* on the ankle. This is why heel hooks failed for so many people for so long. With inside-leg dominance you can break **even one-handed**, because the leg — not the arm — is the engine.

(How to read it: **your knees show the direction of force.** Heel exposure → knees point one way; breaking → knees switch to point the other way, across or even down toward the floor.)

## Hip Weight (reinforcing the wedge with weight after all)

Earlier the system said ashi garami can't use body weight (both players are grounded). That was a simplification. At the **break**, you *can* recruit weight: **lift your hips off the floor.** If your hips stay on the mat, your weight transmits to the **floor** and **caps** your pressure. When the hips come up, the **hard hip-bone fulcrum drives into the inside of the opponent's knee** and the weight transmits to **their knee**. Finish with only your **feet and elbow** on the mat — everything else lifted. Works whether you're up on an elbow or flat on your back: **hips off the mat = genuine breaking power.**

## The Shared-Spiral Problem (the key conceptual problem of straight ashi garami)

A heel hook is a **twisting/spiraling** lock (unlike the **linear** armbar/juji gatame). The danger: **the opponent's escape route follows the *same* spiral as your lock.** So both players just **roll and roll** with no result, go out of bounds, and reset.

### The solution: finish in outside ashi garami so the spirals oppose
If you keep the **same** ashi garami, your knees point the **same** direction as the opponent's and you roll together forever. Take your legs to **outside ashi garami** so your **knees point across (or down toward the floor)** relative to theirs. Now if the opponent continues the roll to escape, **they break their own leg on your ashi garami — you do nothing.** Your body is locked to one side; if they turn the other way, they break themselves.

### The #1 error that worsens the shared spiral: rolling over the elbow
When you roll into the finish, **never prop on your elbow** — the elbow **slows your rotation**, the opponent rotates faster, and they escape. Instead **lock your hands to your ribs and roll smoothly over your shoulder**, so you rotate **at the same speed** they do and arrive in the opposing-spiral finish.

## Putting It Together (straight ashi garami, full cycle)

From butterfly guard: lift/elevate/hop into the entry → **Arumi ashi garami** (entry & connection, knee inside knee line) → **inside ashi garami** (outside-leg dominant, foot across the centerline to **expose the heel**, false grip on the heel) → **far-hip ashi garami / sankaku** (negate the three escapes: separate legs / rotate faster / distance) → roll over the **shoulder** into **outside ashi garami** (switch to **inside-leg dominance**) → build **tension** (back-heel, head aligned, elbows to ribs) → **hips off the floor**, push toes / pull heel with the chosen grip → **follow through** the break. Seconds, on a resisting opponent.

## Common Mistakes

- Breaking in **one** direction (ashi garami and heel hook the **same** way) instead of opposing them.
- Reaching the tapping point at **full extension** (no follow-through left).
- Attacking the heel in a **relaxed** joint and relying on **speed**.
- Lining the **forearm with the shin** instead of the **foot**, or letting the **wrist ride above the ankle** (toe-point heel-slip escape).
- Finishing on the **shoulder** instead of the **elbow**; letting **head drift from hands** (power bleeds out).
- Staying **outside-leg dominant during the break** (assists the escape, relieves pressure) — the cardinal leg error.
- Leaving **hips on the mat** during the break (caps the pressure).
- **Rolling over the elbow** (slows rotation → opponent out-rotates you and escapes).
- Keeping the **same ashi garami** through the roll so the **spirals share** direction.

## Decision Points

- **Can get your top elbow onto the shin** → **palm-to-palm**. **Can't (hand posted in your tricep)** → **wrist-to-wrist**. **Opponent hand-fighting hard** → **figure-four**. **Opponent turning/twisting out, weak position** → **reverse figure-four**.
- **By phase, choose leg dominance:** exposing the heel → **outside-leg dominant**; breaking → **inside-leg dominant**.
- **Opponent keeps rolling (shared spiral)** → get to **outside ashi garami** so the spirals oppose and let them break themselves; roll over the **shoulder**, never the elbow.
- **Not enough breaking power** → check the order: **back-heel → align head → elbows to ribs → hips off the floor → follow through**.
- **Less-skilled opponent** → you can run the whole cycle and even finish from **Arumi ashi garami**; **elite opponent** → cycle all the way to **outside ashi garami** before breaking.

## Terminology Introduced

- **Follow-through / one-handed drill** — finishing past the break point; the drill that trains it.
- **Tension / slack removal** — pre-loading the ligaments before breaking (rope analogy).
- **Toe push / heel pull** — the second opposing-force pair (the foot as a two-ended lever).
- **Rule of head and hands** — head and hands move together or power bleeds out.
- **Grip progression** — ten-finger → palm-to-palm → wrist-to-wrist → figure-four → reverse figure-four.
- **Inside-leg dominance** — the inside leg drives the break; cycle dominance by phase.
- **Shared spiral** — the escape follows the same spiral as the lock; defeated by opposing the spirals in outside ashi garami.
