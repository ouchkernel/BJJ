# Volume 2 — Inside Control & Skill 2 (Distance and Connection)

> Finishes **Skill 1** by drawing the crucial line between *inside position* and *inside **control***, then teaches **Skill 2: distance and connection** — the knee-line concept and the four "gravity drills" that build it. If you internalize one idea from this volume, make it the **knee line**: Danaher claims ~90% of leg-lock failures trace back to it.

---

## Overview

Two big ideas:

1. **Inside control** (completing Skill 1). Having your feet inside the opponent's knees is only the *prerequisite*. Control means **governing the space between their knees** — and the whole leg-lock entry happens through that space. The volume also covers how to grip-fight and pommel your way *to* inside position from both top and bottom.
2. **Distance and connection** (Skill 2). Every ashi garami needs inside position **plus** correct distance, measured by the **knee line**. The four **gravity drills** are the training method — they use gravity as a pass/fail test of whether your leg connection is real.

Terminology corrected from the transcript: **ashi garami**, **kuzushi**, **sankaku**, **jigotai** (defensive stance, written "gigotai/giogotai" in the audio), **kani basami** ("kani-asami"), **Imanari roll**.

## Inside Position vs. Inside Control (completing Skill 1)

### The core distinction
- **Inside position** = your feet/knees are inside the opponent's. This is the *prerequisite*.
- **Inside control** = your feet **determine the opponent's ability to move their legs relative to each other** — i.e., you control the **space between their knees**. This is the *goal*. *"If you cannot control that space, you will not leg lock anybody."*
- Position becomes control only through **active hooking**: use the **shoelaces of your feet** to stick into the backs of the opponent's knees so your legs *follow* their movement and put tension into their legs.

> **The kneecap tell.** You (or a coach across the room) can read who has inside control by **where the kneecaps point**. When the opponent's knees point in **opposite** directions, the space between them is being controlled. When they point the **same** direction, they have freedom.

### Inside vs. outside control (a general principle)
The two great vulnerabilities of the body are the **centerline** and the **back**.
- **Inside control** works off the **centerline** — extend the opponent's limbs *away from the torso* to weaken them (a dumbbell held close is light; held at full extension it's heavy). Isolate, weaken, submit. The leg-lock/ashi garami game is almost entirely inside control.
- **Outside control** (e.g., closed guard, De La Riva) works off the **back** — controlling a limb from the outside to threaten behind the opponent.

### The hierarchy of inside positions (a daily drill)
From bottom, ranked best → worst:

| Position | Value |
|---|---|
| **Double inside** (both your feet inside their knees) | **Best** — attack either leg many ways |
| **Single inside** (one foot inside) | **Decent** — back-step or step-over entries, e.g. a **kani basami**-type knee-drag into the legs; but you can attack only **one** leg, and they can attack you too |
| **Neutral** (no feet inside for either) | **Viable** — you can pommel a knee in and throw a foot inside |
| **Shut out** (opponent has double inside, you have nothing) | **Intolerable** — you can't keep your leg in; actively avoid this |

Drill the transitions: shut-out → single inside, shut-out → double inside, pommel in and out.

### Pommeling to inside position (vs. a supine opponent who has inside control)
- **Problem**: opponent has feet inside *and* control (your kneecaps point apart, so they off-balance you at will).
- **Fix**: post your hands on their shins, push the shin down **just below your knee**, and **turn your kneecap straight into their shin** so your knees face the *same* direction. Once reoriented, **pull your foot up to inside position**. Repeat on the other side. Now *their* knees point apart and **you** control the space.

### Grip-fighting to inside position from TOP (vs. seated guard)
Approaching a seated opponent is dangerous — don't come in upright with open legs. The sequence:

- **Distance game first.** Three demarcation lines govern the approach: the **toe line** (across the big toes — past it, they can grab you without moving), the **knee line**, and the **centerline** (step outside it and you *give away* inside position). Stay off the toe line and **match head level to head level** (if your head is high, your legs are exposed) — this is the **jigotai** defensive stance. At safe distance, *they* must move first to make contact, and you (standing) have the mobility advantage.
- **Thumb post.** Palm down, thumb posted inside the opponent's shoulder. It lets you attack/negate their stance — drive their **head over their hips** (not over their knees) to make their feet light. If they strip the post, replace it.
- **Cross-grip the wrist** on the side of the leg you want to step in with (your right leg forward → tie up their left arm, the arm best able to control that leg). Then use the thumb post to open space and **step boldly into the knee line** to claim inside position.
- **The wrist-vs-ankle dilemma.** If the opponent keeps their wrists back (smart), you can't grip the wrist — but hands-back **exposes the ankles**. So you play them between the two: hands forward → take the **hands**; hands back → take the **ankles**. On the ankles, **don't lift — pull first, then lift** (force goes in the direction the **toes** point) to drive the shoulders to the mat, then immediately plant a foot on the **centerline** so you own inside position when they come back up.

## Skill 2 — Distance and Connection: The Knee Line

### The concept
- The **knee** is the joint; the **knee line** is the imaginary line joining *your* two knees. (Danaher divides the body with ankle/knee/hip/shoulder/center lines as a coaching shorthand.)
- **The goal of every ashi garami: get the opponent's knee *inside* your knee line.** If their knee is **outside** your knee line, you have **no control of their hip** — a weak, "anemic" ashi garami they escape by posting hands and scooting back.
- To fix distance: turn your palm up and **recline backward** to drag their knee back inside your knee line; reposition yourself whenever they create distance. *"Almost 90% of your failures with leg locks can be traced back to … can you keep your opponent's knee inside your knee line?"*

### The two physical enablers (true for nearly all bottom entries)
1. **Rounded spine** — your spine in the shape of the **bottom of a rocking chair** (shoulders forward, head forward). A rocking chair lets a 400 lb man move his weight with a toe-flick; a rounded spine lets you lift your lower back off the mat and elevate a heavy, resisting opponent with minimal effort. A **flat** spine = you can never come up = a feeble ashi garami. (Danaher: a 51-year-old with a hip replacement can't do 200 sit-ups but can do this drill all day — because the **spine**, not the abs, does the work.)
2. **Penetration past the ankle line** — when the opponent stands over you, their knee line shows up as their **ankle line**. Your **hips must breach that ankle line**, using **hands and feet together** (never hands alone, never feet alone). If your hips don't pass it, you can't even get your leg around theirs.

### The scissoring action (push leg / pull leg)
Most people think ashi garami is a **squeeze** — that's inadequate. It's a **scissor**: one **push leg** (a wedge in **front of the hip**, driving into the thigh) and one **pull leg** (a wedge **behind the knee**, pulling toward your chest). The two legs work in **opposite directions**, putting a wedge on **both sides** of the opponent's leg. That scissor — not a squeeze, and no hands — is what holds you against gravity and keeps their knee inside your knee line. Then **point your bottom knee at your top knee** to put them down into a controlled position.

## The Four Gravity Drills

Danaher's favorite tool for building distance + connection. Gravity is the **acid test** — a conclusive pass or fail, impossible to fake. Even his elite competitors do these as a warm-up before international matches. Common prerequisites for all four: **rounded spine**, **penetrate the ankle line**, **knees/chin to chest**, **connect shoelaces to the hamstring**, finish with **no hands** holding you up.

### Drill 1 — Facing the opponent (most important; start here)
Partner stands over you, square, feet fairly wide, hands away. From a rounded spine, take a **double-leg**, use hands+feet to pull your hips **past the ankle line**, then **throw your legs up** so your lower back rises off the mat and your **knee line locks above their knee**. Hold with the ashi garami alone — no hands. Reinforces the three habits: **penetration, posture, connection**. Drill for high reps ("bite into the hips, no hands, stop" — 10/20/100×) because high reps are only possible if the **spine**, not the abs, is doing the work.

### Drill 2 — Facing away from the opponent
Same setup but you start **facing away**. Rounded spine, recline with knees to chest, grab a double-leg. **Split** one leg inside and one outside: connect the **whole shoelace** of the inside leg to their hamstring (orientation matters — a flat connection is weak), then bring the second leg around to **wedge behind their knee**. Held off the floor by legs alone; reset by putting a foot back on their hip and repeating to the other hamstring.

### Drill 3 — Rolling inversion (half-Imanari)
Essentially the floor half of an **Imanari roll**. Partner stands over you, feet apart. Rocking-chair spine, then **turn to a 45° angle** (never square). From 45°, drive your near-side hand **wrist-deep** inside their knee. The **45° angle + wrist-deep entry** lets you pull your body around the corner to a **two-on-one grip** on the leg. Then invert, **shoelaces behind the hamstring**, establish the **push leg / pull leg** scissor (wedge front of hip + wedge behind knee), keep their knee inside your knee line, and **point the bottom knee at the top knee** to bring them down.

### Drill 4 — Ankle-line split
Come up to the opponent's **ankle line** with your shoulder, holding the **shin just below the knee**, rounded spine, lower back off the mat. **Split** the legs — one **behind** (becomes the pull leg), one **in front** (becomes the push leg) — making the initial catch with the **shoelace behind the hamstring** and the second hook **behind the knee**. Their knee sits inside your knee line; bottom knee points at top knee to finish in a controlled cross position.

## Common Mistakes

- Confusing **inside position** with **inside control** — feet inside but no active shoelace hook = no control of the knee space.
- Letting the opponent get **double inside** on you (the one intolerable position) — actively avoid it.
- Stepping **outside the centerline** on a top approach (hands you inside position to the opponent).
- Approaching a seated guard **upright with open legs**, or with your **head higher** than theirs.
- On ankle grips, trying to **lift before pulling** — you can't lift a planted opponent; **pull in the toe direction first**, then lift.
- Letting the opponent's **knee sit outside your knee line** (the #1 cause of failed leg locks).
- Playing ashi garami with a **flat spine** (can't elevate → feeble position).
- Failing to **penetrate the ankle line** with the hips before throwing the legs.
- Treating ashi garami as a **squeeze** instead of a **scissor** (no push leg / pull leg → gravity beats you).

## Decision Points

- **Opponent has inside control (kneecaps point apart, off-balancing you)** → post on the shin, **turn your kneecap into their shin** to face your knees the same way, then **pommel to inside**.
- **Approaching a seated opponent on top** → **distance first** (off the toe line, head matched), then **thumb post** + **cross-grip** the wrist, then step into the knee line.
- **Opponent keeps wrists back to protect them** → that exposes the **ankles** — attack the ankles (pull, then lift); if hands come forward, attack the **hands**.
- **Opponent's knee drifts outside your knee line** → palm up, **recline back** to drag it inside; never attack from outside the knee line.
- **You're failing to elevate from bottom** → check **posture** (rounded spine) and **penetration** (hips past the ankle line) before anything else.

## Terminology Introduced

- **Inside control** — governing the space between the opponent's knees (vs. mere inside position).
- **Knee line** — the line joining your two knees; you want the opponent's knee inside it.
- **Ankle line / toe line / centerline** — distance and control demarcation lines.
- **Jigotai** — defensive/lowered stance, head matched to the opponent's.
- **Push leg / pull leg** — the scissoring wedges (front of hip / behind knee) at the heart of ashi garami.
- **Gravity drills** — four no-hands drills that test distance and connection.
- **Imanari roll** — a rolling entry; Drill 3 is its floor half.
- **Kani basami** — a leg-scissor takedown action referenced as a single-inside entry.
