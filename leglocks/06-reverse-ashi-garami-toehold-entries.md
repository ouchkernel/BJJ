# Volume 6 — Reverse Ashi Garami, the Toe Hold & Knee Bar, and Standing Entries

> The third subsystem plus the system's **minimalist philosophy** and the first **standing entries**. Reverse ashi garami is the only position where you face *the same way* as your opponent — it's a powerful **gateway** to the other two subsystems, but it has its own central problem: **crossed legs**. This volume also introduces the only two supplementary finishes Danaher teaches (**figure-four toe hold**, **knee bar**) and shows how to get into the whole game from standing.

---

## Overview

Reverse ashi garami completes the three subsystems. Its defining trait: you **face the same direction as the opponent**, sitting on top with both legs in front. That gives easy **double-leg** access but lets the opponent **lock his legs defensively** (the central problem). The volume also steps back to state the system's core philosophy — **two finishes do ~95% of the work** — and finally moves the whole game to its starting point with **standing entries**.

Corrected terminology: **ashi garami**, **figure-four toe hold**, **knee bar**, **MCL/LCL**, **anterior tibialis**.

---

## The Minimalist Philosophy (stated here, true for the whole series)

Leg-lock mastery is **not** about knowing many leg locks. **~95% of Danaher's students' leg-lock submissions come from just two moves: the inside heel hook and the outside heel hook.** Knowing hundreds of exotic locks does nothing for finishing at the top level. *Two* high-percentage locks beat a thousand ineffective ones. The only supplements he teaches are the **knee bar** and the **figure-four toe hold** (both introduced in this volume); beyond that, nothing.

---

## Reverse Ashi Garami — The Position

- **Unique facing:** the only ashi garami where you face **the same direction** as the opponent (all others face toward them).
- **Good news:** both legs are right in front → easy **double trouble** (control both).
- **Bad news / central problem:** because the legs are adjacent, the opponent can **lock them defensively** (triangle, crossed feet) so the **secondary leg defends the primary leg.** This is the **crossed-legs problem**, and it's unique to reverse ashi garami — in straight ashi garami your body obstructs the legs, and in cross ashi garami crossing the legs only *helps* you.

> 📷 **Reference photos:** [Ashi garami family incl. reverse/ushiro ashi — BJJ Graph](https://bjjgraph.org/Positions/Ashi-Garami) · [reverse & rear ashi garami — Attack The Back](https://www.attacktheback.com/the-ashi-garami/).

### Stability and control (control first, submission second)
Reverse ashi garami is **inherently unstable** — spreading your base wide does **not** fix it. Instead:
- Take a **two-on-one on the primary leg**: a **wrist-deep wedge under the knee** + a grip on the **heel**, and **cinch up** toward you.
- **Scissor your legs** with **knees pointing the same direction** (wedges around the thigh), not spread wide.
- **Base reflexively** with hand/foot when off-balanced, but **always return to the wedge.**
- **Never release the knee.** You can release the ankle briefly, but the moment the knee is free, any off-balance loses the leg.
- For genuine stability: put a **knee on the floor**, **scoop both legs** (double-leg-tackle control), and **pitch down to a hip/shoulder.** Now the opponent can't get up on top.

---

## The Crossed-Legs Problem — Solutions

If the opponent didn't lock his legs, you'd just finish immediately (heel hook, toe hold, knee bar — your pick). The locked legs are the whole game. Three answers:

### 1. Fall to the side → toe-hold/knee-bar dilemma
Fall over and play a **dilemma** between a **figure-four toe hold on one leg** and a **knee bar on the other**.

### 2. Untie the knot (then heel hook)
Come in at the **toes**, **peel one leg across the centerline** to **expose the heel of the second leg**, separate/untangle the legs, then go directly to a **heel hook** on the primary leg. (Against a very strong-legged opponent who's hard to untangle, catch differently — heel + ankle — to pry them apart.)

### 3. Figure-four toe hold from the position
See below — especially valuable for sport contexts.

## The Figure-Four Toe Hold

One of the **few locks usable without an ashi garami**. Crucially, **a figure-four toe hold is just a heel hook applied with the hands and arms** — both are a **push on the toes + pull on the heel** simultaneously; only the mechanism differs (heel hook = arm + torso; toe hold = hands + wrists + arms).

### Mechanics
- **Grip the end of the lever:** smallest knuckle of **your pinky** to the **first knuckle of the opponent's smallest toe.** Gripping down the shin loses leverage ("the difference between a gold medal and nothing"). A thumb is optional, correlated to foot size.
- **Thread the framing arm elbow-deep at the ANKLE**, not the shin — minimize the daylight between your wrist/forearm and the foot.
- **Two opposing forces:** the **left hand pushes** (drive the big toe **toward the tailbone**); the **right elbow pulls/frames** (sweep it down to the end of the lever, pulling back **toward your own jaw**). Push with no pull, or pull with no push, does nothing — only the **coupled** forces torque the ankle.
- **Keep the foot close to your hip** (in the hip pocket) — the more the leg extends, the harder the finish.
- **Head follows the elbow:** as your **head moves back, the elbow follows and the hand pushes in** — same **head-and-hands** rule (distance never changes). With the head driving the elbow, ~100% of your body weight goes into the lock → breaking pressure that matches a heel hook, with strong **follow-through**.

> 📷 **Reference photos:** [The toe hold — pinky-to-pinky-toe figure-four grip — LowkickMMA](https://www.lowkickmma.com/toe-hold/) · [Toe hold mechanics & control — BJJ Graph](https://bjjgraph.org/Submissions/Toe-Hold).

## The Knee Bar

The other supplement (a **linear** lock). Used in the crossed-legs **dilemma** with the toe hold, and as a finish in its own right. Same general breaking theory applies — **end of the lever**, **hard fulcrum**, **opposing forces**, **follow-through** (built on the lever/fulcrum principles from Vol 1).

## Reverse Ashi Garami as a Gateway

A key property: reverse ashi garami **falls easily into both other subsystems.** From it you can untangle and drop into **straight ashi garami** or **cross ashi garami** at will. Combined with the standing entries below, this makes it a hub: enter on top, then route to whichever subsystem the opponent's reaction opens.

---

## Standing Entries

The system finally starts from standing. Two recurring entries, unified by one non-negotiable detail.

> **The universal rule for entering from standing/top: fall so your HEAD is OUTSIDE the opponent's lead leg, landing ACROSS his body (his weight across yours, not on top of you).** Falling **between** the legs / head **inside** = you get **squashed** and pull him into a dominant position. This is the single most common entry mistake.

### Collar-tie entry
The opponent has a collar tie; you both have symmetric grips (acceptable for entering). Tie up his **tricep** so he can't use that arm to reach your leg. **Square him up** by **circling away from the far leg and pulling.** Step your foot to **inside position.** To clear his collar tie, put your hand inside and **walk away from his arm** (don't try to strip a strong grip by pulling). Then **fall across the second leg** — your leg points **across**, not toward the leg you want, so your **head ends up outside** — and **fire up into the ashi garami.** Track his head: **head moves away → straight ashi garami**; **squares up → cross ashi garami** (cross-buttock, lift, drop him down).

### Arm-drag entry
Against the common **bent-over, hands-forward** stance (hands are the closest target, so wrist tie-ups and **arm drags** are natural). Match head level, tie the **wrists**, and **arm-drag** — but with the intent of entering the **legs for ashi garami**, not a takedown. It needs a **penetration step** (like a takedown), a **grip on the leg** (like a takedown), and a **different finish.** The drag gives the perfect angle to **shoot past and behind the far leg** ("everything goes past and behind the far leg"), getting your **head outside** the leg, then fire up into the ashi garami and track the head as above.

## Common Mistakes

- Spreading your **base wide** for stability in reverse ashi garami (doesn't work — use **wedges** and go to a **hip**).
- **Releasing the knee** (you can release the ankle briefly; never the knee).
- Going for the **submission before stability/control** (control first).
- On the toe hold, gripping **down the shin** instead of the **toe knuckle**, or threading the arm at the **shin** instead of **elbow-deep at the ankle**.
- Letting the foot **extend away from your hip** on the toe hold (kills the finish).
- Pushing **or** pulling alone on the toe hold instead of **coupling** both forces.
- **Falling between the opponent's legs / head inside** on a standing entry (you get squashed) — the cardinal entry error.
- Trying to **strip a strong collar tie by pulling** instead of **walking away** from the arm.

## Decision Points

- **Opponent locks his legs (crossed-legs problem)** → fall to the side for the **toe-hold/knee-bar dilemma**, or **untie the knot** (peel a leg across the centerline) and heel hook.
- **Heel hooks not available / sport context** → **figure-four toe hold** (a heel hook with the hands).
- **On top in reverse ashi garami** → use it as a **gateway**: track the head and drop into **straight** or **cross** ashi garami.
- **Standing, opponent in a collar tie** → **collar-tie entry** (clear the tie, square up, fall across).
- **Standing, opponent bent over hands-forward** → **arm-drag entry** (penetrate, shoot behind the far leg).
- **Every standing entry** → fall **head outside**, **across the body** — never between the legs.

## Terminology Introduced

- **Reverse ashi garami** — the same-direction-facing ashi garami; a gateway to the other two subsystems.
- **Crossed-legs problem** — the opponent's secondary leg defending the primary by locking the legs.
- **Figure-four toe hold** — a heel hook applied with the hands/arms; usable without ashi garami.
- **Knee bar** — the linear supplementary finish.
- **Double-leg-tackle control / pitch to a hip** — the stability solution in reverse ashi garami.
- **Collar-tie entry / arm-drag entry** — the two standing entries.
- **Head outside, fall across** — the universal standing-entry safety rule.
