# Volume 1 — Foundations: The Four Mechanical Principles & Skill 1 (Grip and Inside Position)

> The conceptual bedrock of the whole system. Almost no finishes here — instead Danaher lays the four mechanical principles that everything else rests on, defines the structure of the leg-lock system (six essential skills, three subsystems), and teaches the **first** of the six skills: getting a grip and inside position.

---

## Overview

This volume frames the entire series. Danaher is explicit that he is **not** teaching "leg locks" in the generic sense — he is teaching an **ashi garami system** (ashi garami = "entangled legs"), built specifically around the **heel hook**, which he argues is the highest-percentage leg attack against a skilled, resisting opponent. The volume is organized around two scaffolds you should keep in your head for all eight volumes:

1. **The four mechanical principles** — lever/fulcrum, directionality of force, kuzushi, and wedges. Every technique later is an application of these.
2. **The system's structure** — a general system of **six essential skills** of heel hooking, containing **three subsystems** (the three families of ashi garami). This volume covers the principles plus **Skill 1: grip and inside position**.

A note on terminology: the auto-transcript mangles the Japanese badly ("ashigarami / rashi-gramming / ashurama," "kazushi," "judigitami," "sinkaku"). Corrected throughout below: **ashi garami**, **kuzushi**, **juji gatame**, **sankaku**.

## The Four Mechanical Principles

These are the load-bearing ideas of the system. They are not techniques; they are the *why* behind every technique that follows.

### 1. Lever and fulcrum
The standard idea (work at the **end** of the lever for maximum leverage) is only half of it. Danaher's emphasis is on the **fulcrum**, judged by three qualities:

- **Hardness** — a hard fulcrum breaks the "pencil" easily; a soft one doesn't. Practical application in the armbar (juji gatame): break the arm over the **hard bones of the hip**, not the soft inner-thigh/adductor muscles. Take the right arm over your right hip.
- **Height** — the higher the fulcrum, the stronger the break over a shorter distance. In juji gatame, raise it by bringing your feet back toward the opponent (back-heel), raising a knee to lift the hip, or putting a foot under the opponent's head.
- **Placement** — a poorly placed fulcrum forces you to apply far more force. Place it efficiently.

The same three qualities (hardness, height, placement) carry directly into how the heel hook is mechanically built later in the series.

### 2. Directionality of force
Applying force in the *wrong direction* doubles or triples the work. Illustrated with a butterfly sweep: sweeping straight **sideways** drives into the opponent's base of support (hard); redirecting to a **forward angle** off-balances them with the same effort. You don't get stronger — you aim the same force more efficiently. This recurs constantly in entries and finishes.

### 3. Kuzushi (off-balancing)
Technically a subset of lever/fulcrum, but important enough in bottom position to study on its own. Against an opponent in good stance and balance, you can do little. The goal is to take their **center of gravity outside their base of support**, which buys you the two things a submission game needs: **extension and movement**. *"If you give me an opponent who's moving and extended, I can submit them."* The number-one expression of kuzushi in this system: get the opponent's **hands to the floor**.

### 4. Wedges (the most important one)
A wedge is a species of inclined plane, and here it is the **primary tool of immobilization** — more important than body weight. Demonstrated by experiment:

- Top player with hands behind back, knees off floor, chest-to-chest (lots of weight, **no wedges**) → bottom player escapes to guard effortlessly.
- Top player with **no weight** (hand on the floor) but a **wedge on each side** of the opponent → bottom player physically cannot recover guard.

**Conclusion: it's the wedges, not the weight, that hold someone down.** Ashi garami is, at root, *a system of wedges around the opponent's leg and hips* — but unlike a pin, it has **no body weight** reinforcing it (both players are on the floor). So the wedges must be **reinforced mechanically** — by locking the legs (triangle/sankaku, or simply crossing the feet).

> **Two rules that fall out of the wedge principle and recur everywhere:**
> - Wedges must be on **both sides** of the opponent's body (one side alone fails the moment they slide away).
> - Wedges should be **reinforced** — by body weight if you have a pin, by locking the legs if you're in ashi garami.

## The Structure of the System

### General system → six essential skills → three subsystems

- **Leg locking** is the generic term (any lock on the legs). What's taught here is specifically an **ashi garami system** centered on **heel hooks**, chosen for being the mechanically most efficient, highest-percentage leg attack. (Danaher's claim: in competition where heel hooks are legal, for every tap to another leg lock there are ~100 taps to heel hooks.)
- The general system = **six essential skills of heel hooking**, learned in a (slightly flexible but largely fixed) sequence. **Skill 1 is grip and inside position** — the subject of the rest of this volume. The remaining five skills are developed across the later volumes.
- Inside the general system are **three subsystems** = the **three families of ashi garami**.

### The three families of ashi garami

Ashi garami means *entangled legs* — typically **two of your legs controlling one of the opponent's**, which is where the built-in mechanical advantage comes from (two of your legs should beat one of theirs even against a bigger person).

| Family | Defining feature | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Straight ashi garami** | Opponent's leg goes over your hip and **stays on one side of your centerline** | Many variations — e.g. single-leg-X / "primi" entry, feet inside in a sankaku, feet crossed outside |
| **Cross ashi garami** | Opponent's leg **crosses your centerline** (their right leg over your right hip) | Variations: feet close together inside, feet over the far hip, sankaku with foot inside or outside |
| **Reverse ashi garami** | The **only** family where you face the **same direction** as the opponent (every other ashi garami you face toward them) | One leg trapped between yours; many variations unified by the shared-direction facing |

> 📷 **Reference photos:** [Basic ashi garami positions — Evolve MMA](https://evolve-mma.com/blog/basic-ashi-garami-positions-explained/) · [Leg-lock positions explained (standard / cross / inside sankaku) — Open Note Grappling](https://www.opennotegrappling.com/p/foundational-guide-to-leg-locks) · [The ashi garami family incl. reverse/ushiro — BJJ Graph](https://bjjgraph.org/Positions/Ashi-Garami).

You're expected to master **all three** subsystems to call yourself competent at leg locking.

## Skill 1 — Grip and Inside Position

Everything in jiu-jitsu begins with grip; control requires physical contact. In an ashi-garami-based game the first skill is **a working grip + inside position of your feet/knees**. The governing idea is **"gripping for a purpose"** — too many people grip just to grip. A purposeful grip has three jobs, in order: (1) form a **strong connection** the opponent can't easily break, (2) **negate** the opponent's offense, (3) **create attacks** by getting their hands/hips to the floor.

### Technique — Two-on-one wrist grips (up and down)
- **Setup / entry**: From seated butterfly vs. a partner on two knees. A generic single grip confers no advantage; the **two-on-one** does.
- **Key details**: Every grip has a **zone of weakness** revealed by the **direction your thumb points** — the opponent escapes by pulling that way. Cover it with the second hand, **thumbs pointing opposite directions**, so each hand covers the other's weakness. **Up grips** (thumbs/palms toward ceiling) are for drawing the opponent forward and getting hands over your shoulders — pair with butterfly hooks. **Down grips** (palms toward floor) are for when the opponent is standing.
- **Finish / next step**: With grips secured, your two feet are free to off-balance and drive the hands to the mat.

### Technique — The immovable elbow
- **Key details**: One of the body's strongest structures is **elbow connected to your own hip**. Experiment: link hands, bring elbow to hip — when the partner pulls, *your whole body lifts off the floor before your elbow leaves your hip* (it recruits the powerful lat/back muscles). So after securing a grip, **close your elbows to your hips**. This creates the *illusion of huge gripping strength* through proper structure, not muscle.

### Technique — Centerline control
- **Key details**: Pull the opponent's gripped arm **across your centerline** so their right hand connects to your right hip. This sharply weakens their ability to pull away and frees your feet to attack.

### Technique — Moving with the pull (never a tug-of-war)
- **Key details**: Never stay stationary and pull back against a yanking opponent — eventually they break free. Instead **move in the same direction as their pull** (walk with them when they walk). This **dissipates** their force and preserves your attachment until you can off-balance and trip them.

### Technique — Tricep grips (vs. upper-body grips)
- **Setup / entry**: When the opponent gets **their** grips first (on your collar/arms).
- **Key details**: Don't grip at the elbows — grip **higher, under the triceps**. This lets you **dominate the arm from underneath** so they physically can't reach down to your legs. Draw both arms **past your shoulders** (not just lift), get your head into position, elbows close to hips (immovable-elbow principle). When they reach for your legs, it's exceptionally difficult.

### Technique — Underhook gripping (the highest-percentage path to the legs)
- **Setup / entry**: Underhooks give the best chance of getting **under** the opponent to access the legs. (Overhooks are great for upper-body attacks and sweeps but make leg entry hard — the opponent's underhook blocks your head from getting under their hips.)
- **Key details**: **Avoid body-locking** the hands here — any lock below the scapula lets the opponent put a forearm across your neck and break your grip. Instead: **elbows pointed inward, hand behind the shoulder, false grip with the wrist bent ~90°, your elbow dominating their elbow**, done on **both** sides. To *get* the underhooks against resistance: hand on their head, second hand pinky-knuckle-up with your forearm over the top of theirs; **pull the head and shoot the first hand through**, elbow dominating; then the second hand through to the matching grip on the other side. Don't sink your weight onto it.
- **Finish / next step**: Bring your hips under, elevate, get their **hands to the floor** — from there, "a thousand different ways" into the legs.

## Common Mistakes

- Breaking the armbar over the **soft inner thigh** instead of the **hard hip bone** (soft fulcrum → weak break).
- Working at the **short end** of the lever instead of the end.
- Applying force in an **inefficient direction** (e.g., sweeping into the opponent's base instead of to a forward angle).
- Relying on **body weight** to immobilize when it's **wedges** that actually hold someone — and using a wedge on **only one side**.
- Gripping **without purpose** — just collecting grips with no plan to negate offense or get the hands down.
- Leaving a **zone of weakness** uncovered (single-hand grip in the thumb's direction).
- Letting your **elbow drift from your hip** (loses the immovable-elbow structure).
- Remaining **stationary** and turning a grip break into a strength contest instead of moving with the pull.
- **Body-locking** the hands in the underhook position (invites the forearm-frame break).
- Trying to enter the legs from **overhooks** (low percentage — the underhook blocks your head).

## Decision Points

- **Opponent's hands are in front of you** → seize the **wrists** (two-on-one).
- **Opponent gets upper-body grips on you first (collar/arms)** → switch to **tricep grips**, dominate from underneath.
- **You need to actually access the legs** → fight for **underhooks** (highest percentage), not overhooks.
- **Opponent yanks/pulls to break the grip** → **move with the pull**, don't resist it.
- **You've got the grip** → use your **free feet** to off-balance and drive their **hands to the floor** — that's the gateway to every leg attack in the system.

## Terminology Introduced

- **Ashi garami** — entangled legs; the positional basis of the whole system.
- **Kuzushi** — off-balancing; creating extension + movement.
- **Sankaku** — triangle (here, a triangle lock of the legs used to reinforce a wedge).
- **Juji gatame** — the straight armbar (used to illustrate lever/fulcrum).
- **The three families** — straight, cross, and reverse ashi garami.
