# Volume 5 — Cross Ashi Garami & The Inside Heel Hook

> The second subsystem and the apex of the hierarchy. The **inside heel hook** from **cross ashi garami** is the most devastating leg attack in the sport. This volume covers why it sits at the top, the **primary/secondary leg** distinction, the **turnout problem** and its cascade of follow-up problems (release, interference), and the control methods (**double trouble**, the **five-/six-point wedge**) that let you pin and finish elite opponents.

---

## Overview

Cross ashi garami = the opponent's leg **crosses your centerline**. From here you attack the **inside heel hook**, which stresses the **MCL** (medial knee) — more catastrophic and harder to fight through than the straight/outside heel hook's LCL+ankle target. The volume is organized around one central problem (the **turnout**) and the chain of sub-problems it spawns, plus the control systems that solve them.

Corrected terminology: **ashi garami**, **sankaku**, **MCL/LCL**, **anterior tibialis**, **Achilles lock**.

---

## The Hierarchy of Ashi Garami (how to rate a position)

Leg locks are hierarchical like BJJ pins, but judged on **two** criteria:

1. **Safety** — every ashi garami exposes **your** feet too (you can be caught in inverted heel hooks / toe holds). How exposed are *your* feet to *their* counters? (A **50-50** can expose your feet badly depending on skill.)
2. **Breaking potential** — how strong is the finish available from it?

> **The apex:** a **cross ashi garami with *your* feet on the inside** of the opponent's legs — **minimal** exposure of your feet, **maximal** ability to break. This is the preferred breaking position of the whole system.

### Why the inside heel hook is the best break
- **Target = the MCL** (inside of the knee). A torn MCL ends the fight; opponents can often keep going through an LCL tear.
- **Tiny range of motion** required to break (smaller than straight/outside heel hooks).
- **Both of the opponent's legs are in front of you** (see primary/secondary leg below) — unlike straight ashi garami, where one leg is behind you.

Foot-position variations inside: foot-over-foot, **inside sankaku**, or foot-in-the-middle-then-around for **wedges on both sides**. (Danaher notes the shoelace-on-hip wedge as **Eddie Cummings'** innovation and declines to teach it.)

---

## Primary vs. Secondary Leg (the key distinction)

- **Primary leg** = the leg held by your ashi garami (the one you attack — you want the inside heel hook on it).
- **Secondary leg** = the other leg. **~90% of the opponent's escape potential comes from the secondary leg** (pommeling a foot inside, turning, countering).

So: **submissions come from the primary leg; escapes come from the secondary leg.** Cross ashi garami's great virtue is that **both legs are in front of you**, so you can control the escape engine (secondary) while attacking the submission target (primary).

## The Central Problem: The Turnout

Everything in cross ashi garami is a battle over **heel exposure**. The opponent's defense is to **turn away**, which puts **your head outside their legs** — and you **cannot access the heel with your head outside.** You need your **head inside** their legs. (When an opponent turns *into* you, they hand you the heel — but smart opponents always turn *away*.)

There are **two families of solution**: **double-leg** (control the secondary leg too) and **single-leg** (you only have the primary leg). Be proficient in both.

---

## Double-Leg Solutions (control both legs — Danaher's preference)

Controlling both legs converts the ashi garami into a **pin without body weight** ("double trouble"). His students use it to hold huge, elite opponents for long stretches and work methodically to the break.

### Maintaining the knee line
Same rule as straight ashi garami: keep the opponent's knee inside your **knee line**. If distance opens, go **elbow-deep around the primary knee** to insert an **extra wedge** and drag the knee back inside the line.

### Ankle-lace ashi garami
Seize the **secondary leg** and hold it with an **Achilles lock** — like **manacling a prisoner's ankles together**. The opponent can't generate effective movement; it's the core double-leg control and the first focus of the subsystem.

### The five-point and six-point wedge
A maximal control structure:
- **Five-point wedge** = (1,2) **two knees** wedging the outside of the legs, (3) **hips** pushing up from underneath, (4) a **hand** pulling down on top of the legs, (5) an **elbow** wedging down over both ankles. The opponent is anchored — wherever they turn, you stick and the knee line holds.
- **Six-point wedge** = make a **knife hand**, spear it through the ankles, and connect the **shoelaces to your neck** as the sixth wedge. Now pulling/rolling away is almost impossible, and the heel is exposed behind you — tilt the opponent (plant a foot, use the cross-buttock position), lock **inside sankaku**, and **reach back** to secure the heel.

Named variants of the wedge control: **over-under method**, **double-elbow method** (when they hide the foot, point one elbow at them, throw the second elbow under to hold the heel and pull the shoelaces to your armpit — fast transition to the finish), and **double-cross method** (move the head back, pull the secondary leg **across your centerline** too, take a **V-grip** on the Achilles so **both** legs cross your centerline in opposite directions; if you lose the leg, just throw your legs over the top and re-attack the other side).

> **Coaching fix:** if you can't reach the heel in these positions, you're **leaning too far back** — **sit up and forward** so your head matches the opponent's head height, then reach back.

### The lace-leg break (finishing on the secondary leg)
Keep your **hands at the end of the lever** (near the toes — hands near the toes preserve leverage; down the shin bleeds power). **Cross the wrists**, bring **hands to your heart / centerline**, pitch onto your side, **push with both feet** and **pull one leg across the other** — the opponent feels it as a **knee bar on the primary leg**. Both legs fully controlled; turning out doesn't help them.

## The Release Problem (and two strategies)

To finish the **inside heel hook** on the primary leg, you usually must **release the secondary leg** — and the instant you do, the opponent **turns out again**. Two ways to handle it:

### Strategy 1 — Don't release the leg
Attack the **secondary** leg directly so you never have to let go. Use a strong **Achilles lock / reverse figure-four**:
- Feed the free hand **inside and underneath** (not in front), so it emerges **below the ankle** — **thumb near the ankle, never down the shin** (down the shin = poor break).
- Target the **anterior tibialis tendon** (front of the ankle) for a real break, not generic foot pain.
- Point the locking **elbow toward the opponent's hips** (don't just split the legs), bringing one leg down on top of the other.
- **Floating-rib placement:** put the **sole of the opponent's heel on your floating rib.** This defeats the standard Achilles counter of **"putting the boot on"** (pushing the foot through), and enables tight figure-four grip variations (e.g., gripping your own bicep/shoulder, elbow at their hips).
- This creates a **dilemma** between the **secondary ankle** and the **primary knee**: when the opponent hand-fights or withdraws the foot, the **secondary leg pops out** and the transition to the **inside heel hook** is so short it's nearly guaranteed.

### Strategy 2 — Release under *your* conditions
Only release the secondary leg **once you already have a wrist around the heel**. Insert a **splitter hand** (forearm between the legs to split them apart), then lock the heel hook. Now if the opponent extracts the secondary leg and turns out, **they break themselves** — and your added force doubles it.

---

## The Interference Problem (the two-foot problem)

A third problem appears once the heel is locked: **a clean inside-heel-hook break needs exactly *one* foot in your armpit.** A knowledgeable opponent **pushes the second foot into your armpit** so the two feet interfere and you can only cause pain, not a break.

**Solution — remove the secondary leg with your *legs*, not your arm.** Pitch onto your side, place **your knee inside their knee** (reposition from over the hip to the knee), and **push knee-to-knee** so the **secondary leg pops out "like a champagne cork"** while the primary leg stays. Then fall back into a strong break.
> Common error: trying to move their leg with your **arm** (you'll never move a leg with an arm), or **pushing too hard** so **both** feet pop out.

---

## Single-Leg Solution: Win the Race by Slowing the Opponent

When you **can't** get the secondary leg, you lose the ability to *stop* the turnout — so instead you **control its pace.** This is the **shared spiral** again (you both spiral the same way), and it's a **race for head position inside the legs.**

> **You don't get faster — you make the opponent slower.** (Danaher: a 51-year-old with a hip replacement beats Usain Bolt in a sprint *if you shackle Bolt's legs.*)

The four "brakes" (apply together):
1. **Handbrake** — hand around the knee, **fingers all the way through**, **wrist bent 90°**, **elbow closed** — won't stop the turn but slows it.
2. **Elbow break** — your elbow into the inside position.
3. **Head post** — put your **head on the floor** as a physical barrier to the roll (Danaher calls this the most important of the four).
4. **Interfering foot** — hook **inside the opponent's knee**.

Together these slow the opponent enough that **your** turn beats theirs: you get your **head inside**, access the heel, and **emerge from the roll ahead** with the inside heel hook locked.

## Common Mistakes

- Working cross ashi garami with your feet on the **outside** (more exposure, less control) instead of inside.
- Forgetting the **knee line** still governs everything here.
- Trying to move the opponent's **secondary leg with your arm** instead of your legs.
- **Pushing too hard** when popping the secondary leg out (both feet pop → lose control).
- Releasing the secondary leg **before** you have a wrist on the heel (Strategy 2 requires the heel first).
- On the Achilles lock, thumb **down the shin** instead of **below the ankle**; missing the **anterior tibialis**.
- Leaning **too far back** so you can't reach the heel (sit up and forward).
- In the single-leg race, relying on **your own speed** instead of **slowing the opponent**.
- Tolerating **two feet in the armpit** and expecting a clean break (resolve the interference first).

## Decision Points

- **Choosing the subsystem for max break** → **cross ashi garami, feet inside** → **inside heel hook** (MCL).
- **Opponent turning out** → if you can reach the **secondary leg**, go **double-leg** (ankle lace / five-/six-point wedge); if not, go **single-leg** (the four brakes).
- **Need to finish but releasing the leg frees the turnout** → either **don't release** (attack the secondary ankle to create a dilemma) or **release under your conditions** (splitter hand once the heel is secured).
- **Two feet jammed in your armpit (interference)** → pitch to your side, **knee-to-knee push** to pop the secondary leg out.
- **Can't reach the heel** → **sit up and forward**, match head heights, then reach back.

## Terminology Introduced

- **Cross ashi garami** — opponent's leg crosses your centerline; home of the inside heel hook.
- **Inside heel hook** — MCL-targeting finish; the strongest leg break.
- **Primary / secondary leg** — the attacked leg vs. the escape-engine leg.
- **Turnout problem** — the opponent turning away to hide the heel and put your head outside.
- **Ankle-lace ashi garami** — lacing the secondary leg with an Achilles lock (a pin without weight).
- **Five-point / six-point wedge** — maximal double-leg control structures (knees/hips/hand/elbow, +shoelaces-to-neck).
- **Double trouble** — controlling both legs to pin the opponent.
- **Splitter hand** — a forearm inserted to split the legs apart before finishing.
- **Anterior tibialis / "putting the boot on" / floating-rib placement** — Achilles-lock target, the standard counter, and the defeat of that counter.
- **Interference (two-foot) problem** — two feet in the armpit prevent a clean break.
