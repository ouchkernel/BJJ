# Ankle Lock From A Z-Lock

## Overview

Continuing the "double trouble" theme (controlling both of the opponent's legs). The Z-lock position is normally used to attack the hip (and sometimes the knee) with hip-lock pressure, but there's an underrated ankle lock available in the middle of that same position — attacking the **secondary leg**.

---

## The position

1. Start with a basic Ashi Garami on the training partner — feet passed over and to the inside.
2. Get a hold of the training partner's second leg, bring your feet in.
3. **Foot stacking matters:** hide your own foot behind (not on top) — if your foot comes over the top, it becomes accessible for the opponent to attack. Stack so your feet are protected.

This position is normally used for a hip lock (bringing the opponent's knees together creates hip pressure) — but this lesson focuses on the ankle lock available on the secondary leg.

## The problem with getting true half-foot here

It can be difficult to get a "true" half-foot position in this entanglement, especially when the opponent is actively pushing their leg through. **Solution: you don't need to.** Use an unusual grip instead.

## The grip: palm-to-palm

1. Go **palm to palm** (not the usual figure-four/wrist-based grips).
2. Bend your support-hand wrist 90 degrees.
3. Bring your elbow very close to your own body — no space. (The wider your elbow, the more the opponent can turn their foot and "put the boot on.")
4. **Swallow the opponent's toes** before going down — instead of moving forward (blocked by their leg), roll **backward over the toes.**
5. Connect hands, bring elbow down, then pinch your knees together.

### Target
This grip focuses pressure directly on the **anterior tibialis tendon** — pulling the opponent's toes back toward their own knee makes the tendon visibly pronounced and puts direct pressure on it.

### Extra detail for a tighter finish
Instead of falling straight back in a line, **rotate your body toward the captured foot.** This tightens the lock significantly faster than a straight-back motion.

## Why the opponent can't escape

- Because both of their legs are tied up (double trouble), physically yanking the leg free is "shockingly difficult."
- Unlike other double-trouble control methods covered earlier, **limping the leg out doesn't work here** — it's a fundamentally more controlling grip.
- Hiding your own foot (per the foot-stacking detail above) also denies the opponent access to attack your foot while you finish.

## Drilled Sequence

1. Basic Ashi Garami, pass foot over the top with the reaping leg.
2. Capture the training partner's ankle.
3. Hide your foot from the opponent's foot (stacking).
4. Grip palm-to-palm, elbow tight, no space.
5. Rotate your body toward the captured foot.
6. Pinch knees together, finish.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The Z-lock/double-trouble position has an underrated ankle lock on the secondary leg, not just hip-lock pressure.
- Foot stacking (hide your own foot behind, not on top) prevents the opponent from counterattacking your foot.
- True half-foot isn't required here — the **palm-to-palm grip** (tight elbow, swallow the toes, roll backward over them) substitutes effectively.
- Target is the anterior tibialis tendon — pulling toes toward the opponent's own knee makes this visible and effective.
- Rotating your body toward the captured foot (rather than falling straight back) tightens the finish much faster.
- This grip resists both yanking the leg free *and* limping out — stronger than other double-trouble control methods against escape attempts.
