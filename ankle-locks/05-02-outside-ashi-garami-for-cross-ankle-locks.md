# Getting The Half Position In Cross Ankle Locks (Outside Ashi Garami Entanglement)

## Overview

Having half-foot in a cross ankle lock isn't enough on its own — the grip alone has limits. This lesson covers the leg entanglement (**outside Ashi Garami**) that binds the opponent's leg and stops the escape that's specific to cross ankle locks: the inward limp.

---

## Straight vs. cross ankle lock: the limp direction flips

- **Straight ankle lock:** opponent's knee turns **outward** to limp free.
- **Cross ankle lock:** opponent's knee turns **inward** to limp free.

This means the leg entanglement that stops each escape has to be built differently.

## The fix: outside Ashi Garami

- Pass your ankle over the training partner's **hip** (not up near the armpit — "nothing good happens up here"). Anchor at the hip.
- This functions as a **lever**: the inside of your knee presses the inside of theirs.
- If your knees are split open, the opponent can still turn the knee inward and cause problems — knees must stay engaged, not split.
- **Cross your own ankles** once anchored at the hip — an uncrossed/exposed foot position up near the far side leaves you open to being leg-locked yourself (anchor-lockable). Cross ankles, feet pulled back toward you, anchored at the hip.
- Result: when the opponent tries to turn the knee inward, your outside Ashi Garami acts as a strong opposing wedge, and the ankle stays exposed and tight.

## Grip and body mechanics once entangled

- Draw the opponent in, pass the foot over the top, protect it immediately (don't leave it exposed).
- **Inside leg goes underneath the outside leg, and the inside leg is dominant** — it actively pushes against the inside of the opponent's knee, so their own body limits how far they can limp (stops flat on the mat).
- Avoid getting turned onto your own inside hip here — hard to break someone from that angle (this problem, and its fix, is covered in the next lesson).
- Use the **surface area of your back** to pin them in place.
- **Elbow comes down** — even before you've finished rotating your body, dropping the elbow alone creates strong pressure.

## Sequencing: half-foot first, or Ashi Garami first?

Either order works — choose based on opponent skill level:

| Opponent | Sequence |
|---|---|
| Less experienced with leg locks | Get half-foot first (using a hand post), **then** pass the foot over into outside Ashi Garami, then close in for the finish |
| Higher skill / more resistance | Pass the foot over into outside Ashi Garami **first**, then use body-weight/hip movement to expose the ankle, then close in |

Outside Ashi Garami described as the single most-used method (roughly 9 times out of 10) for binding the legs in cross ankle locks — there are other methods, covered later.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Cross ankle lock escape = inward knee turn (opposite of straight ankle lock's outward turn).
- **Outside Ashi Garami** (your ankle over their hip, ankles crossed, knees engaged, inside leg dominant) is the control answer to that inward limp.
- Don't leave your foot uncrossed/exposed near the far hip — that invites a counter leg lock.
- Elbow-down alone generates real pressure, even mid-transition.
- Sequence flexibly: half-foot-first against easier opponents, Ashi-Garami-first against resistant ones.
