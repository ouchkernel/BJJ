# Ankle Lock As A Counter To The Short Ankle Lock

Source: `7713668694114-03-05- Ankle Lock As A Counter To The Short Ankle Lock.mp4`

## Overview

The ankle lock's biggest structural advantage — speed of entry, no need to fight for a "perfect" grip — makes it an excellent counterattack against other leg locks. This lesson covers countering an opponent's **short ankle lock** (a very tight triangle-based lock formed by passing the foot over the top and locking around the outside of the legs).

---

## Why ankle locks work well as counters generally

There's no fiddling for the ideal grip. In most cases you can snatch elbow-deep on the ankle and go directly into your own attack. This speed is what makes ankle lock such a good counterattack tool against a wide range of other leg-lock setups.

## The situation being countered: the short ankle lock

- Opponent passes their foot over the top and locks a triangle around the outside of your legs.
- This creates strong opposing force: your ankle is driven up/backward, their leg down/forward.
- The triangle locks everything in place — a very tight lock once pressure is applied.

## The exploitable weakness

Regardless of the specific lock an opponent uses on you, **some part of their foot is exposed to your hands** — that exposure is your counter opportunity. In the short ankle lock specifically, the triangle-forming foot is the closest and most vulnerable to counterattack.

## The counter mechanism

1. Catch and snatch the exposed triangle foot.
2. Because of how this grip naturally sits, it **feeds directly into a half-foot position** with little extra work.
3. Connect your hands.
4. As the opponent continues to attack your foot, you counter-attack theirs — **your lock is the stronger one**, because you're getting a maximized half-foot position while their own legs are simultaneously locking them in place (they can't escape their own triangle to defend).
5. Cover the foot, bring your ear over your own shoulder, and the lock comes on immediately.

### Why the opponent can't escape even though their exposure looks minor
Normally an exaggerated/loose half-foot grip would be easy to slip out of (opponent slips the heel out). Here, the opponent's own legs (locked around you in their short ankle lock triangle) prevent them from being able to slip their heel free — their own attacking position traps them.

## Drilled Sequence

1. Opponent locks a short ankle lock (foot over the top, triangle around your outside leg).
2. Elbow-deep on their exposed ankle immediately.
3. Go directly into half-foot position, cover, finish.
4. **Grip detail:** grab with the **elbow**, not the hand — faster transition into the lock.
5. Bring your other foot underneath the opponent's hip/butt for base.
6. Finish.

## Progression drilled in the lesson

1. Standard entry from a static short ankle lock position.
2. More combat-realistic: opponent on one knee, standing over you, throws the leg over into a short ankle lock/triangle.
3. Advanced counter-response from the opponent: they hook their foot underneath your hips. Counter: pass the foot out — use your right hand to pass it to your left hand, place right hand on the floor, slide your shin toward your buttocks, then fall toward the opponent to apply the lock.
4. Full speed / live version once the sequence is grooved.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Ankle lock's speed-of-entry advantage (no perfect grip required) makes it a strong universal counterattack to other leg locks.
- Any lock an opponent applies to you exposes some part of their foot — learn to spot it and snatch it.
- Grabbing with the **elbow** rather than the hand is faster for the transition into the counter grip.
- The opponent's own triangle/short-ankle-lock position traps them — they can't slip their heel free even from what looks like a loose grip.
- Practiced progression: static entry → standing/one-knee entry → passing the foot when the opponent hooks under your hips → full speed live rolling.
