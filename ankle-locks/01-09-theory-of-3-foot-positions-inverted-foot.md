# The Theory Of The 3 Foot Positions: Solving The Problem Of The Inverted Foot

Source: `7713668694114-01-09- The Theory Of The 3 Foot Positions- Solving The Problem Of The Inverted Foot.mp4`

## Overview

Addresses the hardest defensive foot position a skilled opponent can achieve: the **inverted foot**, where the opponent rotates their foot to point in the exact opposite direction needed to finish. Rather than treating this as a dead end, the lesson frames it as a **spectrum of three foot positions** and gives a recovery method — chase the escape with your outside leg through the turn, and the foot position naturally improves with each rotation.

---

## The 3 foot positions (from attacker's perspective)

There's a reference line between the opponent's **rear deltoid and tricep/lat muscle**. Where the opponent's big toe points relative to that line defines the position:

| Position | Toe direction | Difficulty to finish |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Ideal / perfect** | Big toe points toward the deltoid-tricep/lat line | Easiest — half-foot is trivial, "snap the ankle like a twig" |
| **2. Half turn** | Toes point toward the opponent's own spine | Harder — must attack from outside-in; still finishable via short ankle lock or belly down |
| **3. Fully inverted** | Toes point in the exact opposite direction of position 1 | Hardest — direct finishing from here is "damn near impossible" |

### From the defender's perspective
- If you're defending, deliberately pointing your toes away from that line — toward your own spine, then fully inverting — is your best escape path.
- The fully inverted position is explicitly the **best defensive foot position** available; getting there is the goal of a well-executed escape.

## Why the inverted foot isn't actually a dead end

Even the worst position (fully inverted) is recoverable — you don't need to abandon the lock. The tool is your **outside leg**, thrown over the opponent's leg to follow their turn.

### The recovery mechanism
- As the opponent turns/inverts to escape, throw your outside leg over the top of their leg and **turn with them** rather than staying static.
- Within roughly **1–3 turns**, the opponent's foot rotates back through the half-turn position and can approach the ideal position again — turning is a two-way street; if you follow it, their escape attempt itself feeds you back a better foot position.
- Once recovered to at least a half-turn position, **belly down finishing** is the natural next step — belly down doesn't require a perfect half-foot to work (per the belly-down lesson), so it's forgiving of a still-imperfect position.
- If the recovery is clean enough, you can even work back to a full half-foot position once belly down and get a very strong finish.

## Practical framing

- Don't treat the fully inverted foot as "the lock is gone, disengage." There is a live recovery path.
- The core skill is **not** fighting the rotation directly — it's **matching/following** the opponent's turn with your outside leg so that their own escape motion converts their foot position back in your favor.
- This works regardless of body orientation — same solution applies whether you're underneath with Ashi Garami or on top with a standing opponent spinning.
- Described as one of the most realistic scenarios you'll face in live rolling: competent training partners will not passively accept an ankle lock, they will actively turn into escapes — this is the tool for that exact situation.

## Drilled Sequence

1. Opponent starts in the ideal finishing position — attacker gets easy half-foot and can feel pressure immediately.
2. Opponent makes a half turn (toes toward own spine) — attacker throws a short ankle lock (triangle) or throws the outside leg over the top; still finishable without half-foot.
3. Opponent fully inverts — attacker's outside leg follows the opponent's turn, tracking through 1–3 rotations, foot position gradually recovers back toward half-turn/ideal.
4. Once recovered, transition to belly down finish (or recover further to full half-foot from belly down for a stronger finish).
5. Same principle drilled with the opponent standing over the attacker (attacker underneath in Ashi Garami) — opponent spins violently, attacker follows the rotation with the outside leg and finishes.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Foot position exists on a spectrum of three states relative to a deltoid-tricep/lat reference line: ideal (easy finish), half turn (harder but finishable), fully inverted (hardest, but not hopeless).
- The fully inverted foot is the opponent's *best* defensive position — but it's still recoverable, not a submission-ending event.
- **Recovery tool: throw your outside leg over their leg and turn with them.** Their own escape rotation feeds your foot position back toward ideal within 1–3 turns.
- Belly down is the natural finish to chase once you've recovered to at least a half-turn position — it doesn't require perfect half-foot.
- This is a core live-rolling skill: skilled training partners will actively turn to escape, and matching their rotation (rather than fighting it or bailing) is how you keep the submission alive.
