# Opponent Stands Up To 2 Feet: Caio Terra DLR Ankle Lock

Source: `7713668694114-02-07- Opponent Stands Up To 2 Feet- Caio Terra Dlr Ankle Lock.mp4`

## Overview

Note: the transcript's ASR consistently mis-hears "Caio Terra lock" as "coyote jerolog" — corrected throughout below.

An alternative response to an opponent standing up, using **De La Riva (DLR) guard**: an ankle lock done from the *inside* out instead of outside-in, trapping the leg at the knee rather than the hip. Mechanically strong, but has a known counter (falling to the "wrong" hip) that a knowledgeable opponent will use — so this move works best as a threat that funnels opponents into your other ankle locks.

---

## Why you need this option

If the opponent stays down on his hips, a standard elbow-deep ankle lock is very hard to escape. So skilled opponents try to **stand up to push the foot flat to the mat** — while the foot is on the mat and weighted, transitioning from Achilles grip to half-foot becomes very difficult (unweighted foot = easy transition; weighted standing foot = hard). You need a technique to return him to his hips.

## Setup: entering DLR

1. Switch to **De La Riva guard** on the standing leg.
2. Get a hold around the ankle, and put your gripping-side hand on your **own stomach** — the further across your stomach it reaches, the tighter/stronger the connection to the opponent's leg. This alone resists a leg-yank-out escape.
3. Problem: opponent can still walk around / backstep with his free foot and push down on the trapped knee to reduce the connection.
4. Fix: keep the DLR hook in, and hook your **second foot vigorously behind the opponent's (other) knee** — creating a strong tripping connection, similar dynamic to a tripod sweep.

## The trip and finish

1. With the second-foot knee hook set, push in one direction with the DLR hook while tripping the other leg behind the knee.
2. Bump the opponent down **toward your hand** (the hand on your own stomach) — this is critical: it puts him down onto the hip **on the same side as the leg you're controlling** — the "correct" hip.
3. Close your knees, connect your hands, come down to the shoulder.
4. Strong finishing pressure from here — described as easily capable of breaking the ankle.

## What this lock actually is, mechanically

A variation of a **short Achilles-style lock** but controlling the **knee** instead of the hip — driving your hips forward from the shoulder-down position generates significant breaking pressure.

---

## The Counter (and why you must know it)

This lock is entirely dependent on taking the opponent down to the **correct hip** (same side as the trapped leg). A knowledgeable opponent's counter:

1. As soon as he feels he's about to be knocked down, he **deliberately falls to the "wrong" hip** (away from the trapped leg side).
2. From there he can either:
   - Stiff-arm your grip with his free hand and fake a turn toward you, then limp the leg out, or
   - Scoop and elevate his own ankle to free it and escape.

This single counter, done correctly, **spoils the entire finish** — the lock depends on hip direction and doesn't work if the opponent controls which hip he lands on.

## How to actually use this in live rolling

Treat the Caio Terra lock as a **probe/threat**, not a guaranteed finish:
1. Threaten the takedown to assess the opponent's ankle-lock game IQ.
2. If he has no answer (falls to the correct hip or doesn't defend) → finish directly.
3. If he's knowledgeable and deliberately falls to the wrong hip → **abandon this lock and transition into the other ankle lock variations** covered earlier in the course (pull his near foot in, pass through to another finish).

Playing back and forth between this lock and the standard ankle locks — based on reading the opponent's defensive IQ — is the intended live strategy.

## Key Takeaways

- Answers the "opponent stands and weights his foot" problem via **De La Riva guard**, not just the both-ankles approach from the previous lesson.
- Key grip detail: hand reaches across your own stomach for connection strength; second foot hooks the opponent's far knee for a tripping effect.
- **Must drop the opponent to the correct hip** — same side as the trapped leg — or the lock doesn't work.
- Known counter: opponent deliberately falls to the wrong hip and escapes (stiff-arm + limp leg, or scoop-and-elevate).
- Best used as a threat/probe to assess opponent skill, with a ready transition back into your other ankle lock options if countered.
