# Reverse Figure 4 Grip Cross Ankle Lock

Source: `7713668694114-05-06- Reverse Figure 4 Grip Cross Ankle Lock.mp4`

## Overview

An alternate, higher-power grip for cross ankle locks — not a default, but a tool for when the conventional grip isn't getting the ankle exposed and a tough opponent needs extra horsepower. Trade-off: more power, but an extra step, so it's slower to apply than the standard grip.

---

## When to use it

- Conventional cross ankle lock grips are efficient and remain your primary tool.
- Reach for the reverse figure-four specifically when: you have the outside Ashi Garami in place, you're trying to get the half-foot grip, and **the opponent is tough enough that the ankle won't expose normally.**

## How to build it

Setup: attacking the training partner's **right** leg, outside Ashi Garami already in place.

1. Feed your hands through — go **elbow to elbow**.
2. **Critical detail:** for a right-handed lock, your **left forearm goes behind the right forearm**. Get this backward and "it's going to screw up everything."
3. Equivalently: your left hand goes behind, in between your right arm and your own body.
4. As the hand emerges on the other side, you get the opportunity to lock up the **reverse figure-four grip**.
5. From here, slip down toward the floor, then drive the elbow in toward your own hip.

## Why it's stronger

- Adds meaningfully to breaking power compared to the standard grip.
- Same underlying breaking mechanic as a **figure-four toe hold** — folding the foot to the inside, exposing the vulnerable ligament on the side of the ankle. The reverse figure-four grip gives you more capacity to execute that fold.
- Ankle bone visibility remains the same prerequisite for success as with every other ankle lock — confirm you can see it once locked in.

## Trade-off

- Requires an extra step (feeding the hand through correctly, elbow-to-elbow) versus the standard grip — takes more time to set up.
- Not intended as an every-time tool; use selectively when the standard grip isn't producing ankle exposure against a tough opponent.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Reverse figure-four = situational power upgrade, not a default grip — reach for it when the standard grip stalls against a resistant opponent.
- **Arm-crossing direction matters**: right-handed lock → left forearm behind right forearm. Reversing this breaks the setup.
- Mechanically equivalent to a figure-four toe hold — same vulnerable ligament, same fold-to-the-inside action, just more leverage.
- Cost: an extra step, so slower to apply than the conventional grip.
