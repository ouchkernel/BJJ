# Entering Ashi Garami Ankle Lock On A Standing Opponent

## Overview

Third and final entry scenario: opponent has **no knees down** — fully standing. Covers square stance vs. bladed (staggered) stance, and a separate high-percentage method targeting the back leg specifically.

---

## First question: square stance or bladed stance?

- Most opponents naturally stand in a **bladed stance** (one foot forward, one back).
- Some stand square — you need an answer for both.

## Entry vs. a square stance

1. Approach and get a **two-on-one wrist grip**, transitioning to a two-on-one elbow grip.
2. Bring both feet in together and pull — dig your heels into the mat.
3. Go to a supine position with both feet hooked behind the training partner's knees.
4. Give a small lift to force his hands down toward the mat.
5. Kick through into Ashi Garami.
6. Standard follow-up: hand to the knee, leg around the training partner, Ashi grip secured.
7. Even if he puts weight forward onto the mat, hit a **forward shift** to take the foot off the mat into the half-foot position.
8. If he's strong and throws himself over the top, follow with a hook into the **belly-down position**.

## Entry vs. a bladed stance — attack the front leg

1. Put your foot in front, catch the training partner.
2. Bring both feet close together on the inside.
3. Pull, bringing his knee into your stomach.
4. Kick over into Ashi Garami.
5. Sit up, secure the half-foot grip, finish.

## Entry vs. a bladed stance — attack the back leg (preferred)

Rationale: the front leg is weight-bearing and hard to pull forward. The **back leg is unweighted and easy to pull forward.**

1. Identify the forward leg and the back leg.
2. Go hand-fighting for the wrist **on the same side as the back leg**.
3. Take a two-on-one grip and pull — this catches the opponent behind the knees.
4. Even if he tries to push away, you're wrist-deep around the knee — a very strong grip.
5. Pull him into a seated position with your knees inside his knees.
6. You can now choose to attack **either side** of his body.
7. Proceed into the standard ankle lock technique from there.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Standing opponents come in two flavors — square or bladed — and each needs a specific entry.
- Square stance: two-on-one wrist→elbow grip, feet hooked behind the knees, small lift, kick through.
- Bladed stance, front-leg attack: foot in front, feet inside, pull knee to stomach, kick over.
- **Bladed stance, back-leg attack is the higher-percentage option**: the back leg is unweighted and easy to pull, versus the front leg which is weight-bearing and resistant.
- Once you've caught the opponent behind the knees with a strong wrist-deep grip, you get to choose which side to attack — real optionality, not a forced line.
