# Short Ankle Locks

Source: `7713668694114-01-07- Short Ankle Locks.mp4`

## Overview

Introduces the **short ankle lock** — an alternative to controlling the hip that instead controls the area immediately in front of the opponent's knee. It creates a more direct, more powerful opposing force in a shorter amount of time, but is more readily defended by a skilled opponent than conventional (hip-control) methods. Framed explicitly as an **addition** to your ankle locking game, not a replacement for hip control.

---

## Why control the knee instead of the hip?

- Recall: hip control exists to create an opposing force against your ankle lock's upward pull.
- Controlling the area right in front of the knee does the same job, but **more directly** — the opposing force acts right at the joint being isolated, producing faster, stronger breaking pressure.
- Tradeoff: it's a shorter/quicker lever, so it's easier for a skilled opponent to disrupt by simply moving/pulling on the knee.

## Core mechanic

- Butterfly Ashi on the training partner's leg.
- Take your inside leg over the training partner's ankle/leg and fall down to the side.
- This creates strong, fast opposing pressure directly on the leg — described as "quite difficult for your opponent to defend" **in a short period of time**, though not durable against sustained resistance.

## Variations by opponent posture

### Opponent seated/on the ground, coming toward you
1. Punch the arm in, lock the classic ankle lock grip, half-foot as usual.
2. Control distance — right foot behind the opponent's knee prevents them closing distance.
3. Cast your leg over the top of the lock, over the opponent's knee, come down into position.
4. Creates a strong direct opposing force over the knee → fast breaking pressure.

### Opponent comes up to a knee (posturing up)
1. Foot stays behind opponent's hamstring.
2. Cast leg over the top, lock a **closed triangle**, foot threaded inside the opponent's hip.
3. Advantage: **you don't need the half-foot position for this to work** — even with the whole sole of the foot visible (not half-foot), the leg positioning alone still makes it very difficult for the opponent to drive the foot to the floor.
4. Cast the second leg over as well, lock a quick triangle, place foot behind the opponent's head/leg — even rudimentary hand grips now create strong pressure, half-foot or not.

## The hips-on-floor vs. hips-off-floor rule

A second useful rule of thumb specific to short ankle locks:

| Opponent's hips are... | Use |
|---|---|
| **On the floor** | **Open loop** around the training partner's knee (no triangle) — floor itself does the constraining |
| **Off the floor** (coming up) | **Closed loop / triangle** around the opponent's knee |

Reasoning: if hips are down, the mat already constrains movement, so a loose (open) loop is enough. If the opponent lifts his hips, an open loop lets him simply pull the knee free with both hands — so you need the closed triangle to physically prevent that.

## Knee direction still matters here too

- Knee facing **outward** (with half-foot achieved): favor finishing over the top as previously shown.
- Knee facing **inward** and opponent coming up: exactly the situation for the triangle-around-the-knee finish — you can finish even without half-foot.
- If the opponent's knee begins to turn outward from wherever you are, that's your cue to throw the foot over the top.

## Limitations — why this isn't your primary tool

- The short ankle lock is a **few-down-bikes lever** — i.e. shorter distance / less mechanical stability — a skilled opponent can, more often than with conventional hip control, pull his leg/knee free and start defending or counter-attacking.
- Skilled opponents, sensing danger, will typically open the knee and prevent the triangle from locking in the first place.
- Because it's short, it tends to produce **either a very fast submission, or nothing at all** — it doesn't degrade gracefully into a grind like hip control does.
- Recommendation: **use it as an addition, not the primary method.** Expect to play back and forth between knee control (short ankle lock) and hip control (earlier lessons) depending on what the opponent defends.

## Drilled Sequence (illustrating the play between the two)

1. Get a hold of the opponent, establish the butterfly grip.
2. If opponent stays on the hip (doesn't defend the knee), throw the leg over → finish (short ankle lock).
3. If opponent sees the leg coming over and defends by pulling the knee up short, you must **revert to conventional (hip-control) finishing methods** covered in earlier lessons.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Short ankle lock = control the area in front of the knee instead of the hip → faster, more direct opposing force, but a shorter/weaker lever overall.
- Works even without a half-foot position — a genuine advantage over hip-control methods in some scenarios.
- Rule of thumb: opponent's hips on the floor → open loop around the knee; hips off the floor → closed triangle around the knee.
- Skilled opponents will often defend the knee successfully — treat this as a supplementary weapon, and be ready to fall back to hip-control breaking methods when the knee gets defended.
- The overall skill is **playing between knee control and hip control** based on what the opponent gives up in real time.
