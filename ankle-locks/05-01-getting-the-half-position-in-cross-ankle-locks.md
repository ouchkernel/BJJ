# Getting The Half Position In Cross Ankle Locks

## Overview

Applies the half-foot concept (already established for straight ankle locks) to **cross ankle locks**. Confirms: yes, you still want half-foot in cross ankle locks too — but getting there differs because of the angular change from a straight to a cross Ashi Garami. Covers two methods for exposing the ankle: hollow body, and the instructor's preferred method, the hip heist.

---

## Straight vs. cross ankle lock — definitions

- **Straight ankle lock:** opponent's *left* foot goes to your right armpit.
- **Cross ankle lock:** opponent's *right* foot goes through your right armpit.

## Half-foot recap (straight ankle lock)

Take the heel of the opponent's foot and bring it in front of your chest so only the **top half of the sole** is visible to an observer. This makes it hard for them to kick the leg through *and* hard to pull the foot out — creating real breaking pressure.

## Half-foot in cross ankle locks

Same target result: sole of the foot in front of you, only the **ball of the foot** visible. Opponent can neither yank the foot out nor kick it through.

### Why cross ankle locks hit differently (and often harder)
The half-foot cross ankle lock targets the **anterior talofibular ligament** — the ligament on the outside of the ankle that's commonly injured when you roll your ankle stepping wrong. Cross ankle locks stress this ligament **more strongly** than straight ankle locks do.

- Mechanism: roll the opponent's toes toward the pinky-toe side, rolling the foot inward — similar in feel and damage profile to a **toe hold**.
- Effect: rolls the ankle inward, direct pressure onto the ATFL (anterior talofibular ligament), capable of severe damage.

## Visual cue: same as straight ankle locks

You need to be able to **see the opponent's ankle bone.** If it's hidden, the lock is probably not fully efficient. The moment you first snatch the ankle is when you should check this — once you cover the foot to finish, you obviously won't see it anymore, but on entry, visibility is the check.

## The sweet spot (same Goldilocks problem as straight ankle locks)

- Ankle too far behind you → no breaking pressure.
- Ankle too far in front → opponent can limp the leg free.
- Same balance point applies to cross ankle locks as straight ones.

---

## Method 1: Hollow Body

1. Post on the training partner's leg — heel of the hand on the outside of the knee, or thumb underneath the knee.
2. Push forward on the knee.
3. Round/hollow your body out — this hollowing is what exposes the ankle bone to view.
4. Cover the grip.
5. Test: opponent tries to push foot through (should fail), then tries to limp leg out (should also fail).

### Critical defensive detail: knee-down escape
The opponent's main way to limp the leg out is by **turning their knee down and pulling away.** Counter: once you get the grip, pass your foot and place your knee **inside** the training partner's knee. This forms a natural barrier — when they try to turn the knee down, your knee blocks it. This is where reliable breaks come from.

### Finishing mechanics
- Once tight and knee-blocked, connect your hands — this becomes a rotational finish, similar in feel to a toe hold.
- **Elbow height matters:** the higher your elbow, the less pressure the opponent feels. Keep the elbow down.
- Sequence: (1) get tight, (2) rotate. Rotating through toward the hip, elbow coming down, is what produces the strong finish.

## Method 2: Hip Heist (instructor's preferred method)

Hollow body works but struggles against a strongly resistant opponent who knows what a cross ankle lock is and locks their leg out hard — pushing the leg through even against strong hand pressure (e.g., thumb pressure) can become difficult.

### Mechanism
1. Take the leg, come down into position.
2. **Hip heist:** turn your hips toward the floor.
3. This heisting action is what exposes the ankle bone and slips the heel into the perfect half-foot position — not pure hand-pressure/pushing.
4. If your elbow is too tight, the heel can't become visible — **loosen your grip slightly and flare your elbow forward** to catch the heel.
5. Once locked in this position, it's on immediately.
6. Connect feet, fall to your finishing position (same finish covered earlier).

### Why this is the better method
Works well even against strong, resistant opponents where the hollow-body/pushing method starts to fail. Against a naive opponent, any basic pushing method exposes the ankle fine — but against a locked-out, resistant leg, the hip heist succeeds where pure pushing does not.

---

## Combined Sequence Recap

1. Start with an Achilles grip.
2. Test: opponent tries to yank out (should be very difficult) — but note they *can* push through with this grip alone, so it's not enough.
3. Make the ankle bone visible via either:
   - Post + hollow body, **or**
   - Hip heist (preferred against strong resistance).
4. Protect against the knee-down escape: pass your inside leg over the top, place your knee inside theirs — this caps how far they can turn the knee down.
5. Cross your own ankles for safety (prevents opponent attacking your ankle).
6. Connect hands.
7. Finish: tightness first, then rotation — knee across, elbow down, rotate through to the hip.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Half-foot theory applies to cross ankle locks exactly as it does to straight ankle locks — same visual cue (see the ankle bone), same Goldilocks sweet spot.
- Cross ankle locks target the anterior talofibular ligament and generally stress it more directly than straight ankle locks — feels/damages like a toe hold.
- Two ways to expose the ankle: **hollow body + post** (good baseline) or **hip heist** (better against strong resistant opponents).
- The opponent's main escape is turning the knee down to limp out — block this by passing your leg over the top and pinning your knee inside theirs.
- Finish mechanics: get tight first, then rotate — low elbow, knee across, rotate toward the hip.
- Skill priority: reliably getting to half-foot (in both straight and cross variants) is the foundational skill; everything downstream gets easier once this is automatic.
