# Introducing Belly Down Ankle Locks

## Overview

Introduces **belly down finishing** — described as producing at least 50% of all ankle lock finishes. Going belly down (sprawling face-down over the position) massively increases breaking force by adding body weight and using the floor itself to pin the opponent's knee, creating the strongest version of the "opposing force" concept from the previous lesson.

---

## Recap tying into this lesson

- Ankle lock = two opposing forces: upper body grip creating force **up and away**, and a downward force on the opponent's hip (or knee) as counter-pressure.
- Half-foot is easiest when the opponent's knee points **outward**; hardest when it points **inward**.
- Even without a perfect half-foot (e.g., toes pointing toward the spine), meaningful breaking pressure is still achievable — belly down is how you access that pressure without a perfect grip.

## What "belly down" actually is

- You go face-down/prone over the position rather than staying upright or on your side.
- This lets you add body weight directly into the opponent's leg, and — critically — **the floor pins the opponent's knee**, constraining their movement in a way upright positions can't.
- Belly down finishes are, in the instructor's assessment, the **strongest finishes in the whole system**.

## Why belly down matters practically

- **It's forgiving of imperfect grips.** In an upright/belly-up position, you need many things right simultaneously (half-foot, elbow to centerline, etc.). Belly down generates so much force that even a suboptimal grip is often still enough to finish.
- Use case: whenever you can't get the half-foot position (opponent defends well, knee points inward, etc.), go belly down instead of forcing a bad upright finish.
- The stronger your opponent, the more you'll end up relying on belly down finishes — skilled opponents will fight for escapes, which naturally funnel into belly down opportunities as you follow their movement.

## Mechanics of the belly down transition

1. Lock hands using any grip already covered (figure-four wrist, figure-four bicep, or grab-your-own-elbow).
2. Put a hand on the floor and arch in — a motion similar to sprawling.
3. This sprawling action creates **much stronger** opposing force on the hips than an upright position can generate.
4. The floor blocks the opponent's knee from moving, further constraining escape.

### One-handed vs. two-handed finishes — body mechanics differ
| | One-handed finish | Two-handed finish |
|---|---|---|
| Support | Hand or forearm on the floor | Body curls fully around opponent's leg |
| Head position | Rises up | Comes down, close to the floor |
| Hip position | Drops down | Drops down |
| Body shape | Sprawl (like a wrestling sprawl) | Hollow/curled shape |

Common thread: **head rises, hips drop** in the one-handed version; in the two-handed version, the whole body forms a hollow shape around the opponent's leg while still sprawling to finish.

## Drilled Sequence

1. Start in a seated/standard position, lock a hand grip (figure-four wrist, bicep, or own-elbow — all work).
2. If not already in half-foot, hollow the body and drive to half-foot from this belly-down entry as needed.
3. Sprawl — spread your knees and press into the sprawl to finish.
4. Practiced across all three grip types; grabbing your own elbow was called out as producing the strongest finish of the three.
5. **Turning to the outside:** when the opponent's knee points outward, transition naturally into belly down from there.
6. **Turning to the inside:** if the opponent turns the other way (avoiding half-foot), lock a closed triangle/loop over the opponent's legs, separate your knees to create the same breaking force, and finish two-handed with a sprawl.
7. Regardless of direction the opponent turns, belly down provides a route to a strong finish.

## Notable observation from the roll

Training partners were reported sweating heavily and needing extra reps specifically during belly down drilling — attributed to the position being **more physically painful** to defend against due to the added body weight and the mat pinning the knee, creating a very strong opposing force.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Belly down finishes account for roughly half of all ankle lock finishes in this system — a core skill, not an optional add-on.
- Mechanism: body weight + floor pinning the opponent's knee = much stronger opposing force than any upright position can generate.
- Belly down is the answer when you **can't** get a perfect half-foot grip — it compensates for imperfect hand positioning.
- One-handed finish = sprawl with head rising, hips dropping. Two-handed finish = body curls/hollows around the opponent's leg while still sprawling.
- Works regardless of which direction the opponent's knee points or which way they try to turn — outside turns flow naturally into belly down; inside turns use a triangle/closed loop + knee-spread to the same effect.
