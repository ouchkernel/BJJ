# The Best Upper Body Grips For Ankle Locks

## Overview

Different top ankle lockers use different hand grips, which raises the natural question of which is "best." The answer given here: **the specific hand grip barely matters — elbow position is what matters.** This lesson catalogs the main hand grip options and the tradeoffs between them, all judged against a single standard: how well does this grip let you drive your elbow to the centerline.

---

## The core principle

- Goal: **make a training partner tap from the upper body grip alone** — this is the first benchmark of the course.
- **Elbow position, not hand grip, determines success.** The closer the elbow gets to your own centerline (ideally settling inside your own hip), the more effective the lock.
- This is why the instructor isn't a fan of the classical ankle lock — it locks you into a bad (wide/outside) elbow position by design.
- Different hand grips are just different tools for achieving the same goal: elbow deep around the opponent's ankle, elbow driven across to the centerline.

## The 4 grip options

### 1. Figure-four wrist grip (classic hand grip)
- Elbow goes around the partner's ankle, grab your own wrist, other hand grabs the partner's shin, lock up.
- **Pros:**
  - Enables both a pulling force AND a pushing force — useful for moving the foot into half-foot position.
  - Hand-on-shin lets you hold your elbow position and get straight into the grip.
  - Puts a **floor** on how low your hands can drop (hand catches on your own forearm) — forms a hard bar under the opponent's ankle, hard to mess up.
  - Limits how far the opponent's heel can rotate when they try to slip it — some inherent heel-slip resistance.
- **Cons:** Own forearm gets in the way, making it harder to push the elbow all the way across the centerline.

### 2. Figure-four bicep grip
- Grab your own bicep and your own shoulder, lock up.
- **Pros:** Easier to get the elbow inside your own hip (best pulling grip for centerline).
- **Cons:** Can only pull, no push. If opponent gets a hold of your wrist, easier for them to limp their heel through and slip out.

### 3. Grab your own elbow
- **Pros:** Strongest of all three for driving the elbow all the way to a good breaking position — best centerline pull.
- **Cons:** Highest exposure to heel-slip — opponent has the best opportunity to grab your hands and limp the leg out.

### 4. Compromise grip (bar over the ankle — described but not explicitly named)
- Puts a bar on top of the opponent's ankle, making heel-slip difficult, while still allowing push/pull like the figure-four wrist grip.
- Forearm can never drop too low.
- **Con:** Harder to bring the elbow all the way across the centerline compared to bicep or elbow grips.

## Tradeoff summary

| Grip | Push+Pull? | Elbow-to-centerline | Heel-slip resistance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Figure-four wrist | Yes | Moderate (forearm blocks it) | Good |
| Figure-four bicep | Pull only | Good | Poor |
| Grab own elbow | Pull only | Best | Worst |
| Compromise/bar grip | Yes | Moderate-poor | Good |

**Practical note:** once feet are in the correct position (proper half-foot / breaking position), heel-slip risk drops substantially regardless of which grip is used — grip choice matters most when foot position isn't yet ideal.

## Common failure modes

- **Daylight** around the elbow/ankle connection — usually an easy fix, just get elbow-deep.
- **Foot too far through, or too far in front** — opponent can slip out. Solution: half of the foot visible only (half-foot theory), regardless of which grip you use.

## What comes next

Drilling revealed a new problem: even with a locked-in grip, the opponent can **raise/move his hips** to relieve pressure — no amount of upper body grip quality solves this on its own. This sets up the next lesson's focus: immobilizing the opponent's hips.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Hand grip choice is secondary. **Elbow position (drive to centerline, ideally inside your own hip) is the real determinant of success.**
- Four grip options, each trading off push/pull capability, ease of reaching centerline, and heel-slip vulnerability:
  - Figure-four wrist: push+pull, moderate centerline, good heel-slip resistance.
  - Figure-four bicep: pull only, good centerline, poor heel-slip resistance.
  - Grab own elbow: pull only, best centerline, worst heel-slip resistance.
  - Compromise/bar grip: push+pull, moderate-poor centerline, good heel-slip resistance.
- No matter the grip, failure modes are the same: daylight (fix: elbow-deep) and foot too far through/too far in front (fix: half-foot).
- Good upper body mechanics alone aren't enough — opponents will move their hips to escape pressure, which is the next problem to solve.
