# The One Handed Cross Ankle Lock Variant

## Overview

An underused variant: one-handed cross ankle locks. Seemingly contradicts earlier advice (favor locked/connected hands so the grip can't slide down and bleed power), but one-handed grips unlock a different mechanism — a stronger turning/toehold-like force — when body orientation is right.

---

## The Golden Rule

**The more your chest faces down toward the floor, the more effective one-handed variations become.**

| Chest orientation | Grip choice |
|---|---|
| Chest facing up (ceiling) | Use connected/locked hands |
| Chest facing to the side | Still recommend connected hands |
| Chest rotating down toward the floor | This is when one-handed grips generate real power |

Reasoning: turning your chest toward the floor lets you enact a stronger turning force on the opponent's ankle, similar to a toehold, that a locked two-hand grip can't replicate as effectively in that orientation.

## The failure mode: hand slipping down

- The biggest risk with one-handed grips is the hand sliding down and bleeding pressure — the training partner reports feeling pressure release the moment the hand lowers.
- **A closed fist reduces surface area and contact/drag, making slippage more likely.**
- Fix: **use the blade of the hand**, placed as high as possible, then turn the hand flat onto the opponent's stomach to maximize drag/friction against your own body. This grip resists sliding far better than a fist.

## Mechanics of the finish

1. Always expose the training partner's ankle first — same rule as every other ankle lock in this course. Never give the opponent an easy limp-out.
2. Put the knee over the top, then get the hand in position.
3. **Positioning check:** thumb should land just below your own pectoral muscle. The further the thumb slips down toward your hips, the less effective the grip becomes.
4. From there, roll over the top and pin the training partner's knee.
5. Drop the knee, drive head up and hips forward — finish.

## Distance/positioning detail

- If you stay too close to the opponent, they get an opportunity to grip-fight you.
- Better: **move away from the opponent (toward the camera/away from their body), pin their foot to the floor** — the more distance and pin you create, the harder it is for them to hand-fight and find something to grab. Driving in from that distance tightens very quickly.

## Direction flexibility

- Demonstrated with an outside turn, but **works in either turning direction** (inside or outside) — regardless of which side the foot started on, turning through and connecting the feet creates the same devastating one-handed lock.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Golden rule: **chest down toward the floor → one-handed grip becomes viable and strong; chest up or to the side → stick with connected hands.**
- Grip with the **blade of the hand held high**, then flatten it onto the stomach for drag — not a fist, which slips more easily.
- Thumb position is a built-in gauge: just below your pec = good; sliding toward the hips = grip degrading.
- Create distance from the opponent and pin their foot to the floor to deny them a hand-fighting target.
- Works turning either direction (inside or outside) — not locked to one side.
