## Overview

Framing video for the whole ankle lock series. No technique yet — sets up why the ankle lock matters, how it's changed, and how the course is structured.

---

## Why the ankle lock matters

- First leg lock most jiu-jitsu students encounter.
- **Only leg lock allowed at all levels, in both gi and no-gi** — unlike heel hooks, which are restricted by belt level in most rule sets.
- High percentage, competition-proven submission at every level.

## How its evolution differs from heel hooks

When the instructor changed the heel hook game, most of the innovation was in **surrounding structure** — decision trees, control systems, how locks are organized together as a network rather than isolated moves.

The ankle lock's evolution is different: **the lock itself changed**, not just the system around it.

### What actually changed
1. **Target of attack**
   - Old: back of ankle — Achilles tendon, attacked via compression from the blade of the wrist. Produces discomfort, not real damage.
   - New: front of ankle — anterior talofibular ligaments, anterior tibialis tendon, anterior ankle joint capsule. Capable of real structural damage, on par with heel hooks/toe holds.
2. **Grip**
   - Old: wrist-focused grip around the Achilles.
   - New: elbow-focused grip — the **half-foot grip**.
3. **Leg control**
   - Old: standard Ashi Garami entanglements.
   - New: more effective methods of pinning the opponent's **hips and knee**, not just the ankle itself.

## Advantages over heel hooks

- Grip on the leg is more robust/simpler to apply, harder for opponent to strip.
- Generally less learning time required to become effective.
- **Synergizes with heel hooks**: the defensive movements opponents use to escape heel hooks tend to funnel directly into ankle lock opportunities.

## Course structure — 3 core skills

1. **Upper body grip** — getting an efficient, devastating grip on the foot.
2. **Leg entanglement control** — using Ashi Garami to pin the opponent's hip or knee, creating opposing force against the upper body grip.
3. **Decision trees for resistance** — how to adapt when a skilled opponent counters your initial lock and you need to transition to a different ankle lock variation.

Course will also cover **straight ankle locks vs. cross ankle locks** — similar but with important differences; both need to be trained.

## Proof of concept

Modern ankle locking isn't theoretical — cited as effective in current gi/no-gi competition by athletes including Mateus Diniz, Michael Musumeci, Adele Fornarino, and the instructor's student Helena Crevar (described as possibly the most prolific ankle locker in modern competition).

---

## Key Takeaways

- Ankle lock = only universally-legal leg lock, high percentage, worth prioritizing early.
- Unlike heel hooks, the **lock mechanics themselves** evolved, not just the surrounding system.
- Three things changed together: target (front vs. back of ankle), grip (elbow-deep half-foot vs. wrist-deep), and leg control (hip/knee pinning vs. basic Ashi Garami).
- Course roadmap: grip → leg entanglement control → decision trees → straight vs. cross variations.
