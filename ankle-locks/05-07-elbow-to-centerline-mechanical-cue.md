# A Simple Mechanical Cue That Will Add Greatly To All Your Ankle Locks

## Overview

A single, universal cue that applies to **every** ankle lock covered in this course — straight or cross. Simple enough to remember under the stress of live sparring or competition, and described as making a real, concrete, fast difference to performance.

---

## The cue

**The more your elbow moves toward your centerline, the more successful you'll be. The more it drifts away from your centerline and forward, the less successful you'll be.**

- Weakest position: elbow out and away (forward, away from centerline).
- Strongest position: elbow retracted back toward yourself, then moved toward the centerline.

This applies during the transition from Achilles grip into ankle lock grip, regardless of lock type.

## What actually happens mechanically

- Bringing the elbow back (without yet moving to centerline) tightens the lock, but **the hand doesn't rise** at that stage.
- Only once the elbow moves **toward the centerline** does the hand start to rise. That rise is the mechanism that matters.

## Why hand height matters so much

- **The lower your hand relative to the opponent's ankle, the easier it is for them to limp their leg free.**
- **The higher your hand relative to their ankle, the harder it is for them to limp out** — even before you've reached full half-foot position.
- The only reliable way to raise your hand is to fold the elbow back and bring it toward the centerline — hand height is a direct consequence of elbow position, not something to chase independently.

## Effect on both major failure modes

Applying the cue (elbow back + toward centerline) makes both classic escapes harder, even before half-foot is achieved:

- **Kick-through:** harder because of the elbow position alone.
- **Limp-out:** harder because the resulting higher hand position sits above the ankle, denying the leverage the opponent needs to slip free. Not impossible, but significantly tougher.

## Applies to cross ankle locks too

- Same logic: the further the elbow sits forward (away from centerline), the easier it is for the opponent to either kick through or limp out.
- Fix is identical: get elbow-deep around the training partner's ankle, then bring the elbow forward/back toward the centerline so your hand sits higher than their ankle.
- Result: much tighter connections and a harder person to escape from or defend against.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Universal cue for every ankle lock in this system:** elbow toward centerline = good; elbow forward/away = bad.
- Hand height is the actual mechanism — it only rises once the elbow comes toward centerline, not just from pulling the elbow back.
- Low hand relative to opponent's ankle = easy limp-out. High hand relative to their ankle = hard limp-out.
- One cue defends against both major escapes (kick-through and limp-out) simultaneously, and works identically for straight and cross ankle locks.
- Simple enough to consciously check for even under full sparring/competition pressure.
