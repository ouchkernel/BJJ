# The 4 Main Finishing Positions For Ankle Locking

Source: `7713668694114-01-08- The 4 Main Finishing Positions For Ankle Locking.mp4`

## Overview

Once opponents get good at defending ankle locks, resistance comes in two categories — **linear** (opponent's leg pumping forward/back — "putting the boot on" or limping the leg out) and **rotational** (opponent spinning/inverting/turning). To handle rotational resistance across all possible angles, you need the ability to finish from **four distinct positions**, covering a full 360 degrees of potential opponent rotation.

---

## Two categories of resistance

### Linear resistance
- **Toward you:** opponent "puts the boot on" — kicks/pushes the leg through, making the lock hard to finish.
- **Away from you:** opponent fights your hands and limps the leg out.

### Rotational resistance
- Opponent puts a foot in an inside position, then inverts/spins.
- Once inverted, if you just hold your grip statically, the toes end up pointing the wrong direction and the finish is lost.
- **Key defensive requirement for the opponent:** they must secure an inside foot position before it's safe for them to invert/rotate. If they try to rotate without that inside position first, you simply follow the rotation and keep the same lock.

## The 4 finishing positions

You should be able to finish comfortably in **all four** of these, since a skilled opponent can rotate to any of them at any time:

1. **Outside shoulder / outside hip** — the standard finish when the opponent's knee points outward. Fall to the outside shoulder, finish.
2. **Inside shoulder / inside hip** — when the opponent's knee points to the inside (harder to get true half-foot), pass your leg over the opponent's legs and finish from the inside.
3. **Belly down** — regardless of which direction the opponent rotates, you should be able to transition to belly down and finish (see prior lesson — belly down doesn't require perfect half-foot).
4. **Flat back finish** — used when the opponent comes up to a knee/posts up. Get a strong foot position (foot on the opponent's calf), then rotate your own body so you end up flat on your back, and finish from there.

## The grip robustness vs. breaking-pressure tradeoff

A central concept introduced here: **the Achilles/ankle grip depth is a tradeoff, not a free choice.**

- Take the opponent's foot only a **little** way through → grip is extremely robust (opponent basically can't pull the foot out), but you get **minimal breaking pressure**.
- Take the foot **all the way through** to a strong breaking position → strong breaking pressure, but the grip becomes fragile and the opponent can lift/pull the foot free.
- **You must find a satisfactory middle ground** between robustness and breaking mechanics — you cannot maximize both simultaneously.
- This middle ground is found through **live feedback from a resisting partner**, not a fixed formula:
  1. Take the foot partway through.
  2. Have your partner violently try to push the foot through toward you — you should be able to stop it.
  3. Have your partner violently try to limp the leg out / twist the knee — you should be able to stop that too.
  4. Pinch your knees around the opponent's knee to add a layer of security while you calibrate.
  5. Adjust foot depth until both tests fail for your partner — that's your working middle ground.

## Drilled Sequence

1. Establish grip and calibrate the robustness/breaking tradeoff via partner feedback (push-through test, limp-out test).
2. Once calibrated, practice finishing from all four positions in sequence:
   - Outside hip.
   - Inside hip.
   - Flat back (opponent comes up to a knee — foot on calf, rotate to flat back, finish).
   - Belly down (opponent spins — follow the spin, finish belly down).
3. Goal: be equally comfortable finishing from any of the four regardless of which way the opponent rotates or postures.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Resistance to ankle locks splits into linear (push through / limp out) and rotational (spin/invert) categories.
- An opponent needs an inside foot position secured before it's safe for them to invert — if they rotate without it, you just follow and keep the lock.
- Master **four finishing positions** — outside hip, inside hip, belly down, flat back — to cover a skilled opponent's full 360-degree range of rotation/escape attempts.
- Grip depth is a genuine tradeoff between robustness (hard to strip) and breaking power (hard to generate) — there's no setting that maximizes both. Calibrate the middle ground using live partner resistance testing (push-through test + limp-out test).
- Being equally dangerous from all four positions is what separates a dangerous ankle locker from one who only wins when the opponent cooperates.
