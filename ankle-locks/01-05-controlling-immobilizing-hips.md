# The Crucial 2nd Step Of Ankle Locking: Controlling And Immobilizing Your Opponent's Hips

## Overview

Recap of skill #1 (upper body mechanics) and introduction of skill #2: **hip control**. No matter how good the upper body grip is, an opponent can relieve all pressure by moving his hips — so a second, independent mechanism is needed to immobilize the hips (or later, the knee) as an opposing anchor.

---

## Recap: upper body mechanics (skill #1)

- Elbow-deep around the ankle, no daylight.
- Half-foot position: heel in front of you, ball of foot still behind — the middle ground between too-far-through and too-far-out.
- Elbow inside your hip / aligned with centerline whenever possible.

## The core problem this lesson solves

- Once breaking grips are applied, the opponent's default reaction is to **lift/move his hips** — this relieves the pressure you're generating.
- No matter how you orient relative to the opponent, moving the hips is always available to them as a release valve.
- This is why classical ankle locks were easy to escape: the classic Ashi Garami let opponents simply sit up on top of their own heel, with the attacker having zero ability to control hip movement.
- **Common escape patterns that all involve hip movement:**
  1. Opponent comes up on top, gets foot to the floor.
  2. Opponent inverts — spins/turns and moves through on their shoulders.
  3. Opponent skips over the Ashi Garami and moves out to the side.
- Conclusion: to succeed, you need to either immobilize the opponent's hips, or (more realistically) **move with** the opponent's hips rather than trying to stay static against them.

## The fix: create an opposing force

Your ankle lock grip creates force **up and away**. You need an opposing force **down** on the opponent's hips (or later, the knee) to make that upward force actually break something instead of just repositioning the opponent.

### Classic Ashi Garami doesn't work for this
Standard Ashi Garami is criticized here — opponent can easily split the feet and step up on top of it. Not effective for hip immobilization.

## The golden rule of Ashi Garami for ankle locks

Two broad categories based on the direction the opponent's knee points:

| Opponent's knee points... | Primary controlling leg |
|---|---|
| **Outward** | Your **inside** leg |
| **Inward** | Your **outside** leg |

This single rule governs which leg configuration to use in almost every ankle lock scenario in the course.

## Ashi Garami control variations (knee pointing outward → inside leg dominant)

### Variation A — Butterfly Ashi Garami
1. Pass your foot underneath the training partner (butterfly-style).
2. Foot lands either **on the hip** or **over the hip**.
3. Lock the position: back-heel strongly with one foot, push forward with the other, so opponent's knee is pinched between your knees.
4. Result: opposing forces — up/away from the ankle lock, down/away from the Ashi Garami.
5. Can be reinforced by stacking your second foot on top of the first foot.

### Variation B — Foot as a wedge
1. Place one foot underneath as a wedge.
2. Second leg goes over the top; fall to the outside.
3. Cover the hips, then cover your own foot (so opponent can't strip your foot off the hip).

## Countering a knee that turns inward to escape

If the opponent turns his knee inward specifically to defend (this makes half-foot harder to achieve):
1. Pass the **outside leg** over the training partner's set (over their leg).
2. Lock up, create a strong back-heel over the opponent's leg.
3. As they continue to turn, the outside leg generates strong down-pressure, forcing them back into the hips.
4. Works regardless of whether the elbow lock is fully set.

## Drilled Sequence (knee pointing outward example)

1. Get initial grip; identify opponent's hips are free to move (test: try to break with hands alone — watch how easily they escape by raising hips).
2. Put right foot in the opponent's hamstring, throw left leg over the top.
3. Come down to a shoulder, squeeze knees together so opponent's knee is trapped between yours.
4. Apply breaking pressure.
5. **Second variation:** left foot on the hip, cover left foot with right foot, sandwich knees around opponent's knee, rise and apply pressure.
6. **When knee points the other way (inward):** throw the leg over the opposite direction — lock the triangle (with the legs) if desired, back-heel, and break.
7. Always aim to make the opponent's heel "disappear" (half-foot) — the more it disappears, the closer your elbow gets to your hip, and the stronger the lock.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Two crucial, independent elements are required: upper body mechanics (elbow to centerline) AND lower body mechanics (immobilize the hips). Neither alone is sufficient — better upper body technique just means the opponent relies more on hip movement to escape.
- **Golden rule:** knee points outward → inside leg controls. Knee points inward → outside leg controls.
- The mechanism is always the same idea: create a downward opposing force at the hip (or later, the knee) against the ankle lock's upward force.
- The more the opponent's heel "disappears" from view (half-foot), the closer your elbow gets to your hip and the stronger your lock.
- Classical Ashi Garami fails at this job — it doesn't constrain hip movement, which is why it's historically easy to escape.
