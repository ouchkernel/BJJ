# Opponent Performs An Inside Spin: Follow His Turn With Your Outside Leg

## Overview

Decision-tree material: what to do when a knowledgeable opponent responds to your ankle lock with an **inside spin**. Covers why the escape works, how it beats you if unanswered, and the fix — connecting with your outside leg to realign your hips with your opponent's and ride the spin into a finish.

---

## Why the ankle lock is strongest in the base position

The lock is most effective when the opponent's toes point up toward your rear deltoid — from there the transition to half-foot and finish is simple and fast.

## The opponent's escape: the inside spin

- The opponent's real goal isn't just turning — it's turning **in a way that lets him extract his foot**.
- Method: opponent puts his (near-side) hand on his own inside leg, inverts up onto his shoulders, and spins through — coming up to his knees.
- **Why it works:** as he inverts and spins, the foot becomes fully inverted, and the two athletes' hips end up facing **opposite directions** (his down, yours up). In that opposing-hip configuration it becomes very difficult to hold on — described as "super hard to hold on under those conditions," even with a strong grip. Simply limping the leg out also works if the grip is otherwise a "death grip."
- Note: don't stop the opponent's turn itself — the danger is the *hip misalignment* it creates, not the rotation per se. (If the opponent commits to the turn without properly extracting, you can also just grab the calf muscle, pull him in, and finish with a triangle over the legs.)

## The fix: outside leg connection

As soon as you see the inside turn starting (hips about to go opposing directions):

1. Take your **outside leg** and throw/pass it **over the opponent's leg**.
2. This creates just enough connection that, as the opponent continues to turn, you can **follow the turn and progressively realign your hips** to face the same direction as his.
3. The more he turns, the *more* your hips realign — and the stronger the lock becomes as alignment improves (this is a feature, not a problem — his own spin does the realigning for you).
4. Sometimes this takes one spin; sometimes the opponent's momentum carries you through two or three rotations before hips fully realign — this is normal, don't fight it.
5. Once hips are aligned (both facing down toward the floor), finish: belly-down is the preferred/most common finishing position, but flat-back, inside, and outside finishes all work too from this recovered position.
6. If you can't get a perfect half-foot position after realignment, just settle for belly-down — it's strong enough to finish from.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The inside spin escape works by inverting the opponent's foot and creating **opposing hip direction** between the two athletes — that configuration is what actually breaks your grip, not the spin itself.
- Counter: throw your **outside leg over** the opponent's leg as the turn starts, to form a connection point.
- Follow the opponent's spin — don't resist it — and let it progressively realign your hips with his. More turning = stronger lock, not weaker.
- May take multiple rotations to fully realign; that's expected.
- End state: belly-down finish (preferred), though flat-back/inside/outside finishes also work once hips are aligned.
