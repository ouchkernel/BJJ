# The Clamp

## Overview

Introduces the "clamp" — described as the single most important tool for entering triangles from flat-on-your-back (supine) positions. The clamp is essentially an **upper-body version of Ashi Garami**: the same mechanical principle (a knee in front, a knee behind) applied to the shoulder girdle instead of the hips.

## Why Supine Entries Need Their Own Solution

It's easier to generate movement from a seated position, which is why triangles are taught seated first. But at least half of bottom-position work happens flat on your back — you need an equally effective way to enter triangles from there.

## What The Clamp Is

- For any guard where you're on your back (inside or outside control, open or closed — doesn't matter), get onto your side and bring one knee **in front of** one of the opponent's shoulders, and the other knee **behind** the opposite shoulder.
- Result: the opponent's head and one arm are caught between your knees. If they push into you, the front knee stops them; if they pull away, the back knee stops them. **You now control the distance between your head and theirs.**
- Because you control that distance, you can easily create angle: touch a foot, look directly into the opponent's ear (a perpendicular angle), and it becomes simple to extract your bottom foot and go into the one-in-one-out position.

## The Ashi Garami Parallel

In a standard Ashi Garami, one knee sits in front of the opponent's hip (blocks them coming forward) and one knee sits behind their knee (blocks them going backward) — the space between their knee and your chest is governed entirely by your legs. The clamp applies the exact same in-front/behind, inside/outside structure to the **shoulder girdle** instead of the hip/knee. Distance is governed by knee position; angle is governed by turning across.

## Getting Into The Clamp

The entry motion is the same shrimping action used for a basic elbow escape — except instead of elbow-escaping inside the opponent's hips, you're escaping inside their **shoulders**, which is mechanically easier since shoulders are weaker than hips.

## Sequence

1. From flat on your back, get onto your side (even with the opponent in close).
2. Bring a knee/elbow-skate motion in front of one shoulder.
3. Combine a scissoring action — one knee pulls toward your chest, the other pushes forward (a scissor, not a squeeze; squeezing isn't the strongest available motion here).
4. Elbow goes over the opponent's elbow, so pulling their arm free becomes very difficult.
5. With distance/angle now under control, extract the bottom leg — you're in business for the triangle.

## Why It Matters So Much

Stated directly: to call yourself a master of triangles, you have to master the clamp. It's described as, without question, the single most effective way of governing head-to-head distance from the bottom position — and, among the instructor and his students, by far the most commonly used entry method into triangles from bottom position. Nothing else comes close.

## Key Takeaways

- The clamp is an Ashi-Garami-style knee wedge applied to the shoulder girdle — same mechanics, different body part.
- Getting in is just a shrimping/elbow-escape motion aimed at the shoulders instead of the hips — genuinely easier because shoulders are weaker.
- Once in the clamp, you control both distance and angle, which is what makes extracting the bottom leg into a triangle reliable rather than a fight.
