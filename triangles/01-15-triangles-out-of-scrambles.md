# Triangles Out Of Scrambles

## Overview

Extends triangle entries into scramble situations — moments where neither athlete has a controlling grip that lets them dictate the other's movement. Covers the most common scramble scenario in jiu-jitsu, the dangers to manage before attacking, and the actual mechanism for forcing an entry: get the opponent's hand to the floor, then convert that into a triangle.

---

## Defining a scramble

A scramble is any situation where neither athlete has a controlling grip that gives them more influence over the other's ability to move than the other has over theirs.

## The most common scramble scenario

- Both athletes on their knees, facing each other.
- One athlete has a tight waist grip; the other has an overhook (whizzer) — "his tight waist against my whizzer."
- This exact scenario recurs constantly in the sport.

## Dangers to manage before attacking

- **Opponent gets a body lock.** If the opponent locks their grip into your body from this position, you can be crunched backward and put down to your back — potentially very bad depending on ruleset (see below).
- **Opponent gets a front headlock.** If they turn in front of you and secure a front headlock, that's also dangerous.
- **Head position discipline.** Because of both dangers above, you can't let your head drop too far forward while working — head position has to be actively managed, not an afterthought, while you look for the scramble-triangle opportunity. If your head stays upright, the opponent's attempt to lock his hands for a body lock becomes much easier for *you* to exploit (see below).

## The core mechanism: force the hand to the floor

Most people don't know how to use their weight effectively here. The way to apply weight to the opponent is **through the hips**, not by pushing straight across into his body.

1. Put your hand on the mat.
2. Drive forward (never across into the opponent's body) — make a strong frame and fight into his body with a subtle shift toward a forward angle.
3. This turns and shifts the opponent's weight forward, forcing him to post his own hand on the mat to avoid being driven down and blinded.
4. Once his hand is down, keep walking him down onto his own hip, putting him under more and more pressure — this forces his hand to the floor very easily and rapidly.

**Training note:** deliberately drill this from a body-lock start (whizzer in, opponent's hands locked) and practice varying the *directionality* of your force until this becomes second nature.

## Converting the forced hand to a triangle

Once the opponent's hand is down on the mat:

1. Rotate your hips toward your own hand on the floor — never push straight across his body.
2. At the same time, throw your far leg across the training partner's body, with your hand driving into his armpit.
3. Hang your body weight off the opponent so your other leg becomes weightless.
4. That weightless leg goes easily into the triangle (Sankaku) lock.
5. **Don't let go too early** — releasing before the lock is secure lets the opponent pull the wrist back out and the entry fails.
6. Stay locked in and scoop inside the opponent's legs; as he postures up in the usual way, this results in a good, tight triangle lock.

An alternate finishing detail: take your knees off the mat and drive forward, going foot-to-foot, with your hand and leg crossing his body at the same moment — even if this puts you on your back, it doesn't matter, because you're already going into the submission.

## The back-exposure question

- The common warning "never step over your training partner's back in this situation" is **true only if your concern is top position** (e.g. wrestling, or rulesets that penalize your back touching the mat).
- If you're training for a sport (like standard jiu-jitsu) with **no penalty for being on your back**, this position can be used freely — being taken to your back mid-entry is not a problem if the triangle is already secured.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Scramble = neither athlete has a controlling grip advantage over the other's movement.
- The classic entry scenario: both on knees, tight waist grip vs. overhook/whizzer.
- Before committing to a triangle attempt out of a scramble, manage head position first — a body lock or a front headlock are the two live dangers that can turn the scramble against you.
- **Directionality of force is the key mechanical idea**: drive forward through the hips, never push straight across the opponent's body — this is what forces his hand to the floor.
- Once the hand is down: rotate hips toward that hand, throw the far leg across, hand to the armpit, hang your weight off him to make the other leg weightless, then finish into the triangle — don't release the position too early.
- Whether it's safe to go to your back mid-entry depends entirely on the ruleset — a real concern in top-position/wrestling contexts, a non-issue in standard no-penalty-for-back jiu-jitsu.
