# Side Triangle — Yoko Sankaku

## Overview

Introduces Category B of the triangle system (the front, rear, and opposite triangles make up Category A) with the side triangle, Yoko Sankaku. Covers why it's more prominent in judo than in jiu-jitsu, the core mechanics, and the single most important detail for making it actually work — a body-repositioning move called the "belly down shift."

## Why Side Triangle Matters More In Judo

- In BJJ, the front triangle is by far the most common triangle; in judo, the side triangle is actually the most common form. Reason: judo throwing actions expose the back much more often, and mat time is limited, so maneuvering into a side triangle from that back exposure is a natural fit.
- Side triangle also has a **dual function** in judo — it works as both a strangle and a pin (pins held 20-25 seconds end the match). BJJ has no pinning-win condition, so there's historically been less incentive to develop it — the instructor argues it deserves much more use in BJJ regardless.

## Basic Mechanics

1. Place your foot in the opponent's armpit, leg crossing their neck and chest — this creates the standard **one-in-one-out** situation (one arm outside your legs, one inside).
2. Divide your legs into strangle leg and support leg, same as any other triangle. Support leg goes underneath the opponent's neck and shoulders.
3. As you draw the opponent's arm across their body, get the **hollow of your knee** all the way around their **tricep** — not just the calf muscle. Calf-only contact caps you at a trap triangle; you'll never reach a full figure-four from there.
4. Draw your foot back so the heel touches the opponent's rear deltoid — this is what allows a full figure-four lock.
5. **Eliminate the shoulder**: take your thigh out underneath the opponent's head, turn your knee down to the floor so the shoulder comes out, and hook over the tricep, not the shoulder. This detail matters even more here than in other triangles because Yoko Sankaku covers more body area.
6. When locking the figure-four, pull everything back toward yourself with a back-heel action — don't extend your legs.

## Common Mechanical Failures

- If your leg doesn't penetrate all the way through so the hollow of the knee sits behind the tricep, you end up with a weak crossed-ankle grip — you'll never get past a trap triangle, and strangulation is off the table (other attacks may still be available).
- Pulling the opponent's elbow in doesn't fix this — the real variable is how **extended your strangle leg** is, not how close the opponent's elbow sits. Always extend the strangle leg first, then contract.
- Correct foot placement: the shoelaces of your foot belong in the hollow of your own knee (same rule as any good triangle). If the shoelaces sit on your calf muscle instead, you won't strangle anybody from here.

## The Critical Detail: The "Belly Down Shift"

- The single most common cause of failure: the support leg ends up under the opponent's **scapula** instead of under their head like a pillow. Leg-under-scapula is described as "condemning you to failure from the start" — the opponent can resist with their arms and no amount of squeezing produces a strangle.
- The fix: unlock the triangle, put your bottom foot on the mat, and **turn belly down**, pointing both knees at the mat. This correctly repositions your legs every time, so that when you shift back to a hip, the strangle leg's knee lands properly on the tricep.
- Once repositioned: one leg extends along the shoulder line, the other retracts — the two legs move in opposite directions to lock a genuinely tight strangle.
- This move is nicknamed the "belly down shift," and is described as necessary in the **vast majority of cases** — getting a perfect side-triangle position on the first attempt is rare.

## Reputation Vs. Reality

The side triangle has a reputation for being weaker than other triangles. The instructor calls this "absolute garbage" — done correctly (with the belly-down shift), it's as tight as any triangle in the system. The bad reputation comes from poor execution (leg under the scapula), not a flaw in the technique itself.

## Key Takeaways

- Side triangle (Category B) is judo's dominant triangle for a reason — more back exposure from throws, plus a dual strangle/pin function.
- The universal one-in-one-out rule and the "eliminate the shoulder" principle both apply here, same as every other triangle in the system.
- The **belly down shift** — bottom foot to the mat, both knees pointing down, then shift back — is the single biggest determinant of success. Expect to need it almost every time.
- A weak-feeling side triangle is a mechanics problem (leg under the scapula, not under the head), not a flaw in the move itself.
