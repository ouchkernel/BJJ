# Magic Of 2 On 1 Gripping

## Overview

Builds on the basic pronated elbow two-on-one from the previous chapter with three more 2-on-1 grip variations, each suited to a different way the opponent presents their arms. The underlying logic is always the same: locking down two of your limbs on one of theirs is a guaranteed local strength advantage regardless of overall size.

## Why two-on-one works

- For a front triangle you only need full control of one of the opponent's arms and their head — the other arm doesn't matter, it stays outside.
- A two-on-one locks two of your arms onto one of theirs — a built-in mechanical/strength advantage no matter how big or strong the opponent is.

## Variation: supinated two-on-one

- Secure the opponent's elbow first, draw it toward you, hold with no thumbs (hug it in rather than gripping).
- Their forward push is blocked by your own chest; the elbow can't move.
- Fast to apply and very effective — contrasted with the pronated grip (thumb grip) which is slower to set but also very strong once locked, since hiding the thumb-side weakness against your hip removes the opponent's one escape route.

## Variation: counter-grip off the opponent's own collar tie

- Common scenario: the opponent grabs a strong collar tie on you (a reasonable choice for them — it sets up a front headlock / guard pass).
- Counter: as they collar-tie you, lock their elbow with one hand and chop your second hand over their bicep, locking it in place.
- Net effect: the opponent came in looking to establish control and instead gets their own arm locked up — they can't even let go once you've secured it.
- From here, foot on the hip, unweighted leg, pop up, cross feet — straight into the standard trap-triangle sequence.

## Variation: two-on-one wrist grip (for high hands)

- Used specifically when the opponent keeps their hands high, typically because they're trying to dominate your shoulders.
- Grip doesn't look like much on its own, but one hand covers the structural weakness of the other, so the opponent can't yank free even though it looks loose.
- Same follow-up as always: foot to the hip, unweighted leg, draw them in, lock the trap triangle, knee points at the centerline/crotch as you turn the corner.

## Key Takeaways

- Two-on-one gripping isn't just a beginner-friendly teaching tool — it's the grip family most responsible for actual triangle finishes in live competition.
- Match the grip variation to how the opponent presents: pronated elbow grip (default), supinated elbow grip (fast, no-thumb hug), collar-tie counter-grip (punishes an aggressive opponent), two-on-one wrist (for opponents keeping their hands high).
- Every variation funnels into the same follow-up: foot on the hip → unweighted leg → pull in and lock the trap triangle → knee points at centerline as you turn the corner.
