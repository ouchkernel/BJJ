# Front Triangle Side Position 4

## Overview

A different method of controlling the opponent's arms from side position — instead of wedging the elbow above the shoulder line, this method pins the opponent's bicep to the floor with the shin ("shin pin"). It creates a genuine dilemma between an arm bar and a triangle.

---

## Setup — the shin pin

1. Sit your body through as the opponent pushes into your hips.
2. Take your head over the opponent's **far shoulder**.
3. Push down using the inside-wrist grip (thumb inside, wrist bent back 90° — referenced from earlier in the course).
4. Drive your body weight forward, bring your knee forward to take the inside position.
5. Your **shin** pins the opponent's bicep to the floor.

## Controlling both arms

With one arm shin-pinned, take the opponent's second hand, push it outward, and underhook it. Now both arms are controlled — one via the far-side underhook, one via the shin pin — creating one-in-one-out.

## Entry sequence

1. Drive across the opponent's body, knee into the armpit.
2. Fall across — the knee behind the opponent's shoulder blocks him in place.
3. Pull your foot back, pommel over the opponent's shoulder.

## The dilemma

- If the opponent stays in this position, he's given you **Udigatami** (straight arm lock) — described as effectively suicide for him.
- To escape the arm bar threat, the opponent has to commit forward and come up — but coming up drives him straight into the triangle.

## Finish

Clear the opponent's arm (either the method already shown, or a more old-fashioned elbow-across method both work). Establish angle and postural control, then lock the figure-four.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The shin pin is an alternative to the elbow-above-shoulder-line method for controlling an arm from side position — pin the bicep to the floor with your shin instead.
- Controlling both arms (underhook one side, shin pin the other) sets up a genuine dilemma: stay put and get arm-locked, or escape upward into the triangle.
- The opponent has no good option once both arms are controlled this way — that's the point of framing it as a "dilemma" rather than a single attack.
