# The Front Triangle

## Overview

Opening framing for the front triangle (omote sankaku) — the most common of the five triangles. Addresses the "my legs are too short" excuse directly, then introduces the first two mechanical rules: keep the strangle leg above the opponent's shoulder line, and eliminate the shoulder from the triangle entirely.

---

## The short-legs myth

- Body type does play a real role in jiu-jitsu — a 5'1" person against a 6'4" broad-shouldered opponent has real limits.
- But mechanics can compensate for a lot of that gap. Instructor describes himself as 5'11" with short legs for his height, a crippled leg, and a hip replacement — and still locks good triangles.

## Strangle leg vs. support leg

- **Strangle leg**: goes across the opponent's neck, does the actual choking (covers one carotid artery — the opponent's own shoulder covers the other side).
- **Support leg**: locks the strangle leg in place.
- Goal: bring the strangle leg across **perpendicular to the opponent's spine** — not aligned with it. The more your leg lines up with their spine, the less efficient the triangle; the more your calf crosses the shoulder line, the less leg length/dexterity you need.

## Rule 1: Stay above the shoulder line

- The "shoulder line" = the imaginary line joining the opponent's two shoulders.
- The strangle leg (specifically its lower portion) must stay **above** that line at all times.
- If the leg drops below the shoulder line before locking the figure-four, you only get half a triangle — you physically can't close it.
- Coaching cue, repeated constantly: **"stay above the shoulder line."**

## Rule 2: Eliminate the shoulder

- Goal: minimize the volume caught inside your legs — ideally just the head and one arm, never the shoulder.
- If the opponent's shoulder pushes forward and gets caught inside the triangle, you need much longer legs to finish — this is why "short legs" fail.
- Mechanism: form an angle so your knee sits **directly in front of the opponent's deltoid**. From the camera's point of view, the shoulder should visually disappear behind your knee/thigh.
- If you can still see the shoulder, the position is bad — reset.
- Demonstrated with two athletes: when the attacking leg goes around the corner and the knee blocks the shoulder, the shoulder is excluded from the lock and the figure-four closes easily; without that angle, the shoulder gets trapped inside and the triangle becomes very difficult to finish, especially for shorter legs.

## Why the triangle has to be a narrow lock

- A triangle is a strangle — it only works as a *tight* space. The actual gap of a properly locked triangle is only slightly wider than a forearm.
- A broad-shouldered opponent without a long-legged attacker makes a tight lock hard to achieve unless the shoulder is excluded first.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Two foundational mechanical rules, both about position, not strength or leg length: (1) strangle leg stays above the opponent's shoulder line, (2) the shoulder itself must be excluded from the triangle (knee blocks the deltoid so it disappears from view).
- Short legs are not a hard limiter — good angle and positioning let a short-legged athlete lock as tight a triangle as a long-legged one.
- These two rules recur through the rest of the course as the base layer every other detail builds on.
