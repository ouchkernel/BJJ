# Transition From Opposite Triangle To Joint Locks

## Overview

Step 4 of the triangle system applied specifically to hantai sankaku: what to do when the opposite-triangle strangle isn't working. The diagnosis comes down to one specific detail — knee position — and the fix is a clean transition into a kimura.

---

## Diagnosing why the strangle fails: knee position

- Enter as usual: secure a working grip, establish a trap triangle, turn around the opponent's body into hantai sankaku.
- **When it works:** your knee ends up underneath the opponent's armpit. With a wedge placed behind the opponent's head and the figure-four locked, the strangle is extremely tight.
- **When it doesn't work:** if your knee ends up *above* the armpit instead of underneath it, no amount of squeezing produces a strangle. You can hold the position all day and nothing happens — the position itself has zero strangle potential when the knee is high.
- This is presented as the single reason hantai sankaku strangles fail: knee position, not effort or squeeze strength.

## The fix when knee position is bad: kimura

If you recognize your knee is too high (locked up without the knee under the armpit), abandon the strangle attempt and transition directly to a kimura:

1. Push away slightly so your knees clear your own chest.
2. Secure the training partner's wrist.
3. Bring the opponent's elbow up to the line of their shoulder (your own elbow tucked to your own armpit accomplishes this).
4. Place the opponent's fist just inside your knee.
5. Result: a very tight kimura — the strangle failure becomes a joint-lock finish instead.

## Full example run-through

1. Secure a working grip (e.g., an elbow two-on-one).
2. Penetrate with a foot over the opponent's leg to establish the trap triangle — one arm inside the legs, one arm outside.
3. Turn in the same direction the opponent is facing — this rotation is driven largely by your outside leg.
4. **If the knee gets through underneath the opponent's armpit:** the strangle is on — lock the figure-four, and resistance from the opponent is nearly impossible to overcome.
5. **If the knee ends up on top of the shoulder instead of underneath it:** the strangle chance is essentially zero from there. Take the kimura instead — catch the wrist, bring the opponent's elbow to your own armpit (aligning it with the shoulder line), hand on the inside of the wrist, and finish the arm break.

## The four-step system, restated for hantai sankaku specifically

1. Secure a working grip.
2. Convert the grip into a trap triangle (one-in, one-out).
3. Manage the transition from trap triangle to full figure-four, and attempt the strangle. Good knee position wins right here.
4. If knee position is bad and the strangle isn't working, move to the joint lock. For hantai sankaku specifically, **kimura** is named as the best and most applicable option — described as a very simple transition from this position and "a devastating lock."

---

## Key Takeaways

- Hantai sankaku strangle success or failure comes down almost entirely to **knee position**: under the armpit = strangle works; above/outside the armpit = strangle is not available at all, regardless of squeeze.
- When knee position is bad, don't keep fighting for the strangle — transition immediately to a kimura (push knees off chest, catch the wrist, elbow to shoulder line, fist inside your knee).
- This is step 4 of the general triangle system: grip → trap triangle → manage transition to figure-four (win here if position is good) → joint lock if the strangle fails.
- For hantai sankaku specifically, kimura is the recommended joint-lock backup — simple to transition into and highly effective.
