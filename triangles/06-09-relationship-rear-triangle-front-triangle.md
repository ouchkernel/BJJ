# Relationship Between Rear Triangle And Front Triangle

## Overview

Introduces the system's category structure and shows how a stalled rear triangle converts directly into a front triangle — using the opponent's own defensive movement against them.

## The 5-Triangle System, Categories

- There are **5 triangles** total in the system, split into two categories:
  - **Category A** — 3 triangles (rear triangle is one of them).
  - **Category B** — 2 triangles.
- All triangles **within** Category A are linked to each other — once you have access to one, you effectively have access to the others in the same category.
- Crossing **between** Category A and Category B is hard, and when it happens it's usually because the opponent made a mistake, not because of anything the attacker specifically did.

## Converting A Stalled Rear Triangle Into A Front Triangle

Scenario: you're locked into Ushiro Sankaku but the strangle just isn't landing — maybe the opponent is pushing hard on your knee so you can't bring the support knee to the strangle knee, or they grabbed your foot before you could lock the triangle and pulled it down their body.

1. Move your head away from the opponent.
2. As they hold/frustrate one foot, take your other foot over the top, hold tight, and pass it over — this untangles the trapped foot.
3. The opponent now has to start turning **into** you. If they don't turn in, there's a direct arm lock available — so turning in is effectively their only option.
4. As they turn in, they're turning directly into a **front triangle** (Mote Sankaku) setup.
5. This is the moment to lock up a trap triangle: get a hold of the opponent's head, scoop inside their leg, find a good angle, lock the figure-four.

## Key Takeaways

- The 5-triangle system splits into Category A (3 triangles, including rear) and Category B (2 triangles) — triangles inside the same category back each other up.
- A stalled rear triangle isn't a failure state — moving your head away and clearing the trapped foot forces the opponent to turn into you, which delivers a front triangle setup.
- The opponent's own escape attempt (turning to avoid the arm lock threat) is what creates the front triangle's angle — you're not fighting for it, you're redirecting their movement into it.
