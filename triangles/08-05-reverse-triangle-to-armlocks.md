# Reverse Triangle To Armlocks

## Overview

Closes the volume with a recap of the four-step triangle system and its specific application to Gyaku Sankaku: when the strangle isn't landing (typically bad knee position), a strong American lock becomes available — and the opponent's own defense against the strangle knee is what sets it up.

## The Four-Step System (Full Recap)

1. **Working grip** — stabilize the opponent enough to move proactively into the triangle sequence.
2. **One-in-one-out** — trap one arm inside your legs, keep one outside. This creates the **trap triangle**: a control around the head and one arm, not yet a locked strangle.
3. **Trap triangle → figure-four** — the figure-four is what actually strangles. If it works, the match is over; the sequence stops here.
4. **Strangle fails → switch to a joint lock.** For Gyaku Sankaku, the go-to joint lock is typically an American lock.

## Setting Up The American Lock From Bottom Side Control

1. Starting scenario: opponent controls your legs from the inside while you're in bottom side control — an easy entry point into reverse triangle attempts.
2. Bring your knees to your chest, take a reverse cross-face, shift around the corner and begin locking the reverse triangle.
3. Push with both legs so the opponent's head moves away — but if your knee ends up **above** their ear here, the strangle is ineffective.

## Reading The Opponent's Specific Defense

- The opponent, aware that a specific knee is the "strangle knee," will often reach up with their hand and push that knee off.
- **This defensive action is exactly what opens the American lock.** Every time they push the knee, score their wrist, go over the top, and lock the American lock — connecting their elbow to your armpit.
- Because the opponent's head is trapped and can't move (a requirement for most American lock escapes), this version of the American lock is unusually tight. Normally, pressure relief in an American lock comes from head movement; here, that relief is unavailable.

## The Critical Detail: Elbow-To-Armpit Distance

- **Never leave the elbow away from your chest** — there's no strength in the lock from that position.
- Drag the opponent's elbow across your chest and connect it directly to your armpit first. The further their elbow sits from your armpit, the more play (and less effectiveness) in the lock.
- The opponent's only real counter is to thrust their arm straight forward, away from the lock — but doing so makes it *easier* for you to transition back into the original strangle attempt.

## The Dilemma: Playing Strangle Against American Lock

Once this dynamic is established, you play the opponent between the threat of the American lock and the threat of the strangle — each defense they mount against one opens the door to the other.

## Key Takeaways

- The four-step system's final step (strangle fails → joint lock) maps onto Gyaku Sankaku via the **American lock**, triggered specifically by the opponent defending their strangle-side knee.
- Because the opponent's head is trapped in this position, the American lock here doesn't suffer from the usual head-movement pressure relief — it's tighter than a standard American lock.
- Elbow-to-armpit connection is the single detail that makes or breaks the lock's strength — never let the elbow drift away from your chest.
- This creates a genuine dilemma dynamic: defending the American lock reopens the strangle, and defending the strangle (pushing the knee) opens the American lock.
