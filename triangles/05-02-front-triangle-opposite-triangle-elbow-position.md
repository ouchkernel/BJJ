# Front Triangle, Opposite Triangle And Elbow Position

## Overview

Explains *when* and *why* to reach for Hantai Sankaku instead of just sticking with the front triangle — centered on the opponent's elbow position, and on the problem of short legs. Also addresses the flexibility requirement, which turns out to be minimal.

## Why Not Just Use The Front Triangle?

Two main motivations to bring Hantai Sankaku into the game:
1. **Elbow position** — when the opponent defends a front triangle by using their elbow to block at the hip (bringing the shoulder forward into the triangle, as covered earlier), a straight front-triangle finish gets much harder.
2. **Short legs** — people with short legs often find front triangles difficult once the opponent's elbow comes forward.

## Reactive Use — Countering Elbow Defense

1. From a trap triangle, the opponent smartly uses their elbow to block at your hip, bringing the shoulder forward into the triangle (same defensive logic covered earlier in the course).
2. Instead of bulldozing through resistance, switch to Hantai Sankaku: control posture first (always), then gain angle — but the angle for the opposite triangle is the **literal opposite** direction from a normal front-triangle turn.
3. Get your body lined up almost north-south with the opponent — checked by touching your foot to the mat in that direction.
4. Take your foot and shin all the way up to the opponent's rear deltoid — this removes the shoulder from the equation entirely (only head and arm remain locked inside).
5. Get your knee underneath the opponent's armpit — this is what eliminates the shoulder from the triangle.
6. Lock the figure-four — an amazingly tight strangle results.

## Proactive Use — Going Directly To Hantai Senkaku

You don't have to wait for the opponent to defend with elbow position first. If you find yourself already on the "wrong side" to hit a conventional front triangle (hips positioned incorrectly for the standard angle), just go straight into Hantai Sankaku instead of repositioning for a front triangle.

**Rule of thumb**: for a conventional front triangle, if the opponent's left arm is trapped, your hips should go out to your right. If your hips end up on the "wrong" side instead, that's not a problem to fix — it's a direct invitation to just spin into Hantai Sankaku from where you already are.

## Flexibility Requirement

- Contrary to appearances, this does **not** require advanced flexibility (no full splits needed).
- All that's required: the ability to bring your knees to your chest and roll up onto your shoulders/toes toward the floor.
- Most people can do this with minimal training. The instructor notes doing it himself at 52 years old with a hip replacement.
- A large stomach can get in the way mechanically — unlike leg length, that's something you can control.

## Complementary Strengths

The strengths of Hantai Sankaku directly cover the weaknesses of the front triangle: elbow position problems and awkward hip angulation both make a front triangle harder, but both make an opposite triangle easier — and vice versa. Using the two together functions like a boxing one-two combination.

## Key Takeaways

- Use Hantai Sankaku reactively when the opponent's elbow defense brings their shoulder into a front triangle, or proactively whenever your hips are already on the "wrong" side for a conventional front-triangle angle.
- The flexibility bar is low: knees-to-chest and rolling onto the shoulders, not full splits.
- Front triangle and opposite triangle are complementary — what makes one hard tends to make the other easy.
