# Rotating Around Head And Shoulders With Triangles

## Overview

Direct, concrete demonstration of the multiplicity principle from earlier in the course: within category A (front/rear/opposite-side), all three triangles are really the same lock — two of your legs wrapped around the opponent's head and shoulder — just applied from a different angle. Moving between them is a matter of rotating your body around the opponent's head/shoulder, not resetting the position.

---

## The core idea

- "A triangle is always the same thing — it's my two legs wrapped around your head and shoulder. That's where the stranglehold comes from." Category A's three triangles (front, rear, opposite-side) are locked together by their very nature: whenever you have one, just by moving your body around the opponent's body, you'll always have access to the other two.
- Demonstrated concretely from category A: starting in a basic front triangle (Amote Senkaku) on a partner who's on two knees, the instructor rotates around the partner's head and shoulder to move effortlessly from one triangle to another within the category — without releasing the leg lock.

## Why this matters practically

- This turns the abstract multiplicity principle into a physical skill: rotation around the head/shoulder axis is the actual mechanism that lets you "use all five triangles as a team," specifically within a category.
- If a given angle on a triangle isn't producing the finish, the fix isn't necessarily a different technique — it can be rotating around the same head/shoulder connection into a different triangle in the same category.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Front, rear, and opposite-side triangles are mechanically the same core lock (legs around head + shoulder) — the differences are angle/orientation, not a different technique from scratch.
- You move between them by rotating your body around the opponent's head and shoulder, without releasing the leg position.
- This is the practical, drillable version of the multiplicity principle covered earlier in the course.
