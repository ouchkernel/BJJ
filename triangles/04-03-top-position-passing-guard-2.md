# Front Triangles Top Position Passing Guard 2

## Overview

A second entry into the triangle while passing a seated opponent's guard from standing. It looks similar to the first (Top Position Passing Guard) but differs in angle — more front-on rather than diagonal — and in grip: a collar tie plus a wrist, instead of an over-tie.

---

## Grip and misdirection

Take a collar tie and grip one wrist (say, the opponent's left arm). This draws the opponent's attention and defensive focus onto the gripped arm. But that arm is **not** the target — the plan is to triangle the *other*, ungripped arm. You need one arm trapped inside your legs and one kept outside (one-in-one-out), and the gripped arm is the one being deliberately pushed outside.

## Entry mechanics

1. Secure collar tie + top or inside wrist (either works).
2. Push the gripped wrist down.
3. Jump more front-on into the opponent (not diagonal, unlike the previous entry) — step your outside foot, then go straight over the top like jumping guard.
4. Cross your ankles — that's the trap triangle.
5. Transition trap triangle → figure-four: lift the trapped hand off the outside, punch through, control the head. As the opponent postures up and away, turn the corner, wedge around the head and shoulders, lock the figure-four.

## Mental image

Don't think about "locking a triangle" during the jump. Think of it like jumping guard on a seated opponent — except you're locking a guard around the **head and one arm**, instead of around the waist.

## Setup detail — the pullback reaction

Opponents instinctively try to pull their gripped elbow back to their own hip when it's controlled. Use that: pull the gripped arm toward yourself, let the opponent supply the pullback reaction, then step up and jump — this sets up the exact entry above.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Grip is collar tie + one wrist — this is a deliberate decoy: the gripped arm draws defensive attention while the *other* arm becomes the triangled arm.
- Jump angle is front-on here, unlike the diagonal jump of the previous entry — two distinct, both effective, variations of the same top-position jumping triangle.
- Mental cue: jump like you're locking a guard around the head and an arm, not like you're locking a triangle directly.
- The opponent's own pullback reaction (trying to retract the gripped elbow) can be used to trigger the timing of the jump.
