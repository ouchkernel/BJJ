# Overview

## Overview

Framing chapter for the whole Triangles: Enter The System series. Lays out why the triangle matters, the four-step system used throughout the course, the five triangle variations and how they're categorized, and the three central problems every triangle attempt has to solve.

---

## Why the triangle matters

- Legal and effective in gi, no-gi, and MMA — versatile across rule sets and weight classes.
- Strangles are generally favored over joint locks: a tough opponent can fight through joint damage, but nobody fights through unconsciousness.
- Triangles demand unusually high leg dexterity/coordination compared to most submissions — that skill development carries over broadly, since jiu-jitsu is fundamentally a sport of using your legs to fight an opponent's upper body. A triangle is the purest expression of that idea: your legs and hips against one of the opponent's arms plus their head and shoulders — a favorable strength matchup that lets smaller athletes beat bigger ones.

## The four-step triangle system

1. **Create the one-in-one-out scenario** — one of the opponent's arms trapped inside your legs, the other arm kept outside your legs. This precursor is common to every triangle, regardless of type. Built mainly through grip and kuzushi (off-balancing).
2. **Shoot into a trap triangle** — ankles crossed behind the opponent's back, head and one arm controlled. This is the control phase, not yet a strangle.
3. **Manage the transition to a figure-four triangle** — the actual strangulation phase. Triangles are almost never born fully locked; they start as a trap triangle and get adjusted into figure-four under resistance.
4. **Fall back to joint locks if the strangle doesn't come** — either upper body (juji gatame, kimura variations) or, if the opponent postures/stands to escape, transition into Ashi Garami and attack the legs.

## The five triangles, in two categories

| Category | Triangles | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Front (Amote Senkaku) → Rear (Ushiro Senkaku) → Opposite-side (Hantai Senkaku) | Front is the "king" — most finishes of any triangle. Within category A you can rotate from one to another around the opponent's head/shoulder without releasing. |
| **B** | Side (Yoko Senkaku) → Reverse (Gyaku Senkaku) | Rarer in jiu-jitsu (more common in judo), but valuable additions. Same rule: within category B you can move between the two. |

You cannot jump directly from a category A triangle to a category B triangle — the leg configuration doesn't allow it. But within a category, losing one triangle doesn't mean losing the position; you likely still have the others.

## The three central problems

1. **Circumference problem** — how do you fit the opponent's head, shoulder, and arm inside the length of your legs, especially against a big/powerfully-built opponent? Solved by the **shoulder elimination principle**: aim to keep only the neck and arm inside the triangle, not the shoulder.
2. **Posture problem** — how do you stop the opponent posturing up and out of a trap triangle before you reach figure-four? Three named counters used throughout the course: the knee pull method, the head pull method, and the shin pull method.
3. **Stacking problem** — how do you stop the opponent stacking (or lifting) you while you're mid-transition to figure-four? Solved with a scoop grip inside the opponent's leg, used to work to an advantageous angle off to the side — this also defeats lifting attempts.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Triangles are strangles first — favor them over joint locks, but the system lets you attempt both simultaneously and fall back to joint locks if the strangle isn't there.
- Every triangle starts the same way: one-in-one-out → trap triangle → figure-four → (joint lock if needed).
- There are five triangles split into two non-interchangeable categories (A: front/rear/opposite, B: side/reverse) — mastering only the front triangle leaves a lot on the table.
- The three problems to solve on every attempt: circumference (shoulder elimination), posture (knee/head/shin pull), and stacking (scoop grip to an angle).
