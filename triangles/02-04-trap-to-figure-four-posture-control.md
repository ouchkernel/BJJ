# Managing Transition From Trap Triangle To Figure Four Triangle

## Overview

First of two lessons on the highest-risk moment in triangle attacks: the transition from an initial trap triangle (ankles crossed, not yet a figure-four) to the finished figure-four lock. This lesson focuses on controlling the opponent's posture during that transition — the single biggest defensive reaction you'll face.

---

## Why this transition is the danger zone

- If you lose a triangle, ~9 times out of 10 it happens during the trap-to-figure-four transition, not before or after.
- The moment a trap triangle lands on a knowledgeable opponent, their default reaction is a series of postural defenses aimed at breaking it before you can finish.
- Most common first reaction: hands on the mat, pushing the head up, trying to pop your feet apart, pushing pressure toward the ceiling.

## Two grip options to control posture

### 1. Single collar-tie-style grip on the head (preferred)
- One hand controls the back of the opponent's head; the other becomes a scoop grip that goes inside the opponent's leg.
- Avoid grabbing with two hands too early — that locks you directly in front of the opponent, which lets them stack you and prevents you from turning to angle.
- With one hand on the head: when the opponent tries to posture, this grip makes it exceptionally difficult, and frees you to turn your body and get the angle needed to lock a tight triangle.

### 2. Hold your own shin
- Forms a locked, reinforced wedge around both sides of the opponent's head.
- When the opponent tries to yank out, this is also very difficult to escape, and it lets you use the same turning action to get the lock directly behind the head.

Both options achieve the same goal: prevent the head from moving away while you turn to angle. Either is acceptable — pick based on what's available in the moment.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The trap-to-figure-four transition is where most triangles are lost — treat it as its own skill, not an afterthought.
- Posture (head coming up/away) is the opponent's default first-line defense the instant a trap triangle lands.
- Don't commit two hands early — it traps you in front of the opponent and invites stacking.
- Two viable ways to control the head during the transition: single-hand grip on the back of the head (scoop hand goes inside the leg), or holding your own shin to form a reinforced wedge around the head. Both prevent posture and enable the turn to angle.
