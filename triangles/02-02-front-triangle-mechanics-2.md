# Front Triangle Mechanics 2 — Eliminating The Shoulder

## Overview

The single most important mechanical element of the front triangle, building directly on the shoulder-line rule from the previous chapter: physically taking the opponent's shoulder **out of the triangle** entirely.

## The concept

- Goal: minimize the volume your legs wrap around — ideally just the head and one arm, never the shoulder.
- If the opponent's shoulder drives forward and gets included inside your leg lock, you're forced to resort to pulling down on the head and other compensations.
- **Fix:** form an angle so your knee sits directly in front of the opponent's deltoid. Done correctly, the shoulder becomes physically invisible from the camera's (or your own) point of view.
- If you can still see the opponent's shoulder inside your legs, the triangle is poor — full stop.

## Why it works

- The gap of a locked triangle is narrow by necessity (barely wider than a forearm) — that's what makes it strangle. A narrow gap can't accommodate the extra mass of a shoulder without requiring much longer legs.
- Angling your knee in front of the shoulder removes that mass from the equation, letting even short legs lock a tight figure-four.

## Drilling notes

- Demonstrated with two athletes: without angle, the shoulder sits visibly inside the locked triangle — a poor, easily-defended position.
- With the leg brought around the corner so the knee/thigh sits in front of the shoulder, the shoulder is excluded and the resulting figure-four and strangle are immediately tighter.

## Key Takeaways

- Two governing mechanical rules so far: (1) strangle leg stays above the shoulder line, (2) the shoulder itself must be eliminated from inside the triangle.
- Test: if you can see your opponent's shoulder from outside the lock, the triangle isn't tight enough yet.
- Both rules compound — get both right and success rate goes up immediately, even for short-legged athletes.
