# Opposite Side Triangle — Hantai Sankaku

## Overview

Introduces Hantai Sankaku, the "opposite sided" triangle — a triangle locked in what looks like the wrong direction. Famously used by the instructor's students; most notably, Gordon Ryan finished Felipe Pena (referred to here as "Harlick Gracie" in the ASR) with this exact move. Despite looking mechanically broken, done with correct body alignment it produces an extremely tight strangle.

## What It Looks Like

- The familiar front triangle (Mote Sankaku) has the legs locking in the "normal" direction.
- Hantai Sankaku locks the legs in what appears to be the **wrong** direction — a "wrong sided triangle."
- It looks unworkable at first glance — the alignment appears broken, and it looks more like a setup for a kimura or armlock than a strangle.
- The actual problem when it looks broken is **alignment** — poor body alignment means the training partner feels no threat. Corrected alignment produces an enormously tight strangle.

## Basic Mechanics — Getting Into Position

1. Start from a classic **trap triangle** (head + one arm trapped, no figure-four yet).
2. Common opponent defense: hand behind your hip, which brings their shoulder forward into the triangle — a sound defense, since a shoulder included in the triangle blocks the figure-four close (established earlier in the course).
3. Counter: get a hold of the opponent's head, reach your hand deep inside your own legs, and use it to pull your body to a **90° angle** across the opponent. Your arm only gets you to 90° — no further. Don't try to pull further with the arm.
4. What completes the alignment is **not** the arm — it's the **opposite-side leg acting as a pendulum**. Swing that leg so your knee goes underneath the opponent's ribcage/armpit as your foot reaches toward the floor.
5. Bring your knee in behind the opponent's head as a wedge — this makes pulling out very difficult.
6. Grab your own shin, pull the legs into a figure-four so the lock sits directly above the opponent's head.
7. Result: a strangle that becomes very hard to escape once fully locked.

## The Core Principle: Arm Initiates, Leg Finishes

- Keep your legs relaxed as you pull yourself around.
- The arm initiates the turn and gets you to 90° — it does **not** complete it.
- The **pendulum action of the opposite leg** completes the turn, taking you all the way to a north-south position relative to the opponent.
- Success/failure of the whole move comes down to reaching that north-south alignment. The arm gets you halfway; the leg finishes the journey.

## The Vertical-Shin Detail

- Your shin/foot must point **straight up to the ceiling**. If the foot points off to the side instead, you cannot get an effective strangle from here — you might land a kimura or other joint lock, but not a strangle.
- The knee has to transition from perpendicular to almost north-south relative to the opponent; control of the opponent's head is what allows you to make that turn around the corner.

## The Strangle-Leg / Support-Leg Reversal

- In a basic front triangle, it's obvious which leg is the strangle leg (the one on the carotid artery) and which is the support leg.
- In Hantai Sankaku, this is much less clear — **neither leg makes direct contact with the carotid**. Instead, it's the **thigh** that contacts the carotid, and the leg roles effectively swap compared to a normal triangle: what would normally be the support leg becomes the strangle leg.

## Two Requirements To Remember

1. Full north-south alignment — turn your whole body to face the same direction as the opponent.
2. Vertical shin — feet pointing up to the ceiling, not off to the side.

## Key Takeaways

- Hantai Sankaku looks broken because it usually IS mis-aligned when done wrong — the fix is always alignment, not extra squeeze.
- The arm gets you to 90°; the leg (pendulum swing) finishes the turn to north-south. Don't try to force the last half with your arm.
- Vertical shin/foot pointing to the ceiling is the visual check for whether the strangle is even possible from this position.
- Unlike a normal triangle, the strangle-leg and support-leg roles reverse — the thigh contacts the carotid, not a shin.
