# Front Triangle Side Position

## Overview

Shifts the whole chapter's emphasis from bottom-position front triangles (still the most common in competition) to top-position triangles, starting with side control. This lesson covers the core setup problem for every top-side triangle: getting one of the opponent's arms trapped, specifically the **near arm**, which is the harder of the two to isolate.

---

## The setup problem

Pinning an opponent cross-side gives him a near-arm frame and a far-arm frame. To enter a triangle you need a **one-in-one-out** situation — one arm trapped inside your legs, one kept outside. You can choose to trap either arm; this lesson focuses on the near arm, since it starts in a strong defensive position (framed against your hips).

## Method — hip-drag near-arm trap

1. Switch your control from the usual cross-face grip to an **over-grip** on the opponent.
2. Walk down toward the opponent's hips ("crowd the hips").
3. Once there, put your buttocks and lower back against the opponent's trapped elbow.
4. Walk backward, using the power of your lower back (not arm strength) to shift the elbow **above the opponent's shoulder line** (the line joining his two shoulders). Opponents want their elbow tucked in close to the ribs — you want the opposite: elbow extended out, above the shoulder line, in a vulnerable position.
5. Return to your knees carefully, without losing the trapped elbow: post a hand, hip-heist, brush your knee up into place.

Result: the opponent's elbow ends up trapped between your own elbow, his own head, and your hip — very hard for him to retract to safety from there.

## Finishing the entry

1. From the trapped-elbow position, rise off chest-to-chest (the opponent is in a strong defensive frame there).
2. Catch the opponent's wrist, push it inward.
3. Take your hand behind the opponent's head.
4. Rise in level, and — critically — **face forward**, not across the opponent's body. Don't put your head over his chest; that makes it very hard to free your leg.
5. Bring your body forward past the opponent's head, chin over your own knee.
6. Whip your leg around the corner, hook it underneath the opponent's neck.
7. Bring your legs forward, head to the mat, lock the figure-four.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Top-side triangles require the same one-in-one-out setup as bottom triangles — the challenge is isolating an arm that starts in a strong defensive frame.
- The near-arm trap uses your lower back and hips, not your arms, to walk the opponent's elbow above his shoulder line.
- Facing forward (not across the opponent's chest) as you rise in level is the detail that makes the leg easy to free — putting your head over his chest makes the leg feel impossible to pick up.
- This near-arm isolation method is described as very effective for entering triangles from top side position, and it recurs as the base grip for several later variations in this chapter.
