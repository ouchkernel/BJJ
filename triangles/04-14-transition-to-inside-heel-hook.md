# Transition To Inside Heel Hook

## Overview

Closes out the volume with a second major theme (after all the triangle-entry variations): the **upper body / lower body attack alternation** principle. Rather than a triangle finish, this chapter shows how a triangle attempt that gets defended naturally flows into a leg attack — specifically an inside heel hook via X-guard/reverse X-guard.

---

## The upper body / lower body principle

- Whenever you work from guard, there's a basic choice: attack upper body or lower body.
- If the opponent is down on two knees, you generally can't attack the legs yet — something has to get his knees off the floor first (him standing, or you elevating him).
- **General rule:** opponent down on you → attack the upper body. Opponent posts/postures up and away (the standard defense to upper-body attacks, ~90% of the time) → the lower body opens up.
- This produces a repeating pattern: attacks alternate ends of the body as the opponent defends — start upper body, he defends by rising/moving away, you shift to lower body (or vice versa if he starts elevated).
- Conceptually simple; executing it live against a resisting opponent is the actual skill.

## Applied sequence

1. Push one of the opponent's hands to the centerline, take an elbow two-on-one grip.
2. As he yanks away: bring your body in close, elbows to your side, his hand close to your hip.
3. Regulate distance with your foot on the hip (same side as the trapped arm) — foot regulates when he pushes in, the upper-body grip regulates when he pulls away.
4. This produces an **unweighted leg** that's very hard for the opponent to track or grab — use it to attack an initial triangle.
5. Standard opponent response: posture up and away to break the triangle grip.
6. Counter the posture: pull with the knees, add a hand behind the head to remove his last postural option (he now has to physically stand to escape).
7. As he stands and starts thumb-posting on your knee, etc.: bring your **outside leg** through first, making contact behind his knee. (Using the outside leg first is what keeps you aligned with him if he tries to throw your legs by and pass — using the inside leg first here leaves no contact and lets him pass.)
8. As you feel the triangle slipping, bring your foot to your own buttocks to minimize the distance you need to cover, then shift straight into **X-guard**.
9. In X-guard: drive his hands to the mat (knee, bum, take his foot off the floor).
10. Switch from X-guard to **reverse X-guard** — bring him up into the air, lock the figure-four around his legs.
11. Finish: from reverse X-guard, lift his hips over your head, lock the figure-four, control his hands, and finish with an **inside heel hook**.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Core principle: attacks alternate between upper and lower body as the opponent defends one end and exposes the other — this is one of the recurring organizing ideas of the whole system, not specific to triangles.
- The unweighted leg created by proper distance management (foot-on-hip + upper-body grip) is what makes the initial triangle threat hard for the opponent to defend cleanly.
- When the triangle is being lost to a standing opponent, **always bring the outside leg through first** — it's what keeps you connected if he tries to throw your legs by.
- A failed/contested triangle against a standing opponent naturally converts into X-guard → reverse X-guard → **inside heel hook** — the volume's parting message is that the front triangle and the leg-lock game are directly connected, not separate systems.
