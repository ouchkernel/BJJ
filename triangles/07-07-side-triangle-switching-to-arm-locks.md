# Side Triangle Switching To Arm Locks

## Overview

Closes the volume with the 4-step system recap, then demonstrates that Yoko Sankaku is especially well set up for joint-lock backups — offering kimuras on both arms plus wrist locks, more options than most of the other triangles.

## The 4-Step System (Recap)

1. Useful grip on the opponent.
2. One-in-one-out — one arm trapped inside your legs, one outside.
3. Trap triangle → figure-four transition (the actual strangle). Land it, and the match is over — no need for step 4.
4. If the strangle proves insufficient, switch to a joint lock — for the side triangle, this menu is unusually rich.

## Option 1 — Kimura, Far Arm

1. Drag the opponent's far arm in, take their wrist.
2. As you lock your legs, push the wrist out and away — a one-handed kimura.
3. Take their wrist down toward your knee, push across, pull your legs back — strong kimura attack.
4. Make it stronger: hold the wrist with one hand and the elbow with the other, pulling the elbow while pushing the wrist, for a significantly stronger joint lock.

## Option 2 — Kimura, Near Arm

If the far-arm kimura isn't landing:
1. Lock the near-arm kimura, pop up to your base, put their hand in behind their back.
2. Sit up into the power line, legs separate.
3. Bring everything up to the line of the opponent's shoulder, then pull for the finish — the shoulder-line detail is what actually produces the break.

## Falling Back To The Strangle

If the opponent locks their hands to defend both kimura attempts, you can always return to Yoko Sankaku itself: ear to the opponent's hip, forward, kick through, relock the strangle. The kimura attempts and the strangle threaten each other — you can play between them.

## Option 3 — Wrist Lock

1. Back-heel with the legs, push the opponent's wrist — already a strong attack.
2. Make it stronger with a two-on-one: push with one hand, pull with the other.
3. Alternative: lock the arm in tight, shorten at the elbow, cup over the opponent's knuckles, lock everything in close.
4. Key detail: jam their tricep against your bicep, then close hand-over-hand — twisting out becomes very difficult, producing a nasty wrist-lock finish.

## Cycling Between Near-Arm Kimura, Far-Arm Kimura, Wrist Lock, And Strangle

The chapter emphasizes that all of these live in the same position — you don't need to abandon Yoko Sankaku to find a finish, you cycle through its attached joint-lock menu until one lands.

## Key Takeaways

- The 4-step system closes the loop one final time: grip → one-in-one-out → trap-to-figure-four → joint lock fallback.
- Yoko Sankaku offers an unusually deep joint-lock menu from one position: kimura (far arm, one- or two-handed), kimura (near arm, via the power line), and a wrist lock (tricep-to-bicep jam).
- These options threaten each other — defending one often exposes another, including the strangle itself, which stays available throughout.
