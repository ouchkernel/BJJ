# Front Triangles Top Position Passing Guard

## Overview

Most front triangles happen from bottom position, but effective top-position triangles exist too — including while you're actively passing guard. This chapter covers the first entry: attacking a seated opponent from standing, via an over-tie, while he's grip fighting to stop the pass.

---

## Why this works

Seated guard vs. a standing opponent produces heavy grip fighting and reaching on both sides — which means extended arms. That extension is what gets exploited. Throwing a submission attempt into a guard pass also splits the opponent's attention: he now has to worry about being submitted, not just about stopping the pass.

## Entry mechanics

1. Engage in normal hand-fighting/posting.
2. When you see a posted/extended hand, go over the top and take an **over-tie**.
3. Prefer **outside foot position** here — this is an exception to the usual preference for inside position, and it works especially well against opponents who like to play inside position themselves.
4. Step your foot to the outside, over-tie, and in one motion take your leg directly over the opponent's arm.
5. Lock up and land on an elbow (not flat) — this keeps you mobile: if the opponent stays put, pass through to top position; if he rolls up on top of you, you still have the triangle.

## Body angle detail

Don't crash straight forward into the opponent — jump/fall across at a diagonal angle onto one side. Example: left-hand over-tie + left-foot outside step penetration → the right leg becomes the strangling leg, and you fall onto your right side.

## Setup variant — reading the inside-position player

If the opponent has both feet on the inside (a common defensive habit), step your own foot to the outside and over-tie directly into the same entry.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Extended/posted arms during a seated-guard-vs-standing hand fight are the trigger for this entry.
- Outside foot position (unusual for this instructor's general preference) is correct here, especially against inside-position players.
- Fall across the opponent at a diagonal, not straight forward — this keeps you landed on an elbow with options either way.
- Landing on the elbow (not flat) preserves both a top-position pass and the triangle depending on how the opponent reacts.
