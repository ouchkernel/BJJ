# Over Hook Grip To Triangle

## Overview

Introduces a broader principle first — that mastering the front triangle should begin from a **seated** position, since it's easier to generate movement (both your own and your opponent's) from seated than from supine, which is how beginners are traditionally taught. Then covers the overhook as one of the best grips for this — it holds the opponent close (defeating posture-based defense) but at the cost of foot-entry room, solved here with a deliberate sweep-and-recover setup.

## Why Seated First

Learning triangles from a seated position first, then progressing to supine, is recommended because it's much easier to move both yourself and your opponent into the one-in-one-out position from seated than from flat on your back — the traditional beginner teaching context.

## The Overhook Tradeoff

- Overhooking keeps the opponent close, which strongly counters their instinct to posture up and away — the single most common defense against triangles.
- The cost: with the opponent held that close, it's not always easy to throw your foot over into the one-in-one-out position.

## The Sweep-And-Recover Trick

1. Draw the opponent forward into a collar tie, then lock the overhook (thumb close to your own pectoral muscle).
2. Secure their wrist with your second hand, push it inward (elbow close to your hip), grab your own wrist in a figure-four.
3. Tilt the opponent and hit a **Sumi Gaeshi** (hook sweep) to put them onto their back.
4. If they stay down, you win top position outright. But almost no one accepts being swept — the overwhelming majority will fight to get back up to their knees.
5. Step over their body and **let them recover** to their knees — your grip (the same figure-four, now underneath their chest) never changes.
6. As they climb back to reclaim top position, they climb straight into the beginnings of a triangle.
7. Cover the head (always the first thing to control), get a scoop grip, turn the corner with both feet facing forward.
8. Lock the figure-four, knee pointing toward the opponent's groin, directly behind their head — immediate strangle.

## Butterfly Guard Variant

Same shape from butterfly guard: hand-fight, draw forward, overhook, secure wrist-to-wrist grip, shoulder down, sweep and stand over the opponent. As their head rises on the way back up, throw the leg under, keep the wrist grip to throw over the top and secure the head, then turn the corner into the winning position.

## Key Takeaways

- The overhook's strength (holding the opponent close) creates its own weakness (hard to get a foot over) — the fix is using the opponent's own instinct to recover from a sweep as the delivery mechanism for the triangle.
- Almost nobody accepts being swept to their back — that near-universal reaction is the reliable trigger this entry depends on.
- The grip never changes through the sweep-and-recovery cycle; only your foot/leg positioning changes as they climb back up.
