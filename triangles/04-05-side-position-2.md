# Front Triangle Side Position 2

## Overview

Reviews the near-arm-trap entry from the previous lesson through the lens of the **foot penetration vs. knee penetration** framework introduced earlier in the course, then teaches the knee-penetration alternative in full.

---

## Recap as foot penetration

With the opponent's arm trapped against your hip and his hand pushed toward his sternum: rise/level-change upward, bring your head over your own knee, and this creates a **weighted leg and an unweighted leg**. The unweighted leg steps over the top and locks around the opponent's neck into the triangle.

**Balance detail:** the head goes over the knee that's bearing weight — not between your knees. That's specifically what makes the other knee light and easy to pick up.

**Disadvantage:** this method opens a window of space while you're rising, so it's best suited to fast athletes who can close the gap before the opponent recovers — described as a "fast man's move." Committing fully and immediately once you start is important; hesitating in the exposed position lets the opponent pull the elbow free.

## Alternative — knee penetration

Slower, but tighter and gives the opponent far less space to escape through.

1. Same starting trap (near arm against your hip, defensive position cleared using hips/buttocks).
2. Different grip this time: shoot your hand through to the opponent's **bicep** (not holding the wrist as in the foot-penetration version).
3. Bring your head **down and across** — not up — fist to the mat, clearing the opponent's fist out of the way as you go.
4. Bring your knee up and penetrate with the **knee**, not the foot.
5. Turn around the corner, scoot — the knee penetrates through the shoulder easily.
6. Windshield-wipe the legs into the trap triangle.
7. Control the head, turn the corner, lock the figure-four.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Foot penetration and knee penetration are two ways to convert the same near-arm trap into a one-in-one-out triangle entry — the choice trades speed for tightness.
- Foot penetration is faster but leaves a window of exposed space; it favors fast, committed athletes who don't hesitate mid-entry.
- Knee penetration is slower but gives up much less space, and uses a different grip (bicep control, not wrist) and a different head direction (down/across instead of up).
- Balance rule for foot penetration: head goes over the *weighted* knee, not between the knees — that's what frees the other leg.
