# Sweeping And Off-Balancing With Opposite Triangle

## Overview

The opposite triangle isn't just a strangle in isolation — it's a hub for combination attacks. This chapter covers using hantai sankaku to sweep and off-balance the opponent, and how those sweeps lead into either the front triangle or the rear triangle (ushiro sankaku) depending on how the opponent reacts. It closes with a recap of the overall four-step triangle system, setting up the joint-lock chapter that follows.

---

## The big picture: category A triangles work as a set

Category A = front triangle, opposite (hantai) triangle, and rear (ushiro) triangle. Whenever you have access to one of these three, you generally have access to the other two just by changing your angle. Playing all three against each other creates a scrambling nightmare for the opponent — they can hold off one or two triangle threats, but rarely all three in sequence. The instructor frames this as the core reason to develop all three, not just the front triangle most people already know.

## From hantai sankaku, sweeping is almost always available

Once hantai sankaku is locked, you generally have strong sweeping/off-balancing potential built in — and from there you can shift around the opponent's body into ushiro sankaku (rear triangle).

## Combo 1 — sweep forward, opponent recovers into front triangle

1. From a trap triangle, turn into hantai sankaku and lock the figure-four (opponent's head pinched inside the triangle).
2. Take your body and drive **forward** (not to the side).
3. Opponent trying to brace on their hands and lean backward makes the knockover almost impossible to stop.
4. As the opponent recovers from the sweep, they frequently come back up directly into the position for the **original front triangle**.
5. Finish there: slide inside the leg, turn the corner to eliminate the shoulder, and finish the strangle as usual.

## Combo 2 — sweep to the side, opponent stays down into rear triangle

1. From hantai sankaku, sweep the opponent down onto their back.
2. If the opponent is smart enough to avoid turning back in (turning in would hand you the front triangle), use one hand as a base on the floor.
3. Reach your other hand underneath the opponent's trapped arm. (A kimura is available here if you want it, but it isn't required for this pathway.)
4. Plant your foot and shift your hips to rotate around the opponent's body.
5. Gather the opponent's fist/knuckles onto your own chest.
6. This converts the position into **ushiro sankaku** (rear triangle) — close your knees over each other for the strangle finish.

## Variant entry into rear triangle

A near-identical sequence is shown again: sweep the opponent down, use hands on the floor to shift and secure a grip, plant the foot, change the angle, and lock ushiro sankaku directly. The point emphasized both times: whichever way the opponent reacts — turning back in (front triangle) or staying flat on the mat (rear triangle) — you have a finish available.

## The core skill underneath all of this

Developing the ability to move your legs around the opponent's head and shoulders freely, so you can switch fluidly between front, opposite, and rear triangle attacks. This combination is described as extremely difficult to defend — an opponent can stall off one or two triangle threats, but by the third or fourth switch it becomes very hard to keep surviving.

## Recap: the four-step triangle system (leads into next chapter)

1. **Working grip** — secure some form of advantageous grip on the opponent.
2. **One-in, one-out** — trap one of the opponent's arms inside your legs and force the other arm outside, with the head caught in the middle. This is the trap triangle.
3. **Manage the transition from trap triangle to full figure-four** — most successful finishes happen right here; this transition is described as the biggest determinant of triangle success overall.
4. **If the strangle still fails** — escalate to a joint lock. Any time you have a triangle locked, some joint lock is typically available as a second avenue to the finish.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Category A (front, opposite, rear triangle) functions as one interconnected system — access to one usually means access to the other two via a change of angle.
- From hantai sankaku, you almost always have a sweep available; drive **forward**, not sideways, to off-balance the opponent.
- Two sweep outcomes both lead to a finish: opponent recovers forward → front triangle; opponent stays down/avoids turning in → rear triangle (ushiro sankaku).
- The real skill being built is fluidly repositioning your legs around the opponent's head/shoulders to move between all three triangle types.
- The general triangle system is four steps: grip → trap triangle (one-in-one-out) → manage the trap-to-figure-four transition → joint lock if the strangle doesn't finish it.
