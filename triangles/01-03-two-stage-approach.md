# The 2 Stage Approach To Triangles

## Overview

Second of the four critical themes. Most instruction treats "the triangle" as a single move. This course insists on splitting every triangle into two distinct phases — and argues that managing the transition between them is the single biggest determinant of whether your triangle game succeeds in live competition.

---

## The two phases

1. **Trap triangle phase** — ankles simply crossed behind the opponent's back, head and one arm trapped. This is a control position, not a finish. It's the near-universal starting point: "triangles are almost never born fully grown."
2. **Figure-four triangle phase** — the legs are fully locked in figure-four. This is the phase that actually produces the strangle.

## Why the transition is the whole game

- In top-level competition, the pattern is consistent: athletes enter with a trap triangle, then adjust over time into the figure-four.
- The opponent feels the threat the moment the trap triangle locks on, and will resist the transition — so managing this adjustment **against active resistance** is what separates a triangle that finishes from one that doesn't.
- Understanding exactly what's required in each phase, and how the two phases connect, is presented as more valuable than knowing either phase in isolation.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Don't think of "the triangle" as one movement — think trap triangle (entry/control) → figure-four (finish), as two separate skills.
- Expect to start in trap triangle almost every time; a triangle beginning already in figure-four is the exception, not the rule.
- The opponent's resistance kicks in specifically during the transition — that's the moment your triangle game is actually tested.
