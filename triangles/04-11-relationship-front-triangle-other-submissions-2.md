# Relationship Between Front Triangle And Other Submissions 2 (Juji Gatame From Pin)

## Overview

Continues the "submission threat → triangle" theme from the previous chapter, moving from closed guard to a **top-position Juji Gatame pin** (opponent flat on his back, arm isolated). Covers how a stalled armbar attempt from this pin converts into a front triangle strangle.

---

## Building the pin: leg alignment

1. Opponent is down in a classic Juji Gatame position, arms locked defensively.
2. First priority: stability. Hand on your own thigh.
3. **Misalign your legs** — if your legs are dead-straight/symmetrical and the opponent explodes into you, you fall straight backward. Take one foot back to the opponent's ear, the other up near his armpit — **asymmetrical legs**.
4. Come down to an elbow, invert your knee so it points down at the mat. One leg hooks back, the other hooks up into the armpit, lower legs pointing in slightly different directions.

## Isolating the arm

1. Asymmetrical legs let your knee take the **inside position** against his bicep.
2. Bring your foot close to your own knee, push a space open, thread your other foot through.
3. This forces the opponent to re-lock his hands or face real armlock danger.
4. Bring both feet over his shoulder, pull tight — still propped on your elbow.

## Converting to the triangle

1. Catch his leg, bring his head up off the floor (reaching across and pulling brings the head up even if he tries to keep it down).
2. Swap your cross-face leg for a knee wedge behind his neck.
3. Bring his head forward, lock the figure-four.
4. As he pulls away, the strangle applies easily and tightly.

---

## Key Takeaways

- From a top Juji Gatame pin, **asymmetrical/misaligned legs** are the stability foundation — symmetrical legs risk getting knocked straight backward if the opponent explodes.
- The inside-knee-against-the-bicep position (built off the misaligned legs) is what lets you thread a foot through and force a re-lock-or-armlock dilemma.
- Bringing the head up off the floor (by pulling on the caught leg) is the key mechanical step that allows the cross-face leg to be swapped for a neck wedge.
- Same finishing pattern as always: knee wedge behind the head, bring the head forward, figure-four.
