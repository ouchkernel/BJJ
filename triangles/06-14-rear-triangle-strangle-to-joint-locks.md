# Rear Triangle Transitioning From Strangle To Joint Locks

## Overview

Closing chapter of the volume: recaps the 4-step system that underlies every triangle, then delivers the joint-lock backups available from Ushiro Sankaku when the strangle itself won't close.

## The 4-Step Triangle System (Recap)

1. **Working grip** — establish a grip that gives an effective connection to the opponent.
2. **One in, one out** — use that grip to trap one of the opponent's arms inside your legs and lock the other outside; this lets you lock a **trap triangle** (feet crossed, head+arm trapped, not yet strangling).
3. **Trap → figure-four transition** — manage the shift from the trap triangle to the figure-four triangle, which is what actually produces the strangle.
4. **Fallback to joint locks** — if the strangle proves insufficient (e.g., the opponent posts on the knee and prevents closure), move to joint locks instead. Stated general preference: strangles over joint locks, but joint locks are the explicit 4th-step fallback when the strangle stalls.

## The Joint-Lock Menu (From A Stalled Ushiro Sankaku)

Starting point for all of these: locked into Ushiro Sankaku with an extended arm, but the strangle itself won't close.

### 1. Armbar (Jujigatame) — Basic Version
Take the opponent's hand, bring their elbow over your hip, connect their hand to your armpit. This creates a strong fulcrum and turns their arm into a long lever for breaking pressure. Grip **half on their hand, half on their wrist** (too low = short lever; too high = the hand slips out) — double up on the grip and connect their pinky knuckle to your chest.

### 2. Armbar — Stronger Version
V-grip on the wrist, bring their arm in behind your own lat muscle, grab your own buttocks, come up to an elbow. Produces even more breaking pressure than the basic version.

### 3. American Lock
If the opponent bends their arm to escape the armbar: bring your head forward over their head, physically hold their elbow joint, hand-over-hand on the elbow, and apply the American lock — assisted by your lat muscle.

### 4. Kimura
If the opponent instead brings their hands together to re-lock (defending the American lock): post over their head, apply a kimura, take their hand outside your thigh. This version is unusually tight because the opponent's head can't move. Connect their elbow to your chest for maximum effect.

### 5. Wrist Lock
If the opponent keeps their arms in tight, resisting all of the above: chop your wrist into theirs while driving their knuckles over the top. Their elbow is caught on your hip, an obstacle sits in front of their wrist, knuckles are driven forward, and you go elbow-over-elbow (their elbow should not be visible from your side). This finish does **not** go through a figure-four lock — it's just bend-the-wrist, elbow-over-elbow, hands over their knuckles.

## The Complete Package

From a single Ushiro Sankaku entry, you now have **six potential finishes**: the original strangle, plus five distinct joint locks (2 armbar variants, American lock, kimura, wrist lock) — each one a direct answer to how the opponent defended the previous option.

## Key Takeaways

- The 4-step system (grip → one-in-one-out → trap-to-figure-four → joint-lock fallback) is the universal skeleton for every triangle in the system, not just the rear triangle.
- Joint locks are an explicit, planned 4th step — not an improvised backup. When the strangle stalls, this is where you're supposed to go.
- The five joint locks chain off each other based on how the opponent defends: bends the arm → American lock; re-locks hands → kimura; keeps arms in tight → wrist lock. Each defense opens the next option.
- One entry, six finishes — the practical payoff of the whole rear-triangle system.
