# Inside Wrist Grip

## Overview

One of the most effective and versatile grips for entering the front triangle from almost any guard position, especially when flattened onto your back. Unlike the standard overhand wrist push, it works on inside control rather than pushing the opponent's hand toward your centerline.

---

## The standard method (for contrast)

Most people attacking triangles use a basic overhand, pronated grip that pushes the opponent's hand toward the centerline while the other arm extends, throwing a leg over to create the one-in-one-out. It's a good, functional method.

## The inside wrist grip — a different principle

Instead of pushing toward the centerline, this grip is built on **inside control**:

1. Put your thumb inside your opponent's wrist, right at the veins.
2. Fold your hand back so your wrist is bent at roughly 90° — not straight. This puts your wrist **higher** than theirs.
3. With the wrist bent this way, your opponent's only muscles available to pull their hand back toward the centerline are the small, weak rotator cuff muscles at the back of the shoulder — essentially the same effect as an American lock. It doesn't look like much of a grip, but it doesn't need to be strong, because their escape muscles are weak.
4. Their only real direction of movement is **out and away** — which doesn't help them; it actually makes it easier for you to throw your leg over their shoulder.

If your wrist is straight instead of bent, the opponent is only fighting your thumb and can pull their hand back in easily — the bend is what makes the grip work.

## Applications

- **From a flattened supine position:** get the inside wrist grip, extend your arm, keep the wrist bent at 90°. Turn onto your side; your other hand catches the head/collar tie so posturing up is blocked. Bring your foot directly over the opponent's neck. Once the legs are in place, the wrist grip can be released — cover the head, turn the corner (feet forward), wedge in behind the head/neck, lock the figure-four.
- **While being pushed down:** if your wrist stays bent (not straight), the opponent is fully shut out from pulling their hand back in. Control head and neck, turn your hips to the side, bring your knee back to your shoulder to pass your legs over the top, then release the wrist grip and go straight into head/arm control and the finish.
- **Combined with an underhook:** take the inside wrist grip on the far side while underhooking — the opponent essentially cannot body-lock or pull away.
- **Combined with an overtie:** snatch the inside wrist grip once the overtie is on; the opponent can't push their hand back in, letting you pull them around the corner into the usual attacking position.
- **From bottom half guard:** hand goes under the opponent's armpit as they reach for you, then take the inside wrist grip and proceed into the same attacking sequence.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Different mechanism from the standard grip: **inside control**, not centerline pushing.
- Key detail: thumb inside the wrist, wrist bent back ~90° so your wrist sits higher than theirs — this forces them to fight with weak rotator cuff muscles only.
- A straight wrist ruins the grip — the opponent just fights your thumb and escapes.
- The opponent's only available direction (out and away) actually helps your leg get over their shoulder.
- Extremely versatile — combines with an underhook, an overtie, half guard, or as a standalone entry from supine.
