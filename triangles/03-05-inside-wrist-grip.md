# Inside Wrist Grip

## Overview

Introduces one of the favorite grips for entering Mote Sankaku (front triangle) from almost any guard, particularly supine — the "inside wrist grip." Unlike the common pronated push-in grip, this one works through leverage against weak rotator-cuff muscles rather than brute strength.

## The Standard Grip, For Contrast

Most people attack triangles with a basic overhand pronated grip — pushing the opponent's hand inward toward their centerline while the other arm stays extended, creating the one-in-one-out situation as the legs come over. It's a good, workable method.

## What The Inside Wrist Grip Is

- Based on a completely different principle: **inside control**, not pushing.
- Place your thumb inside the opponent's wrist, at the veins.
- Fold your own hand back so your wrist is bent at roughly 90° — not straight. This puts your wrist higher than theirs.
- Result: when the opponent tries to bring their hand back toward their centerline, it's physically impossible. The only muscles available to them for that motion are the small, weak rotator-cuff muscles at the back of the shoulder — comparable to being caught in an American lock. The grip doesn't need to look strong because their available muscles are inherently weak.
- The opponent's only real option becomes moving their arm **out and away** — which doesn't help them and actually makes it easier to throw your leg over their shoulder.

## Sequence From Supine

1. Take the inside wrist position, hold the arm extended, wrist bent back at 90°.
2. As the opponent fights to bring the hand back in, it should feel to them like fighting a brick wall.
3. Turn onto your side; your other hand catches them at the head (e.g., a collar tie) — controls posture directly.
4. Bring your foot directly over their neck.
5. Once the legs are in place, the hand grip is no longer needed — release it.
6. Get a hold of the head, turn the corner with both feet forward, wedge behind the head/neck.
7. Lock the figure-four, knee into the groin, elbow fully controlled — a very tight strangle.

## Grip Detail That Matters

If your wrist is straight (not bent back), the opponent is only working against your thumb and can bring their hand back in easily. The bent-wrist, higher-than-their-wrist positioning is what actually shuts them out.

## Versatility

This grip combines with essentially any other setup already covered — works from an underhook (they can't body-lock you once you dominate the inside position), from an overtie, and from half guard (paired with an elbow-escape motion and a hand under the opponent's armpit). It doesn't matter what your other grip is; snatching the inside wrist grip funnels into the same one-in-one-out entry every time.

## Key Takeaways

- The inside wrist grip works through leverage against weak rotator-cuff muscles, not raw strength — the bent-wrist (90°), thumb-inside-the-veins setup is what makes this work.
- Once your legs are in place, you no longer need the grip — it's a means to the one-in-one-out position, not the finishing mechanism.
- Extremely versatile: layers on top of underhooks, overties, half guard, and more without changing its core mechanic.
