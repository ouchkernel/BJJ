# Unified Legs

## Overview

A focused correction on one specific leg-positioning error: the two lower legs pointing in different directions during the turn to angle. Small detail, large effect on both effort required and opponent's ability to escape.

---

## The problem

- Common mistake: athlete makes a good turn to angle but does a poor job with leg positioning — the two lower legs end up pointing in **different directions** rather than together.
- When the lower legs point in opposing directions, there's effectively no resistance behind the position — the opponent can posture up with no real defense stopping them ("it's a joke," in the instructor's words).

## The fix: both lower legs point the same direction

- Desired state: the two lower legs point **together, like arrows, in the same direction** — not merely close together, but genuinely unified in orientation.
- The more the lower legs share direction, the less muscular work is required to actually close the triangle, and the harder it becomes for the opponent to escape.
- With unified legs and the opponent trying to yank out: very difficult. Their shoulder stays excluded and the finish comes on immediately.
- Without unity (legs pointing in different directions): the opponent postures up freely, and the triangle formation's integrity collapses.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Rule: both lower legs point in the same direction**, functioning as unified "arrows," not independently.
- This is a distinct failure mode from the shoulder-line and shoulder-elimination rules covered earlier — you can nail those and still lose the triangle if your two legs are misaligned relative to each other.
- Unified legs directly reduces the effort needed to close the figure-four and directly increases how hard it is for the opponent to yank free or posture out.
