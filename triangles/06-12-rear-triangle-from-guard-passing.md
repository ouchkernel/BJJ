# Rear Triangle From Guard Passing

## Overview

Rear triangle opportunities that arise while passing a seated guard. A seated guard always exposes some back on the far side — the entry routes through a kimura-style grip that removes the opponent's ability to turn away.

## Setup Logic

- Anytime an opponent is in a seated guard, there's a degree of back exposure on the far side.
- Passing a seated guard involves grip exchanges from both athletes as they reach — that reaching is exactly what creates the opening.

## Sequence 1

1. Post one foot inside the opponent's legs, knee over their far hip — establishes inside position.
2. Work to get your armpit **higher than their head** — usually easy since the opponent is typically driving forward to attack.
3. Either secure the opponent's wrist directly, or post your hand to the floor first — both are viable.
4. At some point, secure the opponent's arm with your frame arm.
5. Roll forward and over the opponent's body, applying a **kimura grip**.
6. If the opponent stays put (naively), attack directly. If they try to turn away, they can't — the kimura prevents it, so their only option is to turn **into** you.
7. As they turn in: knee goes inside/underneath their hip, lift the elbow so they can't complete the turn.
8. Throw your other foot over the opponent's shoulder and use it to return them to the mat.
9. Catch their knuckles, apply your legs (lower legs pointing the same direction — unified), lock the trap triangle.
10. Hand-assist everything north, foot behind the lat muscle, strangle.

## Sequence 2 — Facing The Opposite Direction

1. Same guard-pass starting point: foot inside, knee drives forward to the opposite hip.
2. Catch a kimura, roll across the opponent's body.
3. Form a **T-shape** relative to the opponent.
4. As they try to turn desperately into you, focus on lifting the elbow and driving your knee to their lat muscle.
5. Foot comes through and hooks over the opponent.
6. Result: the "one in, one out" position — foot inside the armpit, the precursor to every triangle entry.
7. Lock an initial trap triangle, hand-assist to a full figure-four, strangle is set.

## Key Takeaways

- The kimura grip is the mechanism that removes the opponent's turn-away option during a guard pass — it forces them to turn into you, which is what sets up the triangle.
- Getting your armpit above the opponent's head early makes securing the frame arm and rolling into the kimura much easier.
- Both directional variants (Sequence 1 and 2) end the same way: one-in-one-out → trap triangle → hand-assist → figure-four → strangle.
