# Underhooks To Triangle

## Overview

Covers underhook-based triangle entries from bottom (supine) position, including the correct hand/elbow mechanics for a strong underhook, and two distinct underhook variations — near-side and far-side — each leading into a triangle.

## Underhook Strengths And Weaknesses

- Strength: distance control, similar to the overhook.
- Weakness: if your head position is poor and you're too far from the opponent, they can easily bring their elbow back inside, strip your underhook, punch in their own underhook, and flatten you out.

## Correct Underhook Mechanics

1. **Hand position**: cup over the opponent's shoulder so the blade of your hand comes over and cups the **front deltoid** — not a superficial grip on the rear deltoid, which lets them slip their elbow through. Clear their elbow out of the way first if needed, then punch through with a false grip (bent wrist).
2. **Elbow position**: keep your forearm **parallel to the mat**, elbow high. A low elbow lets the opponent clear it and steal the underhook back. The higher your elbow, the greater your control.

## Entry 1 — Near-Side Underhook (Flattened On Your Back)

1. From being flattened out, hit a forward shift to come up onto your **elbow** (not your shoulder — you need the mobility an elbow provides; a shoulder doesn't give enough space to work the underhook into a triangle).
2. Elbow on the floor behind you, foot either on the opponent's thigh or the floor outside — this frees your hip to move.
3. Once the hip moves, pull the opponent into the triangle attack: lock the trap triangle, punch the underhook hand through to control the head.
4. As they posture up and away, it's difficult given the head control; wedge in behind, lock the figure-four.

### Handling Opponent Resistance (Stacking)

The first line of resistance is almost always posture (hence controlling the head first); the next is stacking. Get the angle on the opponent *before* they can set a good stack, and stacking becomes largely useless against you — if they're naive enough to keep stacking anyway, you can push them straight over onto their back.

## Entry 2 — Far-Side Underhook

1. Instead of a conventional near-side underhook, clear the opponent's elbow and reach your head/hand across to the **far side** of their neck — controls both head and arm simultaneously, creating an especially deep underhook. Very difficult for the opponent to yank the arm free from here.
2. From here, come up to a kneeling position. This underhook variation strongly negates the opponent's attempt to apply a whizzer against you — a whizzer barely registers because of how the grip is structured.
3. Knee against the opponent's hip, foot close to their knee, throw your leg over their lower back — sets up a quick jump directly into the trap triangle.
4. Punch your arm around the corner for head control, make the quick turn, lock the figure-four.

## Key Takeaways

- Correct underhook mechanics (front-deltoid cup, high elbow, forearm parallel to the mat) are what actually make the grip usable for a triangle — a low/shallow underhook gets stripped before you can use it.
- Come up to an **elbow**, not a shoulder, when working an underhook-based entry from flat on your back — the mobility difference matters.
- The far-side underhook variant trades a slightly different setup for a major bonus: it neutralizes the opponent's whizzer defense almost entirely.
