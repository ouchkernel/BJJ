# Kimura To Back Position: The Body Triangle Entry

## Overview

A third major strategy for the central kimura problem — the times you simply cannot get the opponent's hand behind his back. Rather than incorporating the legs or switching to juji gatame/triangle, this lesson uses the kimura purely as a restraining hold to transition into an entirely different position: the back, described as the favorite position in jiu-jitsu because nothing else offers such a clear gap between what you can do to your opponent and what he can do back to you. Two full demonstrations (mirror-image angles) show the same route into a body triangle and a strangle.

---

## The fundamental parting of ways

- There's a fundamental split in the kimura system depending on whether the hand makes it from front position to back position. Often it won't.
- Strategy recap: strategy one was incorporating the legs into the kimura (cross kimura, kimura plata, trimura); strategy two was switching to an alternative submission (juji gatame, triangle). This lesson is strategy three: abandon the kimura as a submission, use it as a restraint, and switch to the back position entirely.

## Entry sequence (first demonstration)

1. Start in a dorsal kimura position where the hand cannot be gotten behind the back.
2. Bring the chin down so it's lower than the opponent's elbow — this move requires the chin never be higher than the elbow.
3. Turn the hand palm up.
4. Walk around behind the training partner: use the forearm and elbow behind his head, walk past the shoulder line, and drive across in that direction.
5. Put a wedge behind the training partner's back and bring your shoulder down below his head so you are ear-to-ear. (If the head is away, it's hard to reach the hand; ear-to-ear makes reaching the hand easy.)
6. From here, secure a figure-of-four grip — this is a kimura using the arm. Drag the training partner in; he'll be forced to start locking his hands, or else face a simple arm lock.
7. Bring a foot in close and pass it across his body; bring the second leg out. You now have two unweighted feet, and you're on the opponent's back.

## Into the body triangle and strangle

1. Hook a foot into the opponent's thigh and lift, so your body falls across him — this lands you in position to lock an initial body triangle.
2. From the body triangle, seize the training partner in double straight grips (as used in the wider back system).
3. Use a pass-off to gain control of his wrist. When he tries to peel your wrist off, he hands you an easy double cross grip.
4. When he tries to pull away from these grips, it becomes frustratingly difficult for him.
5. Bring the leg over the top and hook it; secure the knuckle line, holding the opponent's palm into his own chest — pulling the arms free from here is shockingly difficult.
6. Walk through into the finished strangle position.

## Entry sequence (second demonstration, mirrored angle)

1. From a conventional kimura position where the arm can't be secured: chin down, post, open the knee, and walk around.
2. Apply pressure forward while guiding behind the opponent's head with the arm; bring ear to ear.
3. This quickly locks a kimura that incorporates both his head and his arm.
4. Drive forward, sliding the knee and elbow in close together.
5. Pass a foot across the opposite side; take the second leg out — two unweighted legs.
6. Hook a foot into the opponent's hip, lift, and drop directly into the body triangle position.
7. Get a hold of both of the training partner's hands at the knuckle line.
8. Use a pass-off to secure the secondary defensive hand; when he peels your hand off, he surrenders the primary defensive hand.
9. Go around the corner and put the foot behind his spine.
10. Secure the knuckle line — pulling away from here becomes surprisingly difficult, and the strangle finish follows relatively easily.

## Why this matters

- This is presented as a genuine parallel option to fighting through the kimura directly: when a dorsal kimura stalls and neither leg-incorporation nor juji gatame/triangle feels right, switching to the back — Danaher's favorite position — via the body triangle is a fully viable route to a finish.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Chin below the opponent's elbow (never above it), palm turned up, and walking the forearm/elbow behind his head are the three technical keys to starting the transition around to the back.
- Getting ear-to-ear with the opponent is the checkpoint that makes reaching his far hand easy — head positioning away makes it hard.
- The figure-of-four kimura grip during the walk-around forces the opponent to lock his hands defensively or accept a simple arm lock, and drags him into range for the back take.
- Two unweighted feet on the opponent's back is the trigger to hook a thigh, lift, and fall into an initial body triangle.
- From the body triangle, standard back-system tools apply: double straight grips, pass-offs to bait the double cross grip, securing the knuckle line, and pinning the palm to the opponent's own chest to make arm-freeing shockingly difficult.
- This is strategy three (after leg-incorporation and armbar/triangle switching) for the kimura system's central problem: use the kimura as a restraint to escort the opponent into the back system instead of forcing the submission itself.
