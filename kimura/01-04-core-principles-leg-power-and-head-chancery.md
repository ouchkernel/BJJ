# Core Principles: Leg Power And Head Chancery

## Overview

Two more of the kimura system's core concepts. The arms-versus-legs asymmetry principle is the direct justification for bringing legs into the kimura whenever hand-fighting stalls. The head chancery principle explains why controlling the opponent's head — not just his trapped arm — is so central to actually finishing the hold.

---

## The arms versus legs asymmetry principle

- A second bodily asymmetry the system exploits: in every human body, the legs are dramatically stronger than the arms.
- You may run into an opponent with arms stronger than yours, but very few opponents have arms stronger than your legs.
- Practical rule: whenever you can't get the hand behind the back quickly with your arms, bring your legs into the kimura game.
- Using legs to force the hand behind the back also solves the structural problem named in the previous lessons: it stops the fight from becoming purely "my arms versus his arms" (the classic weakness kimura is criticized for). Bring the legs to bear and take advantage of the fact that your legs are stronger than his arms.

## The head chancery principle

- On its surface, kimura looks like just a lock of your two arms on one of the opponent's arms. In practice, almost all effective kimura defense comes from the opponent's ability to move his head and escape.
- If you can lock the opponent's head down — usually with your legs — you dramatically increase your ability to control him and prevent escape.
- A locked head also has a mechanical benefit beyond just limiting movement: when the head can't move, the shoulder girdle becomes strongly locked, and the elbow and shoulder become much more vulnerable to breaking.
- The favored method for achieving head chancery/head trapping is through triangles — most often the trimura (the triangle/kimura hybrid introduced earlier). Locking the opponent's head with the legs whenever possible makes the finishing techniques significantly more effective.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Legs are always stronger than an opponent's arms — whenever hand-fighting for the arm behind the back stalls, bring the legs in rather than continuing an arms-vs-arms battle.
- Kimura's real vulnerability isn't the arm lock itself — it's the opponent's head mobility. Nearly all effective escapes come from head movement.
- Locking the head down (most often via the trimura/triangle) does double duty: it removes the opponent's main escape route and locks the shoulder girdle, making the elbow and shoulder much more vulnerable to the break.
- Whenever possible, combine leg power and head control together rather than relying on arm strength alone.
