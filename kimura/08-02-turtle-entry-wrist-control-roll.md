# Turtle Entry: Wrist-Control Roll

## Overview

The realistic, high-percentage entry into the leg-controlled Kimura lock, launched from turtle position. This lesson covers the wrist-control variant: instead of feeding a hand through to reach the opponent's leg, the hand reaches through and grabs the opponent's wrist, then a pendulum action of the free leg drives the opponent into a roll that lands the leg-controlled Kimura, with careful head-position management along the way.

---

## Getting to the crucifix-type position

1. From the training partner in turtle position, move out to one side.
2. As in a basic crucifix attack, place the knee behind the training partner's tricep as a wedge.
3. As control of the arm is gained, sweep the leg around the corner and hook in to a fairly standard back-crucifix-type position.

## Passing the arm from one leg to the other

- With the opponent's arm trapped in one leg, it must be passed to the other leg.
- Plant a hand on the floor and apply pressure on the training partner's trapped arm.
- As the pressure mounts, the opponent has to turn his thumb to relieve it — the moment he does, the arm catches in the second leg.
- Align with the training partner and sit on the arm — from here it's very difficult for him to withdraw it.

## Two options from here

Once the arm pass is complete, there are two good ways to initiate the roll into the Kimura:
1. **Control of the arm** (this lesson) — reach through and grab the opponent's wrist.
2. **Control of the lower body**, to facilitate the roll more easily (the leg-control variant — see the companion lesson).

## The wrist-control roll

1. Reach through and grab the opponent's wrist directly (rather than reaching for the leg).
2. Come up into an inverted position. With the wrist grip locked, it is essentially impossible for the opponent to pull the arm free.
3. Use the second (outside) leg like a pendulum to force the training partner into a roll.
4. Even if the opponent resists and makes his body strong, the pendulum action of the outside leg will always start putting his hand behind his back and taking him down to the mat.

## Head-position timing — the critical detail

- Danger point: as the opponent rolls with you, his head can come up and rotate ahead of yours before you control it.
- Rule: as you roll, your head must stay in front of the opponent's head.
- Once your head is ahead, it's an easy switch of hands to bring your head across the opponent's body.
- With your head established across, when the opponent's head tries to rise and follow, it runs into your tricep — he is now well pinned.

## Full sequence, repeated for clarity

1. Turtle position — wedge behind the tricep, separate the opponent's hands.
2. Leg comes around the corner and catches the arm.
3. Apply pressure on the arm to motivate the opponent to limp the arm free and feed it into the second leg.
4. Turn the corner — bring the near-side hand down past the opponent's ear and grip the wrist.
5. Come up into the inverted position; even resisting, the torque from the legs forces the roll (if he doesn't roll with it, the arm is hurt).
6. Bring the hand across the body — timing matters; the opponent's head cannot get in front of yours.
7. Head stays in front, arm goes across; plant the hands and turn back toward the training partner's head for a strong finish.

## Common mistake

- Failing to keep the head in front during the roll — this lets the opponent's head win the race and escape/counter before control is established.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Entry sequence: wedge behind tricep → sweep leg around → back-crucifix-type hold → pass the arm from one leg to the other via pressure-induced thumb rotation.
- This variant grabs the opponent's wrist directly (rather than the leg) for arm control, then uses the outside leg as a pendulum to force the roll.
- The single most important detail is keeping your head in front of the opponent's head throughout the roll — losing that race lets him escape before you can pin him.
- Once your head is established across his body (with the arm following), his head rising into your tricep completes the pin, setting up a strong finish.
