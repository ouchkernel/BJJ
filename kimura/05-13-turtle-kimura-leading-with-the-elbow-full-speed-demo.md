# Turtle Kimura — Leading With The Elbow (Full-Speed Demo)

## Overview

A full-speed run-through of the turtle-position kimura entry shown from a fresh camera angle, ending with a specific correction on how to fall into the breaking position: lead with the elbow, not the head, so a turning opponent can't put you face down.

---

## Entry from turtle

- Training partner starts in a strong defensive turtle position.
- Bring your head forward so you can access his wrists.
- Sometimes feed a wrist across and bring it in nice and tight.
- Come around the corner and secure a two-on-one grip on the training partner's arm; hold it.
- Use your knee to control the training partner's head.
- With head and arm controlled, you can go straight to the bar arm even if he plants his other hand on the floor and pushes hard to come back — it means nothing, the arm is already trapped.

## Locking and breaking

- Come in and lock up the original kimura grip, working the hand over.
- Even if he keeps his base hand on the floor and refuses to let the trapped arm go over, the pressure of the kimura is directed forwards — it puts so much breaking pressure on that he has to roll just to relieve it.

## The key correction: lead with the elbow

- **Common mistake:** coming around the corner and diving head-first. If he turns to the left as you do this, you will face-plant.
- **Correct method:** always lead with the elbow. The elbow comes over first; when he turns left, your elbow and foot hold you up instead of your face.
- From here, take a big, bold step across his body, come elbow to elbow, and turn back onto the power line — that's the breaking position.
- Because you led with the elbow (not the head), your whole body can continue moving well past the initial breaking point, producing tremendous follow-through on the break.

## Why this matters

- This follow-through is what makes the position so dangerous — the feeling of the arm being absolutely ready to snap is intimidating for the opponent.
- Historically, this kind of follow-through breaking pressure is exactly what Kimura himself produced with this move — it's a strong part of why the technique bears his name and why it was such a devastating part of his game working down on the mat.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Turtle kimura entry: head forward to access wrists, two-on-one grip, knee controls the head, lock the kimura — the trapped arm stays trapped even if he bases out and pushes.
- Never dive head-first around the corner into the breaking position — if he turns into you, you'll face-plant.
- Lead with the elbow instead: elbow comes over first and props you up if he turns, then step boldly across his body to elbow-to-elbow and onto the power line.
- Leading with the elbow lets your whole body continue traveling well past the break point, giving tremendous follow-through pressure — the mechanism behind Kimura's own devastating success with this move.
