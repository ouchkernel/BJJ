# Rule Of Opposites — When One Elbow Drops, The Other Rises

## Overview

A fix for the single biggest frustration with the elbow penetration method: it depends on the opponent's elbow being elevated, and a smart opponent will simply drop the targeted elbow to the floor to kill the entry. The solution isn't a new technique — it's a mechanical fact about the body: as one elbow is pulled down, the other elbow is forced to rise. Attacking whichever elbow is currently up, rather than fixating on one side, turns the opponent's own defense into the opening.

---

## The problem: opponent kills the elevated elbow

- Elbow penetration depends entirely on the opponent's elbow being elevated. If the elbow drops toward the floor, it becomes very hard to get underneath and through to a kimura grip.
- Frustrating scenario: you get to north-south, get underneath the elbow, straighten your legs, and are underneath to some degree — but the opponent recognizes the danger to (say) their left arm, makes the arm strong, turns to that side, and pulls the elbow down and inside to the floor. This kills your ability to get underneath the arm and elevate the elbow.
- Sequence of failure: north-south → underneath → try to lift → opponent turns → elbow lost. Repeating the same attack on the same arm just repeats the failure.

## The rule of opposites

- Key mechanical fact: the more the opponent brings one elbow down to defend it, the higher the **other** elbow is forced to rise. This works symmetrically both directions.
- The real problem people run into is tunnel vision — fixating on "I'm attacking the left arm" and driving for it hell or high water. The opponent brings that elbow to the floor, the attacker fails, resets to neutral, tries the same left elbow again, and fails again.
- **Vital rule**: as one elbow goes down, the other must rise. You do not get to specify which elbow you attack in advance — in north-south, both elbows are in front of you, and the opponent decides which one is defended, not you.
- Practical application: initiate an attack on one arm (e.g., the left). If the opponent defends by dropping that elbow, they are simultaneously handing you the opposite arm to penetrate. Switch to it immediately.
- Once you have the opposite arm elevated: get the hand behind the back if possible, bring the elbow to the floor, and step forward into a strong finishing position.

## Practical guidance

- All you need is **one** elevated elbow — it doesn't matter which one.
- If the opponent depresses one elbow, they have to elevate the other — this is guaranteed, not optional for them.
- Don't get tunnel vision. There is never only one arm available to attack in north-south — there are always two elbows right in front of you.
- When you see one elbow go down, don't get frustrated — simply switch your attack to the other elbow. This is where the consistent success comes from.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Elbow penetration lives or dies on the opponent's elbow being elevated — a low elbow is very hard to penetrate.
- Mechanical rule: as an opponent depresses one elbow to defend it, the other elbow is forced to rise — this is unavoidable for them.
- Never fixate on one arm. In north-south you always have two elbows to choose from, and the opponent's own defense tells you which one to attack.
- When your target elbow gets defended and dropped, immediately switch to the elbow that just rose instead of repeating the failed attack.
- Once you get the opposite elbow elevated: hand behind the back if possible, elbow to the floor, step forward into a strong finish.
