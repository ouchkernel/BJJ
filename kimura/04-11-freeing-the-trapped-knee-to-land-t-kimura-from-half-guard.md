# Freeing The Trapped Knee To Land T-Kimura From Half Guard

## Overview

Explains why so much time is invested in the T-Kimura position earlier in the course: landing in it from any scramble or uncontrolled situation gives excellent control over the opponent's movement, with both bottom-knee and top-knee finishing routes available from there. This chapter shows the mechanics of climbing over the opponent's half guard — specifically freeing the trapped knee — in order to land in that T-Kimura and convert it to a win.

---

## Why T-Kimura matters

- From any scramble or uncontrolled situation, landing in a T-Kimura gives excellent control of the opponent's movement.
- From T-Kimura you can use either bottom-knee finishes or top-knee finishes, converting what looks like a chaotic, uncontrolled scramble into a finished submission.

## Starting point

- The opponent (Matthias) has a good grip on your leg in half guard.
- You've secured the initial Kimura grip on the opponent.
- As you come forward with the Kimura, you work the opponent's hand down toward the floor and start coming up on your head.

## The core problem: the trapped knee

- The biggest concern working from half guard is freeing the knee of your trapped leg.
- As long as the opponent controls your leg above the knee, he controls the leg overall.
- The moment your knee is freed, the opponent has considerably less control — once the lock of his legs is beneath your knee, he has very little control over your leg's movement.
- At the start of this sequence, the opponent is "in the driver's seat" because he's controlling your leg above the knee.

## Freeing the knee

1. Post on your head.
2. Come up into a tripod position.
3. Take your second leg and put your foot in as a wedge (fit inside the opponent's knee).
4. This frees the knee of the trapped leg.
5. Once the knee is free, bring that knee across the opponent's hip and wedge it on the hip.
6. From there, come out and onto a free leg.

## Landing the T-Kimura

- This transitional moment is inherently unstable — it's not a position of stability on its own.
- That instability doesn't matter, because the Kimura is already locked, which is what actually controls the opponent.
- Coming across the opponent's body from this free-leg position lands you in a T-Kimura.

## T-Kimura decision tree

The T-Kimura is framed as giving the opponent no good options:

- **Opponent turns away** — doesn't work, because the Kimura holds him in place regardless.
- **Opponent stands still / doesn't move** — doesn't work either, because you can simply sit up and come into a pin across his side with the arm trapped.
- **Opponent turns up into you** — his only real remaining option. Two responses are available:
  1. **Strategy one**: come in with the bottom knee (covered earlier in the course).
  2. **Strategy two**: throw your leg over as a cross-face and put both of the opponent's shoulders on the floor. From there, come up to your knees, put your knee around the opponent's hands, and rise into a cross-Kimura. Close and finish, using your legs to amplify the power of the Kimura.

---

## Key Takeaways

- T-Kimura is valuable because it converts a scramble into strong control, with both bottom-knee and top-knee finishing options available from it.
- Freeing the trapped knee is the central problem in half guard: control above the knee = opponent controls the leg; free knee = opponent loses most control.
- Sequence to free the knee: post on head → tripod → second foot in as a wedge inside the opponent's knee → knee frees → bring knee across opponent's hip → come out onto a free leg → land T-Kimura.
- The transitional position is unstable, but that's irrelevant as long as the Kimura grip is already locked — trust the grip, not the base.
- T-Kimura gives the opponent no good escape: turning away or standing still both lose; turning up into you is his only option, answered with either a bottom-knee finish or a cross-face into cross-Kimura, finished with the legs amplifying the arm's power.
