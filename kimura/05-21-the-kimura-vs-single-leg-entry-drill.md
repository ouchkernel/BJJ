# The Kimura Vs Single-Leg Entry Drill

## Overview

The dedicated drill for making the Kimura-vs-single-leg entry automatic. It's practiced from a deliberately artificial starting position (both partners on their knees) so the reps can be run repeatedly and efficiently — not as live training. The drill takes a partner from shooting the single leg all the way through to a locked Kimura, and shows two different finishing branches from there: committing to the ground for a T-Kimura, or staying on top for a different follow-up entirely.

---

## Starting setup

- Partners start facing each other, both on two knees, in a fairly standard wrestling tie — hands in the inside position.
- The instructor notes this starting position is a little artificial — he doesn't like to see students starting matches on their knees like this; he prefers starting from a set position or standing. But for drilling purposes (not live training) it's fine, since it lets the specific transition be repeated efficiently.

## The core sequence

1. Surrender head position — move the head away from the opponent. He quickly drops and goes directly into the single-leg situation.
2. First response is always to chop in with a quasi-whizzer (an overhook/whizzer-type control on the near arm).
3. Turn the body side-on to the training partner off that whizzer.
4. Keep the second hand on the partner's head. If his head starts to come up in this position, that creates real problems for getting to the Kimura — so the first requirement is angle plus downward pressure on the head.
5. Off the head control, go through with an open hand and put the wrist over his forearm. Don't settle for a shallow, superficial grip — punch all the way through, over the forearm, and lock.
6. Second hand goes to the floor for base.
7. As the opponent tries to work further around behind, the Kimura frame itself puts a limit on how far he can go — walk with him (as he walks, you walk) and hold position easily.
8. Lock the figure-of-four grip.
9. Touch the partner's back to the ground — that's the full finish of this branch. Sometimes the finisher doesn't even go all the way to the ground himself and instead stays in a seated position (more on this option covered separately).

## Full drill from the resting position

- Start resting in the tied-up position; partner shoots the legs immediately.
- Whizzer goes on, the knee opens, and the head is stuffed down — weight goes onto the opponent so that when he tries to move around, there's good control over him.
- Rise up, shoot the hand through, and go wrist-deep over the forearm.
- **Safety note:** be careful with training partners here — don't bring the hand through carelessly and poke a partner in the eye, nose, or mouth. Consciously avoid clumsiness with the hand when coming through; this kind of mistake makes for unpleasant training sessions.
- Punch in and get locked in place. From here it's easy to lock the hands together — simply reach to the buttocks to complete the figure-of-four.
- From that locked position, two options: tilt the training partner over, or commit all the way down to the back and into the T-Kimura position.

## Repeat rep, with a top-position alternative

- Opponent has the inside position as both wrestle from the tie; he shoots into the legs.
- Initial whizzer goes on, initial push on the head, weight comes over the man as he tries to move.
- Shoot the hand through for the base of support — if the opponent tries to go around to the back from here, it's now difficult for him because of the control already established.
- Lift and lock; extend the right leg through. From here, the opponent can be put down to a hip and taken back to a T-Kimura — one solid option.
- Alternative option: punch the hand through, get the hand in, and as he tries to come back up, hit a hip-heist. Result: ending up in a good position on top of the training partner instead of committing to T-Kimura.
- Key point: you don't have to go all the way to T-Kimura every time — there are many things you can do from this control. The Kimura is what starts the sequence going, not necessarily where it ends.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The drill is intentionally run from an artificial knees-on-knees start so the specific single-leg-to-Kimura transition can be repeated rapidly and efficiently — it's a drill, not live training.
- Sequence in order: surrender head position → opponent shoots → quasi-whizzer + turn side-on → head control (watch for his head rising) → punch hand through wrist-deep over the forearm (no shallow grip) → base hand to the floor → walk with him as the Kimura frame limits his movement → lock figure-of-four.
- Safety detail worth internalizing: bring the hand through consciously and cleanly to avoid poking a partner in the eye, nose, or mouth.
- Two legitimate finishing branches once the Kimura is locked: commit to the ground for T-Kimura (touch his back down, or stay seated), or stay on top and hit a hip-heist as he tries to stand back up.
- The Kimura doesn't have to end in T-Kimura every rep — its real job is to shut down the single-leg attempt and start a sequence, with multiple good positions available from there.
