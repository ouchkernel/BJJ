# Incorporating The Legs: Three New Methods

## Overview

Steps back to restate how the whole kimura system is organized, acknowledges the realistic limits of the simple hand-reinforcement counters against a skilled or forewarned opponent, and introduces the next major tool for those tougher situations: bringing the legs into the kimura for a decisive strength advantage. This lesson sets up three new leg-based methods and names the first of them, cross kimura.

---

## The system's basic split

- The kimura system is bifurcated right down the middle by one simple consideration: is the opponent's hand in an anterior position (in front) or a posterior position (behind the back)?
- General rule: always try, as a first move, to get the hand behind the back — a position of tremendous mechanical weakness that can easily be exploited into a submission.

## The realistic problem

- Against an opponent of similar size and skill, actually getting the hand behind the back is hard work.
- Skilled opponents anticipate the kimura, especially if you have a reputation as a kimura specialist, and will do everything they can to keep the hand reinforced in front of the body.
- Obvious question: what do you do when you simply cannot take a reinforced hand from in front of the body?

## Three-part answer, and the first part: incorporate the legs

- There is essentially a three-part means of dealing with this scenario. The first of these — and a particular favorite — is to incorporate the legs into the kimura.
- Kimura is traditionally thought of as your two hands against the opponent's one arm. But when the opponent has locked his hands together or reinforced a hand with one of his own legs, it's easy to bring your own legs in to create a big strength advantage in your favor.
- General rule: the legs are significantly stronger than the arms, and this advantage can be used any time you're working for a kimura.

## Three new leg-based methods

- Three new methods of kimura are introduced, all of which directly use the legs to gain their effect.
- The first of these, and one of the most important, is the so-called **cross kimura**.

## Cross kimura — the setup

- Demonstrated here from a dorsal position, though it can be applied from many other positions as well.
- Scenario: the opponent has locked his hands together, so getting the hand behind the back directly isn't available.
- In this situation, you post a leg on the training partner and throw your foot from one side of his body across to the other.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The whole kimura system splits on one question: is the opponent's hand anterior (front) or posterior (behind the back)? Always try for posterior first.
- Against a skilled or forewarned opponent, forcing the hand behind the back directly is genuinely hard — they'll actively defend against it.
- When you can't win the hand directly, incorporate the legs — kimura isn't limited to two hands versus one arm, and the legs are significantly stronger than the arms.
- Three new leg-based kimura methods are introduced; the first is the cross kimura, set up by posting a leg on the opponent and throwing the foot across his body when his hands are locked together.
