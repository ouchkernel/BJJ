# Side Control Kimura: Lead With The Elbow

## Overview

Returns to the standard side control kimura with a figure-four grip — the very first kimura most people learn — and isolates one crucial detail in converting from the dorsal position into the attacking (side) kimura: which body part leads the turn. Leading with the hands is a very common mistake that causes a face plant and a mechanically weak position. Leading with the elbow instead turns that same elbow into a wedge against the opponent's hip, neutralizing his ability to turn and escape.

---

## Starting position

- Training partner in side control bottom, initially elevated onto his side in the so-called dorsal position (arm up in the kimura grip).
- The task: convert this dorsal situation into an attacking side kimura.

## The common mistake: leading with the hands

- Sequence of the mistake: get the opponent's hand successfully behind his back, then push forward leading with the hands, going with the hands down to the floor.
- If the opponent turns vigorously toward that side while you're leading with the hands, you tend to face plant — your head goes down to the floor, the exact opposite of what you want.
- Face planting isn't just unpleasant — it's mechanically inefficient. It puts your head in a very poor position to exert leverage on the opponent's arm.

## The fix: lead with the elbow

1. Once the hand is behind the back, rotate and turn, but lead with the **elbow**, not the hands.
2. Take the elbow down to the floor first. This ensures your head never goes down to the mat.
3. Now, when the opponent turns vigorously toward that side, you don't move — you're stuck in place (in a good way), because your elbow acts as a wedge on his hip.
4. This makes it easy to step over the training partner's head and split the legs into the perfect breaking position.

## Why the elbow wedge works

- With the elbow wedged at the opponent's hip, when he tries to pull his arm free, move his hips, or shrimp left and right, he's stuck — pinned by your hip pressure and elbow position.
- Walking across the opponent's body from here produces tremendous breaking pressure, without ever exposing you to his defensive twisting and turning, left or right.

## General rule

- Converting a dorsal-position kimura into a side kimura: **lead with the elbow, not with the hands.**

---

## Key Takeaways

- Leading with the hands when converting dorsal-to-side kimura risks a face plant if the opponent turns vigorously — bad both for control and for leverage.
- Leading with the elbow to the floor keeps your head up and turns the elbow into a wedge against the opponent's hip.
- The elbow wedge stops the opponent's turn dead, letting you step over his head and split into the breaking position safely.
- Once wedged, the opponent's attempts to shrimp, turn, or pull the arm free are neutralized by combined hip pressure and elbow position, letting you walk across his body for the finish without exposure.
