# Pronated Wrist Grip Entry From Bottom Side Control

## Overview

A companion entry to the supinated (palm-up) wrist grip covered previously, used for the opposite hand position: when the opponent's hand has come down below the line of his chest rather than staying above it. Against an opponent pinned in bottom side control who is defending well — balled up, or fighting with an underhook — the entry hinges on first neutralizing the underhook with elbow-to-elbow control, then feeding a palm-down (pronated) grip inside to capture the wrist.

---

## When to use pronated vs. supinated grips

- Hand above the line of the chest → favor **supinated** (palm-up) grips.
- Hand below the line of the chest → favor **pronated** (palm-down) grips.

## The defensive picture

Working from bottom side control against a sound opponent, the common defensive shapes seen are:

- Balling up.
- Taking an underhook (arm underneath, hooking your body) — the most common position addressed here.

## Why elbow-to-elbow comes first

- If you leave two arms on the same side as the opponent's underhook, you've made it easy for him to use that underhook to expose your back and climb up into you — a bad outcome.
- Fix: control down at the hip and control at the elbow joint.
- The further your elbow sits from the opponent's elbow, the more effectively he can use the underhook to move your body around and cause problems.
- So the first move is always to bring your elbow to his elbow (elbow-to-elbow position). Once achieved, the underhook becomes considerably harder for him to use.

## Pronated grip entry sequence

1. With elbow-to-elbow control established, punch back and take a palm-down (pronated) grip on the opponent's wrist.
2. Give a quick lift — even against a strong, resistant arm, it's possible to lift directly over the wrist.
3. Feed your hand inside as you lift.
4. Once the hand is inside, you're in position to attack.

## Finishing depending on opponent's response

- If the opponent is naive and leaves the hand exposed, it's straightforward to bring the hand behind the back and go directly into the finishing sequences.
- If the opponent locks his arms together or tucks the hand inside his own thigh, fall back on the earlier-covered measures: control the training partner, work the hands through, lock the arm over the back with the elbow on the floor, then move into a strong finishing position.

## Full repeat sequence

1. Elbow to elbow first — this alone makes the opponent's underhook surprisingly hard to use.
2. Pull the arm back and take the pronated grip, positioning the hand close to his elbow.
3. Give a quick lift over the wrist — described as "lift, flick, and the hand's in front."
4. From the Kimura grip, use the earlier concepts: bring the hand up, place it in behind the back, elbow down on the mat, etc.
5. If the opponent's hands can't be separated, alternative options remain — e.g., throw a foot over into a cross-Kimura position and attack directly from there.

## "Freebie" openings

- Sometimes an opponent simply makes a mistake and offers an easy target — e.g., posting a hand on the knee while you're in a knee-on-belly position gives a very easy pronated grip entry: just grab the wrist, come up to the top, and take the Kimura.
- These freebies shouldn't be counted on — assume a skilled opponent won't make basic errors, which is why the elbow-to-elbow-first sequence above is the real, reliable route in.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Pronated (palm-down) grips are for when the opponent's hand is below his chest line; supinated (palm-up) grips are for when it's above.
- Against an opponent defending with an underhook from bottom side control, the underhook's effectiveness scales with the distance between your elbow and his — close that gap first with elbow-to-elbow control before attempting the grip.
- Entry: elbow-to-elbow → punch back to a pronated wrist grip → quick lift over the wrist → feed the hand inside.
- If the opponent leaves the hand exposed, go straight behind the back; if he locks up or tucks the hand under his thigh, use the control-and-work-through measures instead.
- Don't rely on "freebie" mistakes (e.g., posting on the knee in knee-on-belly) — treat the elbow-to-elbow entry as the reliable method against a competent opponent.
