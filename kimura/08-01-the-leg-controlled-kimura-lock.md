# The Leg-Controlled Kimura Lock

## Overview

A distinct version of the Kimura, often mislabeled a "reverse omoplata," that embodies two core principles of the whole Kimura system: aggressive use of the legs and tight control of the opponent's head. Unlike most leg-assisted Kimuras, which begin with a hand figure-four that is then transferred to the legs, this version starts and stays with the legs controlling the arm — the hands never lock a figure-four at all. This lesson covers the hold itself and its finishing mechanics; the realistic entry into the position (from turtle) is covered separately.

---

## Why this belongs to the Kimura family, not the omoplata family

- It is frequently called a reverse omoplata because of surface resemblance, but the actual breaking mechanics have far more in common with a conventional Kimura.
- Like any good Kimura, it controls the head and uses the legs as much as possible to generate breaking power.

## Setting up the leg-controlled hold

- The training partner's arm is brought into exactly the same position used for any conventional Kimura, but instead of a hand figure-four, the legs hold the arm in place.
- **Foot placement governs control**: the closer the foot sits to the training partner's elbow, the more control over the arm. If the partner extends the arm and the foot drifts away from the elbow, the arm can come free — the foot must stay cocked back toward the elbow.

## Head control

- Head control is achieved through the arm reaching across the training partner's torso. When the partner tries to sit forward, he runs directly into the back of that arm, blocking him.

## Generating the finish

- From this position, tremendous rotational pressure is available — but only if applied correctly.
- **Common mistake**: driving straight forward toward the training partner's legs. This severely limits the pressure that can be generated.
- **Correct method**: turn back toward the training partner's head — knees come down toward the mat and the turn rotates toward the head. This is where the real pressure comes from.
- Mechanically: get the hips up off the mat and drive a rotational finish back toward the opponent, turning toward his head rather than leaning forward over his legs.

## Getting into the position

- No opponent will simply lie still and let this arm position be manhandled into place — attempting to build it from a static setup is unrealistic.
- The far more common and realistic entry is from turtle position (covered in the next lesson).

---

## Key Takeaways

- This "reverse omoplata"-looking move is mechanically a Kimura: head control plus leg-driven breaking power.
- Foot must stay close to the elbow — drifting away lets the opponent extend the arm free.
- Head control comes from the arm reaching across the torso, blocking the opponent from sitting forward.
- Finish by turning back toward the opponent's head with hips lifted off the mat — driving straight forward toward the legs kills the pressure.
- This position is not built from scratch against a static opponent; the realistic entry is from turtle.
