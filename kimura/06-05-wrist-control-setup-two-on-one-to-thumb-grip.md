# Wrist Control Setup — Two-On-One To Thumb Grip

## Overview

The first proposed fix to the closed-guard kimura problem: replace the traditional momentum-based entry with a wrist-control entry. Instead of trying to beat hands, biceps, and head all at once with speed, you neutralize all three simultaneously by controlling the opponent's wrist early and pulling him off balance with your legs. The chapter also covers extracting a hidden hand and finishing with a pull-dominant kimura on the shoulder line.

---

## Why wrist control over momentum

- The traditional method asks you to beat hands, biceps, and head at once using speed — a fast opponent can always counter with small movements, no matter how quickly you move (see prior chapter).
- A much better approach: gain wrist control first. When wrist control is used to set up the kimura, using the thumb in the grip becomes genuinely important for the setup (even though the thumb isn't essential for the actual arm break — arms break with or without a thumb).

## The wrist-control entry sequence

1. Starting in closed guard with the opponent covering your biceps (dominating the inside space, making it hard to sit up).
2. Pommel one hand at a time to get inside position on his hands — this forces him off the biceps; he's much more likely to reposition onto your chest, stomach, pelvis, etc. (If you give up biceps control, he'll camp there and kimuras get hard.)
3. If he doesn't react, start bringing his hands down toward the floor, off-balancing him.
4. Manipulate his hands: take one hand with a two-on-one grip and, as you straighten your body, push that hand down his body toward your hips.
5. Once the hand reaches your hips, take a thumb grip and lock onto the wrist. A second hand landing on your bicep at this point is irrelevant — inside position can always be recovered.
6. With the wrist controlled, use your legs to pull the opponent forward while blocking his wrist against your hip, off-balancing him.
7. His only way to retain balance is to post his other (free) hand out on the floor.

## Why this beats all three traditional obstacles at once

- With his posting hand out over your shoulder (say, your left shoulder), his head ends up out of your path — it's not blocking you.
- His posting-side hand is controlled by your grip; his posting-side bicep is controlled by default, since it's connected to that same arm.
- Result: open your guard and shoot your body out to the side, come up to an elbow — nothing is in your path. Hand, bicep, and head have all been neutralized by the same wrist-control setup, without needing speed.
- As he repositions to recover, your free hand goes in and it's too late — the kimura grip is locked.

## Handling the thumb after the lock

- Once the kimura is locked, some choose to remove the thumb from the grip, others keep it in — both approaches have produced good results; personal preference.

## Extracting a hidden hand

- Once the kimura is on, the opponent will try to hide the trapped hand — under his stomach, inside his own thigh, or (inadvisably, since it makes him easy to off-balance) by grabbing his own hands together.
- Most opponents post the free hand on the floor, bring the head up, and duck the trapped hand deep into the stomach or thigh.
- Extraction method:
  1. As the opponent makes his arm strong and resistant, focus on disrupting his balance rather than fighting the hand directly.
  2. Put your feet inside his legs (grapevine position).
  3. As he holds on tight, use grapevine pressure to destabilize him.
  4. Lift your hips off the floor — this extracts the hand.

## Finishing mechanics: pull, not push

- A common mistake: applying the kimura with your head/chin in front of the opponent's elbow. From that position, any pressure degenerates into a push as his head rises and he recovers — a "forlorn," ineffective pushing kimura that lets him escape as his head continues to rise.
- Correct finishing sequence:
  1. Shimmy your hips out to the side.
  2. Watch your near leg — if the knee points up to the ceiling, the finish is compromised ("condemned to failure"); invert that knee so the sole of the foot points at the ceiling.
  3. Lock your feet together (cross the legs).
  4. Move your head away from the opponent's head.
  5. Align his far shoulder, near shoulder, and elbow onto the same **shoulder line**.
  6. Pull his elbow all the way to your armpit — this completes the shoulder line.
  7. From here, pull (don't push) with your arm — with the whole shoulder line established, a pull of less than an inch can produce a break, versus large amounts of movement producing nothing with a push.

## Key Takeaways

- Wrist control (two-on-one grip, driven down to the hip, secured with a thumb grip) neutralizes hands, biceps, and head simultaneously — the same three obstacles that defeat the traditional momentum-based setup — without relying on speed.
- Pinning the wrist against your hip and pulling with your legs off-balances the opponent, forcing his free hand to post and clearing your path to come up to an elbow and lock the kimura.
- Extraction of a hidden/trapped hand relies on off-balancing (grapevine pressure + lifting the hips), not on brute hand-fighting.
- The finish must be pull-dominant: invert the near knee (sole to the ceiling, never knee to the ceiling), cross the feet, move your head away, align both shoulders and the elbow on the shoulder line, and pull the elbow to your armpit — a pull of under an inch then produces a break.
