# Kesa Gatame (Scarf Hold) — Strengths And Limitations

## Overview

An honest assessment of kesa gatame — the traditional judo/wrestling side pin where you sit underneath the opponent's arm, controlling head and shoulders with a wide base between separated legs. Done well it's a legitimate, punishing pin; done poorly it's an invitation for the opponent to escape to your back. The chapter explains why kesa gatame is not favored as a primary pin in modern jiu-jitsu, what it's actually good for, and introduces the kimura kesa gatame variation that sets up the following chapters.

---

## What kesa gatame is

- A traditional pinning position, very common in judo and wrestling, occasionally seen in judo/jiu-jitsu.
- You are underneath your training partner's arm, controlling the head and shoulders.
- Base: two separated legs give a strong platform to control the opponent's movement.
- Done well, this can be a devastating pin that holds people for long periods — particularly strong in the gi, where you can also access the jacket.

## The real target: shoulder and elbow, not the head

- The common misconception is that kesa gatame is "some kind of crazy headlock."
- The actual focus of control is the area between the opponent's elbow and shoulder — that's what generates the pressure and the pin, not the head.
- It's usually described as a headlock, but functionally it's arm control.
- Done well and intelligently, it's a punishing hold. Done poorly — if the opponent pulls his elbow in and you lose control of the head — there is significant back exposure risk.

## Why it's not favored in modern competitive jiu-jitsu

- Some dismiss kesa gatame as "a big strong man's move" — that's called unfair; lightweights can do well with it if they understand the shoulder/elbow focus rather than fixating on the head.
- The main critique: it limits the number of high-percentage submissions available compared to other side-control pins. It works for short periods and can set up some good moves, but there are better pins to hold someone from the side long-term.
- This is reflected in the modern sport — elite jiu-jitsu athletes don't use it much.
- Kesa gatame is seen more often in arts that throw opponents from standing (judo, wrestling) — you land in it directly off a throw. Brazilian jiu-jitsu incorporates far fewer standing throws, so the position naturally comes up less.

## Two main variations

- **Traditional kesa gatame**: controls the near-side shoulder and elbow, surrounds the head.
- **Kizu (kimura) kesa gatame**: controls underneath the far shoulder instead — an underhook rather than a headlock-style wrap. This version is preferred (explicitly favored and "loved") because there is very little back exposure — the underhook is in place. The tradeoff of controlling only shoulder/elbow (in either version) is that if things go wrong, they tend to go wrong quickly.

## Why this position gets used despite its limitations

- It's not typically chosen as a primary/first-choice pin, but it comes up naturally as a reactive, transitory position: many jiu-jitsu practitioners are proficient at fighting for underhooks, and when an opponent swims through to take an underhook while being controlled from side position, catching into a kesa-gatame-style hold is an effective way to stall and control him long enough to transition elsewhere (specifically into kimura, covered in the next chapter).
- When the opponent swims through and threatens the back, the head can be surrendered without much cost — since the head was never the real point of control, giving it up loses little.

## Key Takeaways

- Kesa gatame's real control point is the shoulder/elbow area, not the head — treating it as a headlock is a beginner mistake.
- Done well it's a strong, even punishing pin; done poorly (losing elbow control) it invites back exposure and escape.
- It's used less in modern elite jiu-jitsu than in judo/wrestling because BJJ has fewer standing-throw entries and because it offers fewer high-percentage submissions than other side pins.
- Two variants: traditional (near-shoulder/head wrap) and kizu/kimura kesa gatame (far-shoulder underhook) — the underhook version is preferred for its reduced back exposure.
- Its practical value in this system is as a reactive, transitory control — used when an opponent fights for an underhook from side control, to stall him long enough to transition into kimura.
