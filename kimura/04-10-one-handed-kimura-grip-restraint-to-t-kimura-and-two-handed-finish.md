# One-Handed Kimura Grip — Restraint To T-Kimura And Two-Handed Finish

## Overview

The first full Kimura-from-half-guard technique, and it hinges on a distinction the whole chapter is built around: Kimura can function as a **restraining mechanism** as well as a **submission mechanism**. Instead of locking the conventional two-handed figure-four immediately, a one-handed Kimura grip is used to control the opponent's arm while you fight past his legs — only converting back to the two-handed figure-four once you're clear, for the actual finish.

---

## The one-handed Kimura grip

- Instead of locking a conventional figure-four with two hands, feed the opponent's hand through and take a one-handed Kimura grip — one hand goes through and holds the training partner's hand directly.
- **Critical detail: never grip the wrist for a one-handed Kimura.** If the opponent simply straightens his arm, he escapes easily from a wrist grip.
- The actual target is the **blade of the hand**. Take four fingers, no thumb, and lock them around the blade of the opponent's hand.
- When the opponent tries to straighten his arm out of this grip, it's extraordinarily difficult — you're dominating the end of the lever. Gripping the wrist instead puts you nowhere near the end of the lever, and the opponent straightens out and escapes easily.

## Setting the one-handed grip from a figure-four

1. Start with a standard figure-four grip.
2. Release and lift your palm off the opponent's hand.
3. Perform a pass-off: the controlling hand moves from over your own wrist to under your own wrist, converting to the one-handed blade grip.
4. Put your free hand on the floor and hike the opponent's captured hand toward his own armpit, bringing his elbow up to the vulnerable shoulder line.
5. With this grip in place, it becomes very difficult for the opponent to move out.

## Using the restraint to clear the legs

1. Shoot your hips out.
2. Place your free hand on the opponent's near-side knee.
3. As he locks up tight on your legs, scoot your hips out and bring your knee in as a wedge.
4. This looks like an unstable position — trust the Kimura grip to hold the opponent regardless.
5. If you're long through the body, you can sometimes get grips directly on the opponent's legs; if he extends the leg away, that may not be available, and you simply work with the knee wedge in place.
6. Post on the opponent's leg and extract your trapped leg.

## Restraint under pressure

- If the opponent tries to explode up on top of you during this extraction, simply post your arm underneath and hold on to the Kimura — his own captured arm makes it very difficult for him to complete the explosion.
- Even without leg control, if the opponent scissors his legs and comes up to base, the one-handed Kimura alone makes this very difficult. It's described as "a truly fine way to restrain his movement."

## Converting to the finish

1. Post your hand on the floor and switch your base back in toward the opponent.
2. Return to the original two-handed figure-four Kimura grip.
3. Crowd the opponent's shoulder line and step over his head.
4. Move onto the diagonal line of power.
5. Get a strong elbow-to-elbow position and apply a pull-dominant Kimura for the finish.

## Two roles of Kimura in this sequence

- The **one-handed Kimura** is used purely as a restraining mechanism — its job is to control the opponent's arm well enough that you can fight past his legs.
- Once past the legs, revert to the **traditional two-handed figure-four**, move to the diagonal line of power, and finish with a strong arm break.

## Second-angle repeat of the sequence

- Opponent has a solid grip on your leg and a strong underhook; position is initially stable.
- Go across the training partner's body, take elbow-to-elbow position, sit your hips through.
- Hold at the legs if necessary, and keep your head over the opponent's hips.
- Keep your support (base) leg well bent and on the toes — not straight and relaxed, since a straight/relaxed leg leaves you vulnerable if the opponent bridges into you.
- With this base, access the wrist and apply the initial Kimura lock.
- Repeat of the one-handed transition: from the figure-four, lift the wrist to expose the hand, pass the grip from over-wrist to under-wrist to get the one-handed blade grip (grabbing the blade, never the wrist — a wrist grip lets him straighten out and escape).
- Hike the captured hand up so the elbow reaches the shoulder line.
- Read the opponent's legs: if the knees are up high toward you, scoop underneath them; if the legs are extended away, post on the bottom knee, bring your knee in, then post and slip your leg out.
- Free hand goes to the hip or inside the leg.
- With this control, the opponent struggles to get up on top of you.
- Walk around the corner, bring the elbow back, and perform a sit-through action to expose the wrist again.
- From there: figure-four, step over the head, move to the diagonal line of power, sweep elbow to elbow into a strong finish.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Kimura can serve two different roles in the same sequence: a **restraining mechanism** (one-handed grip, controls the arm while you clear the legs) and a **submission mechanism** (two-handed figure-four, finishes on the diagonal line of power).
- One-handed Kimura grip target is always the **blade of the hand**, never the wrist — gripping the wrist lets the opponent straighten his arm and escape because you're not near the end of the lever.
- Set the one-handed grip from a figure-four via a pass-off: lift the palm off, move the controlling hand from over-wrist to under-wrist.
- Once the one-handed grip is set, hike the hand toward the armpit to bring the elbow to the shoulder line, then use hip movement, a knee wedge, and a post to extract your trapped leg — trust the grip even though the position looks unstable.
- The one-handed Kimura restrains explosive escape attempts even without leg control.
- Once past the legs, convert back to the two-handed figure-four, crowd the shoulder line, step over the head, and finish on the diagonal line of power.
- Keep the base leg bent and on the toes throughout — a straight, relaxed leg is vulnerable if the opponent bridges.
