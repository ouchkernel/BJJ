# Review Of The Kimura System's Core Theory

## Overview

A recap of the theoretical foundation laid down before moving into practical application. It restates the central bifurcation that governs every kimura decision — arm break versus restraint — and re-lists the four main methods of applying a kimura lock, plus the T-kimura subsystem for scramble situations.

---

## The core bifurcation

- The moment you lock on a kimura, the first question is which direction you're going to take it. Kimura has two faces:
  - An incredibly effective method of **breaking** someone's arm.
  - An incredibly effective method of **restraining** somebody's movement.
- Both capacities make kimura extremely valuable in a submission arsenal.
- **Rule of thumb**: if you can get the hand behind the back into a weak posterior position, go mostly toward submission holds. If you cannot get the hand behind the back — the arm stays in an anterior position — it makes more sense to switch into other submission holds or other positions.

## The four main methods of kimura

1. **Standard figure-four** — using both hands to lock up the training partner's arm.
2. **Cross kimura** — the legs get incorporated into the kimura attack.
3. **Hybrid kimura** — somewhere between kimura and omoplata; the legs replace the pushing hand on the opponent's arm, producing tremendous breaking power.
4. **Triangle/kimura hybrid** — locks the opponent's head in a way that makes the kimura an incredibly effective breaking mechanism.

Used together within the overall directionality above (break vs. restrain), these four variations give a complete way of attacking with and working the kimura lock.

## The T-kimura subsystem

- A special subsystem, of immense value in uncontrolled scramble situations.
- Lets an athlete lock an opponent down just long enough, and exert just enough control, to transition into very high-percentage finishes.
- Control is exerted using either the bottom knee or the top knee to restrict the opponent's movement.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Every kimura decision starts with one bifurcation: hand gets behind the back (weak posterior position) → go for submission; hand stays anterior → switch to other holds/positions.
- Four core methods exist: standard figure-four, cross kimura (legs involved), hybrid kimura (legs replace the pushing hand, between kimura and omoplata), and the triangle/kimura hybrid (head locked in for breaking power).
- The T-kimura position is a scramble tool — knee control (bottom or top) buys just enough time to convert a chaotic exchange into a high-percentage finish.
- This theory is the foundation for the practical application material that follows — up to this point, everything assumed you already had the kimura locked; the next step is learning how to get there.
