# Pull-Dominant Versus Push-Dominant Kimura

## Overview

A crucial notion behind how force is actually applied in a kimura: the difference between a push-dominant kimura and a pull-dominant kimura. Most people default to pushing, which produces a weak, "feeble looking" lock because it lets the opponent's shoulder — one of the most mobile joints in the body — absorb the force instead of the elbow. The fix is to reorganize the same grip around a pull through the elbow instead, using the immobilizing hand, an anchored head, and the legs.

---

## The problem with pushing

- Starting position: opponent on his side (side kimura position), kimura hold established.
- The common error: applying the kimura primarily as a pushing force.
- Pushing always results in too much movement in the opponent's flexible shoulder joint.
- The actual target of the overwhelming majority of kimura attacks is the elbow, not the shoulder — but the shoulder is still mechanically involved, and the more play there is in the shoulder, the harder it becomes to deliver a breaking force to the elbow.
- Net effect of a push-dominant kimura: the whole arm tends to move through the shoulder, producing a weak, anemic-looking lock instead of a break.

## The pull-dominant alternative

- Remember the kimura grip has a pulling hand and a pushing hand — rather than driving most of the force through a push into the opponent's wrist, focus the force as a pull through the elbow.
- Sequence:
  1. Immobilize the opponent's hand with the push hand.
  2. Sweep your elbow to his elbow.
  3. Pull through the elbow — this only works if your head moves back and away, generating the pulling action.
- Goal state: immobilize the hand, immobilize the shoulder and head, so the only moving element left is the elbow itself, which is being pulled.
- Result: a break happens in a matter of millimeters of elbow travel, as opposed to no break at all across many inches of arm movement under a push-dominant approach.

## Key principle

- Always aim to create immobility in three places — the hand, and the shoulder/head — so that the elbow is the sole moving part under load.
- Pull-dominant kimuras applied this way produce significantly more breaking success as you work down through positions on the mat.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Push-dominant kimuras waste force on shoulder movement and rarely break the arm; pull-dominant kimuras isolate force onto the elbow.
- The kimura grip has a pulling hand and a pushing hand — organize the attack around the pull, not the push.
- Immobilize the hand (push hand), sweep elbow to elbow, then pull the elbow toward you by moving your head back and away.
- The goal is total immobility everywhere except the elbow — that's what turns inches of movement with no break into millimeters of movement with a break.
