# The Case For Triangle-Assisted Kimura From Bottom

## Overview

The conceptual pivot from top-position leg-controlled Kimuras to bottom-position Kimura attacks. The central argument: Kimura's reputation as "a strong man's move" is only true when it degenerates into a pure hand-versus-hand battle. The fix, as in the top-position material, is to bring the legs into the equation — specifically, by using triangle control (not the triangle strangle itself) to immobilize the opponent's head and one arm before attacking the Kimura from bottom.

---

## The core problem with Kimura from bottom

- Traditional Kimura attacks from bottom position are usually attempted from half guard or closed guard.
- The major weakness of both: they don't do a good job of controlling the opponent's head.
- Demonstrated from closed guard: attacking the Kimura directly gives very little control of the opponent's head. As a result:
  - The head can rise and the opponent can posture up.
  - He can lock his hands together.
  - He can tuck his hand inside his own thigh (the same defensive posture seen in the top-position material).
  - As the head rises, the chances of finishing drop steadily.
  - If the opponent is smart, he brings his head over your head — at that point all the pressure shifts onto you, and the chance of finishing goes to near zero.
  - Root cause: with the legs wrapped around the opponent's waist (as in standard closed guard), the head is left completely free to roam and eventually pass over your own head, killing the lock.
- This is one of several reasons Kimura from closed guard has a low percentage success rate in top-level competition.

## The fix: triangle as a control mechanism, not a submission

- The great theme running through the Kimura system: minimizing the role of the two hands and maximizing the role of the legs. The moment legs are diminished, it becomes purely your two hands against his two hands — and that is exactly when the "strong man's move" criticism becomes valid.
- One of the best ways to bring the legs back in from bottom position: use a triangle to immobilize the opponent's head and one arm.
- Important distinction: this is not a triangle as a strangling submission. It is purely a method of controlling the head and the arm. The actual submission goal remains the Kimura.
- This creates a hybrid of triangle and Kimura: triangle for control, Kimura for the finish.

## Why it works

- Once the triangle-style control is applied, the head and one arm are directly controlled by the legs — removing the opponent's ability to posture up, bring his head over yours, or otherwise defend as he could from plain closed/half guard.
- This is described as one of the single highest-percentage ways to improve success rate with Kimura from bottom position.

## Two triangle-control options

1. **Trap triangle** — simply crossing the feet in a triangle-style position around the opponent's head and arm (mechanics detailed in the companion lesson).
2. **Hantai sankaku** (reverse/inverted triangle) — a triangle locked in what looks like the "wrong" way, used to lock up the opponent's head and arm and create tremendous pressure.
- Both are used with a lot of success; which one applies depends on the specific setup.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Kimura is only a "strong man's move" when it becomes two hands versus two hands — the fix throughout this system is always to bring the legs into the attack.
- Standard bottom-position Kimuras (half guard, closed guard) fail to control the head, which lets the opponent posture up, defend the grip, or bring his head over yours and kill the lock.
- The solution is to use a triangle purely as a head-and-arm control mechanism (not as a strangle) before attacking the Kimura — a hybrid of triangle control and Kimura finish.
- Two variants exist: the trap triangle (crossed feet) and the hantai sankaku (inverted/reverse triangle lock) — both control the head and arm effectively and are both used successfully.
