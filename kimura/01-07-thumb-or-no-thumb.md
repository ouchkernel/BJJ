# Thumb Or No Thumb

## Overview

A direct answer to one of the most commonly asked questions about kimura grip: should you lock your thumb over the top of your opponent's wrist, or take a thumbless grip? Two camps exist, each with vocal supporters. The conclusion is that thumb placement is not what actually breaks the arm — but the question does matter later, when it comes to entering and setting up the kimura rather than finishing it.

---

## The two schools of thought

- **Thumb grip**: lock the thumb over the top of the wrist when you close the hold.
- **Thumbless grip**: grip without wrapping the thumb over.
- Both camps argue their version is "the right way," and the debate tends to be argued with strong conviction on both sides.

## The theory behind using the thumb

- If you had the requisite strength to turn your opponent's forearm using your thumb, it would add value.
- Turning the forearm creates tension the opponent feels immediately, and since kimura is a twisting lock, adding a turn should theoretically add breaking power.
- Turning the wrist also drives the opponent's elbow forward, which facilitates the break.

## Why it falls apart against resistance

- The theory holds up against a relaxed training partner who lets his wrist turn.
- Against a strong, resistant arm, attempting to turn the wrist with the thumb almost always results in your own hand simply rotating around his wrist — his wrist doesn't move, and you don't get the intended turning effect.

## The evidence against thumb position mattering for breaking power

- Countless documented arm breaks exist with both thumb grips and thumbless grips — both are seen regularly in competition footage, in roughly even numbers.
- That even split is compelling evidence that the thumb is not what does the breaking.
- Some kimuras (covered elsewhere in the series) use a leg position instead of a hand on the wrist at all — no hand on the wrist means no possible wrist twist whatsoever, and yet these variations still produce devastating breaks. This proves the twisting effect on the wrist is not what supplies the kimura's actual breaking power.

## Practical guidance

- **Don't obsess over thumb or no thumb** — for breaking power specifically, it comes down to personal preference. Both approaches have produced fully effective breaks.
- General personal rule of thumb for grip selection: against smaller wrists, use the thumb; against much heavier, larger-wristed opponents, go without the thumb.
- Leg-based kimura variants involve no thumb at all and can still break an opponent easily.
- Important caveat: thumb vs. no thumb becomes genuinely important when it comes to **entering** the kimura (the setup phase), not the breaking mechanics — that gets significant attention later in the series.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Thumb placement does not meaningfully affect breaking power — arms have been broken just as effectively with thumb grips and thumbless grips.
- Trying to twist the wrist with the thumb only works on a relaxed opponent; a resistant arm just lets your thumb spin around without turning the wrist.
- Leg-based kimuras with no hand on the wrist at all (and therefore zero wrist twist) can still produce devastating breaks — proof the twist isn't the source of breaking power.
- Rule of thumb: use the thumb against smaller wrists, skip it against much larger/heavier wrists — but this is preference, not a mechanical requirement.
- Thumb vs. no thumb does matter for entering and setting up the kimura, even though it doesn't matter for the finish itself.
