# Trap Triangle Kimura: Setup And Finish

## Overview

The detailed mechanics of the trap triangle — two crossed feet locked around the opponent's head and one arm, used purely as a control mechanism to set up a high-percentage Kimura from bottom position. Shown from seated butterfly guard, a favorite general position for triangle-style attacks. Covers the initial wrist grip, closing the trap triangle, breaking the opponent's defensive arm to the outside, and the final knee-inversion detail that locks the Kimura itself.

---

## Starting grip: the pronated wrist control

1. From a seated butterfly guard, take the opponent's hand to the center line.
2. Pass the hand to a **pronated grip** — palm facing away from you.
3. Bring your hand up to the elbow and take the opponent's hand across to your hip.
4. This closes everything around the opponent's arm: even if he posts his free hand on your shoulder and pushes with all his strength, the grip is exceptionally difficult to break.
- The grip's strength is mechanical, not muscular: locking everything around the arm covers the one zone of weakness (against your hip), closing a full circuit around the wrist.

## Closing the trap triangle

1. As the opponent yanks away from the wrist grip, place your foot on his hip.
2. Bring the second leg out and lock it around the opponent's head.
3. At this stage it is not yet a true triangle — the legs haven't figured forward. This crossed-ankle position (like a closed guard, but locked around the head and one arm instead of the waist) is the **trap triangle**.
4. Result: the trapping effect of a triangle, without the strangulation.

## Why the opponent struggles against it

- When the opponent yanks away, the knees pulling into your chest make it difficult for him to posture up using the usual postural triangle escape.
- From the opponent's perspective, moving his arm across his body sets him up to be strangled (even though this isn't a real triangle submission) — so most opponents instinctively try to bring the arm in that direction while raising the head.
- It doesn't matter much whether his arm ends up moved across or stays in the middle — what matters is bringing his head down and forcing his hand outward.

## Breaking the arm to the outside

1. Get a two-on-one grip on the opponent's wrist.
2. Even if he makes the arm strong and resistant, use your hips as a lever to work his hand to the outside.
3. Once there, slide your thumb underneath his wrist.

## Locking down the head and finishing the Kimura

1. The most important detail: control the opponent's head — turn off his shoulder.
2. Invert your knee so the foot points up at the ceiling (not down toward the floor) — this turns off his shoulders and hips.
3. Lock the feet over on the other side; with this done, it becomes very difficult for the opponent to posture up.
4. From here it is relatively simple to lock a clean Kimura: you are lined up right on the opponent's power line, his two shoulders are in a straight line, and his elbow comes up to your armpit — attempting to straighten the arm out becomes extremely difficult.
5. Employ a pulling effect to finish the lock.
- The key mechanical element throughout is the inverted knee (foot to the ceiling, not the floor) — shown from the opposite side in the instructional for clarity.

## Full sequence, repeated for clarity

1. Seated butterfly guard against the opponent.
2. Take the hand to the center line, then to a pronated grip.
3. Connect everything to the hip — very difficult for the opponent to yank away.
4. Foot to the hip, second leg locks around the head — trap triangle formed.
5. Two-on-one the wrist, hips as a lever to bring the hand outside, thumb under the wrist.
6. Invert the knee (foot to ceiling), lock feet on the other side, turn off the shoulders/hips.
7. Line up on the power line, elbow to armpit, pull to finish.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The trap triangle is just crossed ankles locked around the opponent's head and one arm (like a closed guard, but relocated) — a control tool, not a submission in itself.
- The initial wrist control (center line → pronated grip → hand to hip) closes a mechanically strong circuit around the arm before the trap triangle is even built.
- Opponents instinctively defend by trying to move the arm across the body and raise the head, mistaking it for an actual triangle strangle threat — this reaction is expected, not a problem.
- Break the arm to the outside using a two-on-one grip and the hips as a lever, then thumb under the wrist.
- The single most important finishing detail is the inverted knee — foot pointed at the ceiling, not the floor — which turns off the opponent's shoulders and hips and makes posturing up nearly impossible.
- Once locked in, the setup lines the attacker up perfectly on the opponent's power line for a clean, high-percentage Kimura finish.
