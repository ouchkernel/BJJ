# Core Principles: Power From Position And The Gateway Principle

## Overview

The final two core principles of the kimura system, followed by a recap that ties the whole opening chapter together into one roadmap. Power from position explains where kimura's actual breaking power comes from (position, not muscle), and the gateway principle reframes kimura as a hub move that opens into a wide range of other positions and submissions rather than a single dead-end lock.

---

## The power from position principle

- Refers to two power lines used constantly, from both top and bottom position:
  - **Top position power line** — a line running from the opponent's shoulder to his opposite hip.
  - **Bottom position power line** — a line running along the opponent's shoulders, through his elbow, and to your own head.
- Achieving these two power lines (top and bottom) dramatically increases the amount of power you can bring to bear on the kimura lock.
- Core mindset: don't think of kimura as a power move in itself. Think of it as a move that becomes extraordinarily powerful once you achieve the right position. It's the position that gives you power — rely on body positioning, not muscular strength.

## The gateway principle

- Kimura is far more than a submission hold on its own — it's a gateway into many other positions and many other submissions.
- General jiu-jitsu principle underlying this: the value of a given move isn't just the move itself, but what it offers in terms of alternatives — what you can go to if the move fails or meets strong resistance.
- Kimura offers an extraordinary number of alternative attacking methods if the initial figure four is stymied by resistance — many other submissions and many other positions to switch to.

## Chapter recap — the system roadmap

- Start with a **multiplicity** of kimuras — seven in this system.
- Foundation: the **figure four kimura**.
- Central problem: can the hand go behind the back or not?
  - If yes → go directly into submission holds, establishing the appropriate power line (top or bottom).
  - If not quickly → first alternative is bringing in the **legs** for extra power (cross kimura, kimura plata, etc.).
  - Still can't get it behind → switch completely to a different submission (armbar, triangle) or a different position — usually the back, since it's the favored position overall, most often via the **T-kimura position** or the **head and arm kimura**.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Kimura's breaking power comes from position, not muscle — establish the top power line (shoulder to opposite hip) or bottom power line (opponent's shoulders through his elbow to your head), and the lock becomes dramatically more effective without extra strength.
- Gateway principle: judge kimura not just as a submission but by what it opens up — the alternative submissions and positions available when the initial attempt meets resistance.
- Full system roadmap: multiplicity of kimuras → figure four foundation → central problem (hand behind back?) → direct submission with power line, or legs, or switch submission/position (favoring the back via T-kimura or head-and-arm kimura).
