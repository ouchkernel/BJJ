# Why Kimura Vs Single Leg Is Trained Constantly (Drill Rationale)

## Overview

Before teaching the drill itself, the rationale for treating the Kimura-vs-single-leg entry as a constantly-drilled staple is laid out. Single-leg entries are one of the most common situations that occur in any standard grappling match, they tend to happen fast, and the Kimura is an unusually simple and effective way to neutralize them and land in a dominant attacking position — which is exactly why it deserves constant repetition rather than occasional practice.

---

## Why this situation matters so much

- The Kimura down on the mat versus the single leg is described as one of the most common applications of Kimura used on the floor.
- In any standard grappling match, opponents coming up into a single-leg situation are almost always hunting an angle — either to get the second leg and complete a double leg, or to circle around behind toward the waist and ultimately the back.
- The Kimura is an incredibly effective and relatively simple way of countering that game, neutralizing the angle attempt and converting it into a good attacking position: the T-Kimura, from which the attack sequence begins.

## Why it has to be drilled constantly

- This is described as probably the most common manifestation of Kimura that the instructor's students actually use in live rolling and competition.
- Because of that frequency, it's very important to drill the general motion constantly — repetition is what makes the response automatic rather than something you have to think through under pressure.
- The reason the response has to be automatic: people coming up from bottom into a single leg are almost always doing it quickly. No one shoots a single leg in slow motion.
- It's a dynamic movement — the opponent gets a hold of the leg, quickly moves behind, and works in. The defender therefore can't afford to move at a slow speed.
- The standard isn't "be the fastest person alive" — but realistically, in a live exchange where the opponent is actively trying to put you down, you can't hang around either. The movement into the Kimura frame needs to be smooth, efficient, and available on fairly short notice.
- This sets up the purpose-built drill (covered next) designed specifically to make these movements natural and reflexive.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Single-leg entries are one of the most frequent live situations in grappling, and the Kimura counter is simple and highly effective — that combination is why it's worth constant drilling rather than occasional practice.
- Opponents shooting single legs move quickly and dynamically, never in slow motion — so the defensive entry into the Kimura frame has to be smooth and efficient, not necessarily blazing fast, but never slow either.
- The goal of constant drilling is to make the entry automatic under the time pressure of a live, dynamic shot.
- This lesson is the "why" that sets up the dedicated entry drill taught next.
