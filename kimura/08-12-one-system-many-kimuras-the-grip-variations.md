# One System, Many Kimuras: The Grip Variations

## Overview

A survey of Kimura grip variations, framed by a general principle about why any finishing hold — heel hook, armbar, or Kimura — needs multiple variants rather than one fixed version. Three grip variants stemming from the basic figure-four Kimura are shown here: the classic figure-four, the bicep figure-four, and the cross Kimura (legs reinforcing the grip).

---

## Why Have Multiple Variants of the Same Hold

- A recurring principle: for any given finishing hold, students should have alternatives within that hold, not just one version.
- Example already seen in the leg system: students don't rely on a single heel hook — they use a multiplicity of heel hooks, visible in any highlight reel.
- Reason: in any lock (heel hook, armbar, Kimura, etc.), small contingencies come up while trying to apply it that affect which variation works.
- A student with a multiplicity of variants within one system can cover far more of those contingencies than someone who only knows one version.
- Applying this to the Kimura: there are five variations that stem from the basic figure-four Kimura, plus a sixth that's unique because it does *not* stem from a figure-four Kimura at all (arguably closer to an omoplata, though Danaher considers it to have more in common with a Kimura).

## Variation 1: The Classic Figure-Four Kimura

- "The father of them all" — the Kimura everyone already knows.
- Works regardless of the starting position, and regardless of whether you're attacking from top or bottom.
- Grip structure: a push hand at the opponent's wrist, a pull hand at your own wrist.

## Variation 2: The Bicep Figure-Four

- Still a figure-four grip, but the hand placement changes entirely.
- Instead of a push hand on the wrist and a pull hand on your own wrist, one hand goes to the training partner's shoulder and the other goes to your own shoulder.
- Still produces a genuine Kimura action on the trapped arm, but the breaking pressure is generated differently because the grip itself is significantly different.

## Variation 3: The Cross Kimura

- Built around a core system principle: employ the legs whenever you can't get the hand quickly and efficiently behind the opponent's back.
- From a conventional figure-four Kimura hand grip, bring both legs across the training partner's body.
- Feet can be locked or unlocked around each other — it doesn't matter.
- With two legs across the body reinforcing the grip, this produces very strong breaks.

---

## Key Takeaways

- General principle: any finishing hold benefits from having multiple variants, because small contingencies during application favor different versions — this is why students shouldn't rely on a single fixed grip.
- The Kimura family in this system has five variants stemming from the basic figure-four grip, plus a sixth (Kimura-plata-type) variant that doesn't stem from the figure-four at all.
- Classic figure-four Kimura: push hand at the wrist, pull hand at your own wrist — works from any position, top or bottom.
- Bicep figure-four: hands go to the shoulders (yours and his) instead of the wrist — same Kimura action, different pressure mechanics.
- Cross Kimura: reinforce a conventional figure-four grip by bringing both legs across the opponent's body — used when the hands alone can't get behind the back quickly enough.
