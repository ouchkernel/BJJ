# Cross Kimura

## Overview

The first of three methods for incorporating the legs into a dorsal kimura attack, solving the classic problem of an opponent who locks his own hands together to prevent you getting his hand behind his back. Instead of fighting arm-versus-arm — the source of kimura's old reputation as "a strong man's move" — the cross kimura throws a leg across the opponent's body so the legs, not the arms, do the separating and breaking work.

---

## The problem it solves

- Starting from a dorsal position, the opponent has his hands locked together, making it impossible to get the hand behind the back through hand-fighting alone.
- Turning this into a pure arms-versus-arms battle is exactly what gives kimura its reputation as requiring superior strength. Incorporating the legs removes that strength dependency and lets smaller, weaker grapplers compete effectively.

## Entry mechanics

1. With the opponent's hands locked, post a leg on the training partner.
2. Throw the foot from one side of his body across to the other, taking care to bring the leg over the top of your own hand (not underneath it).
3. Sit down into the finished position: two of your legs go across the opponent's body — the cross kimura — a shape that superficially resembles an armbar (juji gatame).

## Finishing mechanics

- As a general rule, cross your feet once in position.
- If the opponent stays still, it's easy to use the leg to add pressure that reinforces the hand, producing strong breaks.
- Most opponents will try to come up to their knees to find a stronger leverage position. Response: don't be intimidated — keep the feet crossed, keep his hand pinned inside your own thigh, and continue using the leg to reinforce the kimura for a strong break regardless.
- If the opponent rolls over, simply follow the roll. You end up in a position where the same lock can be applied for the finish (other options off this roll are covered later in the course).

## Alternate entry (popping up)

- From the same locked-hands starting point, pop up and take the leg right over the top, hiding your own hands inside the crook of your own knee.
- From here, sit down and fall across the training partner's body — this creates tremendous mechanical pressure on the trapped arm.
- If the opponent elects to roll out, follow him and stay in the same position; the same pulling finish is available from there as well.

## Why this matters

- The cross kimura is the first method for directly incorporating the legs into the kimura finish, greatly strengthening finishing potential beyond a pure hand-fight.
- It converts the contest from arms-versus-arms into legs-versus-arm, removing the strength parity problem that gives kimura its "strong man's move" reputation.
- It sets up a family of leg-incorporated kimura variations (a second variation, the kimura plata, follows directly from this same starting position).

---

## Key Takeaways

- Use cross kimura when the opponent locks his own hands together and you can't get the hand behind his back through hand-fighting alone.
- Post a leg, throw the foot across the opponent's body over the top of your own hand, and sit into the cross-legged position.
- Cross your feet, keep his trapped hand pinned inside your thigh, and use leg pressure — not arm strength — to reinforce the break.
- If the opponent tries to come up to his knees or rolls over, stay calm and follow him; the same lock still finishes.
- The core value of cross kimura is turning an arms-versus-arms strength contest into a legs-versus-arm mechanical advantage.
