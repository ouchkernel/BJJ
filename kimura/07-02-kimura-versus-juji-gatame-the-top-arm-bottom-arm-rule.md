# Kimura Versus Juji-Gatame — The Top Arm / Bottom Arm Rule

## Overview

This lesson names and solves the specific risk repeatedly flagged in the kimura-from-half-guard-bottom series: once the opponent frees his leg from your half guard, he can circle around your head and counter your kimura attempt with a juji-gatame (armbar). The chapter breaks down exactly why this confrontation happens, why the hand's position (in front of the body vs. behind the back) decides who wins it, and teaches a top-arm/bottom-arm switching mechanic that flips the leverage back in your favor.

---

## The essential problem

- If you can get the opponent's hand to a **posterior** position (behind his back), there is no armbar available to him — no matter how flexible he is or how he tries to move around your legs.
- The danger only exists in **anterior** situations — where the hand stays in front of the body, safe and strong for the opponent.
- Once your half guard leg control is lost (either because you release it or he extricates it), you're left holding a kimura on bottom while the opponent now has mobility to move around.
- When you hold the kimura, you're lying on your side — meaning you have a **top arm** and a **bottom arm**. The opponent wants to step his leg around your head and attack your top arm specifically.
- As his leg goes over your head, a simple turn from east to west drops him straight into a position where your kimura grip is now trapped inside his juji-gatame. Both people technically have "a lock" — but yours is nearly worthless and his is fight-ending. If you try to hold onto the kimura here, he easily separates your hands and finishes the armbar.

## The fix: convert top arm to bottom arm

- The rule: as the opponent steps around your head, you must convert your **top arm into a bottom arm** before he completes the turn.
- Mechanic (solo breakdown): facing one direction (e.g., east) with one arm on top; plant your foot on the floor and switch your legs; this flips you to face the opposite direction (west) with the opposite arm now on top.
- This single action — starting face-one-way/arm-on-top, ending face-the-other-way/opposite-arm-on-top — is what generates enough leverage that even as the opponent circles around the arm, you can drop him into a winning position instead of losing the armbar exchange.

## Full sequence

1. Half guard bottom, get hold of the opponent's arm, lock a tight, secure kimura.
2. Opponent starts to dance/step past your legs.
3. If you can extract the hand behind the back at this point, none of the rest matters — just roll him over.
4. If not, as he turns around your head: plant your foot on the floor, and as he crosses over your body, apply a pushing force on the far side and drop his shoulders down to the mat — this is the top-arm-to-bottom-arm switch in live action.
5. If you stay static here, the opponent wins the exchange most of the time — so the switch must be active, not passive.
6. Take your foot up; as he steps over the body, turn in the opposite direction. The push on his hands combined with a pull from your other arm sets his own momentum up to roll him through and down to his back.
7. Once his back is scored, come up on top of him, get your elbow down to the floor.
8. Surround his head, step across it onto the power line, and finish with any of the standard breaking finishes.

## Why the hand position is everything

- If you get the hand behind the back, this entire confrontation never arises — you simply lever the opponent over, and he never gets a juji-gatame opportunity because his hand is already posterior.
- What makes the opponent's counter possible at all is that his hand stays anterior (in front of the body). As long as that's true and he passes your legs, there's a genuine threat — and it will always target your top arm.
- Response: as he comes around, hide the top arm by converting it to the bottom arm and get the elbow down to the floor on that side. From there it's a simple scissor-and-come-up-on-top action.

## Timing

- This exchange is time-constrained. Late hand placement or a late transition to top loses the position entirely.
- As the opponent moves rapidly around your head, you must at least match his pace to put him down.
- Once down, he can't turn away from you (the kimura prevents it), but he can come back up into a T-kimura scramble — handled the same way as covered earlier in the course.
- To transition to top efficiently: switch the legs, come up into a high tripod, step over his head, place the elbow down on the mat, then sweep elbow-to-elbow and pull into the finishing position. The high tripod is what lets you cover both sides of his body quickly enough to beat his movement.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Whether the opponent's trapped hand is anterior (in front) or posterior (behind the back) determines everything: posterior = no armbar is possible, period; anterior = a live juji-gatame threat exists as soon as he passes your legs.
- The threat always lands on your **top arm** — the arm on top when you're lying on your side holding the kimura.
- The fix is an active conversion of top arm to bottom arm as the opponent circles your head, executed by planting a foot and switching your legs to flip which side is uppermost.
- Staying static in this exchange loses to the opponent most of the time — you must match his pace and actively push/pull to roll him through on his own momentum.
- Getting to top afterward should use a high tripod (switch legs, step over the head, elbow to the mat) to cover both sides of his body quickly and land on the power line.
