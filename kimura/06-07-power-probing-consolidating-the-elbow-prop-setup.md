# Power Probing — Consolidating The Elbow-Prop Setup

## Overview

A further refinement of the closed guard kimura fix, introducing "power probing": actively forcing the opponent's hand down to the mat by any available means, then using the *pushing* hand (not the traditional pulling/framing hand) as the first point of entry. This is contrasted directly with the classic "jiu-jitsu 101" setup, and the chapter builds out the full sequence — force the hand down, grip the wrist, prop up on the elbow, secure the figure-four, invert the knee, and finish on the shoulder line.

---

## Restating the problem one more time

- Kimura works fine everywhere else; only the closed guard setup is a historical failure — statistically abysmal compared to every other beginner-level move.
- The traditional setups are "unrealistic" in that they force you to fight three separate live opponents to your entry: his hands, his biceps, and his head — each capable of stopping you independently.
- The proposed fix for making closed guard kimura "great again": wrist control combined with **power probing**.

## The classic (failing) method, restated for contrast

1. Bring hands to inside position, get his hands to the mat.
2. Use momentum to come up with the framing/pulling arm first (no wrist control) — this arm "penetrates around" but never controls the wrist.
3. Failure points:
   - No control of his wrist means he can easily lock his hands, put his biceps to your chest, and return you flat to the mat, crushing the kimura.
   - Against a fast opponent, even before that, he can simply put a hand on your chest and stop you completely.
   - Even if his hands do reach the floor, nothing is holding them there — he can post a hand on your chest and throw you back down at will.
   - Nothing has moved his head, so it stays directly in your path and stops you when you try to sit up.
- Conclusion: hands, biceps, and head are all completely unimpeded in the traditional method — nothing has been done to neutralize any of them.

## The power-probing fix: pushing hand first

- Core change: instead of leading the entry with the pulling/framing hand and using momentum, lead with the **pushing hand** and get it onto the opponent's wrist.
- Getting the pushing hand onto the wrist: force the opponent's hands down toward the mat. There are many ways to do this:
  - Steal inside position, then bump forward with the legs and bump the hand out/down to the mat.
  - If he starts with hands on your biceps, work into a collar tie, take inside position with your hand, and pull the hand down to the floor.
  - "A thousand" other small variations exist — the specific method of getting the hand down doesn't matter as much as the fact that it happens.
- Once the wrist is available (it's "not a difficult thing to do"), grip it.

## Full sequence from wrist grip to lock

1. With the wrist gripped, come up to an elbow (propped position). Because you're propped on the elbow, if he pushes to put you back down, it's much harder for him to succeed than in the traditional setup.
2. Keep everything in front of you and go around the opponent's body into the kimura grip.
3. As he tries to recover by bringing his head back up, that's the moment to act: shoot the hips out to the side, invert the far knee, and come up to the shoulder line.
4. With both shoulders and the elbow aligned with your head on the shoulder line, even the smallest pull finishes the kimura.

## Elbow positioning detail

- When coming up to the propped elbow, the elbow must stay **behind** your head.
- If your head is in front of/ahead of the elbow and the opponent pushes with his shoulder, you simply fall back down.
- Correct structure: elbow behind the head, giving you a stable prop that resists his push long enough to secure the figure-four grip.

## Putting it together: the general method

1. Generate enough space in the closed guard exchange that at some point you can get a hand down to the floor and grip the wrist.
2. Come up to the propped elbow (elbow behind the head) even as he fights to keep you down — the prop buys enough time to get the figure-four grip.
3. Once the figure-four is secured, get the far-side knee inverted.
4. Position the feet so you can lift your hips off the floor and bring everything up to the shoulder line.
5. From there, strong breaks follow.

## Why this method should change the numbers

- This is explicitly framed as the method that should make closed guard kimura viable in real competition, where the traditional setup has been "a conspicuous failure."
- The key structural change: using the pushing hand against the opponent's far hand (rather than your framing/pulling arm fighting his near elbow) is what makes the setup actually work under resistance — this, combined with wrist control, thumb grip, and the elbow prop, is what's expected to get past the three traditional stoppers (head, biceps, hands).
- The few times closed guard kimura *has* succeeded historically at a high level, it has almost always come from some version of the hand being controlled at the wrist, allowing the attacker to come up to an elbow — validating this approach as the mechanism behind the rare historical successes, now made systematic and repeatable.

## Key Takeaways

- "Power probing" means actively forcing the opponent's hand down to the mat by any available method, then entering with the pushing hand onto his wrist — not the traditional pulling-arm-first, momentum-based entry.
- The traditional method leaves hands, biceps, and head completely unimpeded; power probing plus wrist control neutralizes all three by controlling the wrist early and propping on the elbow.
- Elbow must stay behind the head when propping — elbow in front of the head collapses under a shoulder push.
- Full chain: force hand down → grip wrist → prop on elbow (elbow behind head) → secure figure-four → invert far knee → hips off the floor → align the shoulder line → finish with a pull.
- The rare historical successes of closed guard kimura at elite level have reportedly always involved wrist control enabling an elbow prop — this chapter turns that occasional accident into a deliberate, repeatable system.
