# Turtle Position Kimura — Identifying The Roll & Exposing The Elbow

## Overview

This lesson introduces the third great bottom position for kimura attacks — after half guard and closed guard — bottom turtle position. It covers the foundational skill needed before any finish is possible: reading which grip the opponent has taken from behind, identifying an attackable arm, exposing the elbow, and executing the first safe roll to a strong, high position across the opponent's body. No kimura is actually locked and finished in this lesson — it is entirely about winning the positional exchange that makes the kimura possible.

---

## Historical note

- Kazushi Sakuraba was famous for using kimura from bottom turtle position. Karo Parisyan was also highly effective with these attacks in mixed martial arts.
- There is always some degree of risk attacking from any bottom position, but with the right knowledge, kimura can be used very effectively from bottom turtle.

## Reading the opponent's grip — first proviso

- If the opponent has a **seat belt grip** (over one arm, under the other), this is **not** the time for kimura — it's a low-percentage attack from that grip.
- If the opponent has a **body lock** (both hands underneath both of your arms), this is where kimura attacks become viable — both the inside arm and the outside arm offer good, high-percentage attacks.
- If the opponent substitutes a body lock for a seat belt mid-scramble, that's a signal things are getting risky — not a smart time to attack kimura; better options exist.
- Bottom line: only commit to kimura attacks when you've confirmed a body lock, not a seat belt.

## Inside arm vs. outside arm

- **Inside arm**: the arm between the two bodies, in the space that separates you.
- **Outside arm**: the arm with nothing between you and the opponent.
- Which one to attack largely depends on how you move around the opponent's body. Personal preference leans toward inside arm attacks.

## Exposing the elbow

- Start moving rather than staying static — the higher your head comes up and the more movement there is, the more an elbow gets exposed.
- The mechanic for exposing the elbow is very similar to exposing a heel for a heel hook: use the blade of the wrist to draw the elbow forward.
- Throughout this process, keep controlling the opponent's wrist — this makes it harder for him to switch to a seat belt, and creates elbow exposure in the process.
- Caution: if you lock the kimura and the opponent throws your head forward, you can face-plant — unpleasant but not dangerous. To avoid it, lock the kimura **one-handed first** until the position favors you:
  1. Monitor the wrist as the opponent moves.
  2. Lock the (kimura) hand in.
  3. As he throws your body weight forward, use your free hand as a base of support.
  4. Pitch by opening the leg up, rolling the opponent over — landing in good position across his body.

## The first skill: rolling the opponent on the inside arm

1. Opponent has a body lock (not a seat belt) — confirmed safe to work.
2. Monitor the inside wrist as you move.
3. Come up, expose the arm at the elbow, use your second hand as a base of support.
4. Trying to throw from a completely static position is very difficult if the opponent makes a strong base.
5. Instead of throwing him toward his own base of support, take him **forward**, toward where he has little to no base.
6. Use the legs as a pendulum to scissor across his body.
7. Don't sit straight down to the floor initially — he'll come up quickly and you'll end up back on bottom. Stay high, keep the head high.

## Full recap

- From bottom position: get the grip, open the body up. The opponent's strong base doesn't matter — throw forward anyway.
- Stay high over his body as you go over, so you can post and lock up with your head kept up.
- If you lie down or slouch instead, he comes up on top and you're left with a lot of hard work still to do.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Grip-reading comes first: attack kimura only against a body lock, never against a seat belt — and treat a mid-scramble switch to body lock as a warning sign, not an opportunity.
- Identify inside arm (between bodies) vs. outside arm (nothing between) before deciding which side to attack.
- Exposing the elbow uses the same wrist-blade mechanic as exposing a heel for a heel hook.
- Lock the kimura one-handed first, using the free hand as a base of support, to avoid getting your head driven into the mat when the opponent throws his weight forward.
- Roll the opponent forward — toward his weak base, not his strong one — using the legs as a pendulum.
- Stay high and keep the head up throughout the roll; sitting down early gives the opponent a fast way back on top.
