# Hand And Elbow Placement: Lever And Fulcrum

## Overview

The nuts-and-bolts starting point for side kimura: precise hand and elbow placement. Kimura is presented as one of the clearest demonstrations of lever-and-fulcrum mechanics in all of grappling, and getting this placement right is what lets a much weaker person generate serious breaking force. The car analogy sets the frame — raw power (from the legs and hips) is worthless if it isn't transmitted efficiently through the hands and arms, just as horsepower is worthless without a working transmission.

---

## The lever

- The lever runs from the point of the training partner's elbow all the way to your own smallest (pinky) knuckle.
- **Grabbing the wrist** takes the short end of the lever — sub-optimal, requires more force for the same result.
- **Grabbing all the way at the end** (over the hand/fingers) is also sub-optimal — you're gripping over a flexing joint (the wrist itself), which diminishes power transmission.
- **The ideal compromise**: hook your middle finger around the bend of the training partner's wrist. This gets a big bite on the end of the lever while holding something fixed (the wrist) instead of the moving hand. This placement is the same regardless of whether you use a thumb grip or a thumbless grip.

## Second hand (pull hand) placement

- Common mistake: grabbing your own hand/wrist anywhere convenient. This doesn't cut it — the pull hand also needs to work at the end of the lever.
- Correct method: grab your own hand covering your own knuckles, bringing your wrist all the way up so there is **no daylight** between your wrist and your opponent's wrist.
- Then hook your middle finger over your own wrist for a tight coverage.
- The more daylight visible between your wrist and the opponent's wrist, the more danger there is of the opponent pulling his hand back toward his own body and slipping out — and the less fixity/tightness the lock has going into the finish.

## Elbow placement

- Bring your own elbow all the way to the training partner's elbow — "sweep elbow to elbow."
- The closer your elbow is to the opponent's armpit (i.e., the farther from his elbow), the less lever force you can apply.
- The farther your elbow is from his elbow, the more the kimura degenerates into a push with your wrist-side hand instead of what it should be: a **pull** with your other arm.

## Pull-dominant kimura (first introduction)

- As a general rule, kimuras should be **pull dominant** — primary force applied through the pull, not the push.
- This concept is introduced here through hand placement and will be revisited in much greater detail later in the series.

## Summary sequence

1. Middle finger hooks over the fold of the training partner's wrist (works with thumb or thumbless).
2. Second middle finger covers your own wrist — no daylight between the two wrists.
3. Sweep elbow to elbow — never let your elbow drift toward his armpit or away from his elbow.
4. Result: a strong breaking position regardless of whether the arm ends up bent more or less than 90 degrees.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Kimura is one of the clearest demonstrations of lever-and-fulcrum mechanics in grappling — hand and elbow placement is what transmits your body's power to the opponent's arm.
- The lever runs from the opponent's elbow to your own smallest knuckle; gripping the wrist wastes lever length, gripping the hand crosses a flexing joint — the optimal grip is the middle finger hooked over the wrist fold.
- No daylight between your wrist and the opponent's wrist on the pull-hand side — daylight lets him slip his hand free and loosens the whole lock.
- Elbow to elbow, always — drifting toward his armpit or away from his elbow turns the kimura into a weak push instead of a strong pull.
- The system favors a pull-dominant kimura: the primary breaking force should come from the pull arm, not the push/stopper hand.
