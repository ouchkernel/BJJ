# Basement Training — One-Handed Kimura When The Power Line Is Blocked

## Overview

Basement footage of two students, Nicky Ryan and Ethan Crowlinson, drilling a one-handed kimura variation. The conventional two-hand kimura relies on getting to the "power line" — when an opponent successfully denies that line by tucking the threatened arm against his hip, the answer is to abandon the two-hand grip and switch to a one-handed kimura, gripping the blade of the hand and rotating the body through a high tripod position for the finish.

---

## The Conventional Kimura And The Power Line

- Nicky Ryan demonstrates a fairly conventional kimura, getting good access to the power line.
- Mechanics emphasized: the head moves away in an arc, generating tremendous pressure.
- Elbow-to-elbow contact is emphasized specifically because it maximizes the use of lever and fulcrum.
- As the head moves away and the legs drive, the kimura becomes very strong.
- The power line is named as the basic element underlying the conventional kimura.

## When The Power Line Gets Blocked

- The opponent can deny the power line by switching his arm position — specifically, hugging the threatened arm against his own hip.
- When that happens, the immediate answer is to switch to a **one-handed kimura**.
- Grip detail: grip down on the **blade of the opponent's hand**, not the wrist.
- The opponent may try to block with his free arm, but that block is largely ignored.
- Mechanics: rotate around into a **high tripod position**, then rotate the entire body — the head turns into the hips while the free hand blocks the opponent's hip. This combination produces a very strong one-handed kimura effect.

## Second Example: Ethan Crowlinson

- Ethan works from a situation where he's initially unable to hit the conventional kimura, using elbow position and a wedge.
- He improves his position with a confident step across the opponent's body, which flattens the opponent to the correct degree.
- His elbows wedge down by the opponent's hip; going elbow-to-elbow maximizes lever and fulcrum, and the head begins moving away — producing a strong pulling action and a clean conventional finish in this first instance.
- In a second rep, the opponent blocks the hips instead, which makes the conventional kimura method difficult. This is exactly the trigger to switch to the one-handed version: block the opponent's hips with one hand, control the blade of his hand with the other, move into a high tripod position, then rotate for a strong one-handed finish.

## Finishing Mechanics

- This one-handed approach is useful any time a satisfactory position on the opponent's power line can't be achieved.
- Take the one-handed grip on the blade of the hand.
- When the opponent uses his free arm to block, counter by blocking his hip in return.
- Tripod up, then rotate into the finish — the head moves almost across the opponent's stomach while the legs walk around the head, creating tremendous breaking pressure.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The one-handed kimura is the answer specifically to an opponent who successfully denies the power line (e.g., by hugging the threatened arm against his hip).
- Grip the blade of the hand, not the wrist, for the one-handed version.
- The finishing mechanism is rotational: high tripod position, body rotation with the head turning into the hips, and the free hand blocking the opponent's hip.
- The opponent's attempt to block with his free arm can largely be ignored and fought through.
- Elbow-to-elbow contact and driving with the legs (head moving away in an arc) remain the core mechanics for the conventional kimura that this variation is a backup to.
- The rotational finish drives the head across the opponent's stomach while the legs walk around his head, generating strong breaking pressure even without the standard two-hand grip.
