# Protecting Your Wrist And Drilling The Sit-Back

## Overview

Addresses the physical cost of holding a locked figure-of-four while a strong opponent explosively fights up from T-kimura, and gives a specific fix (release the grip rather than fight it), followed by a repetition drill — the "sit-back" — for building real confidence in the lever-and-fulcrum control from the previous chapter.

---

## Protecting the wrist

- A word to the wise: there's often a lot of stress on your hands and wrists when a strong, athletic opponent explodes up from underneath a T-kimura. It's common to feel like your kimura hand is hurting.
- Don't be intimidated by this. Open the figure-of-four grip, turn your hand palm up, and curl your wrist backward — release the grip rather than fighting to hold it.
- This feels counterintuitive — the instinct is to think you need to grip harder to hold the figure-of-four together. That instinct is wrong.
- The reason it still works with no grip: the control here comes from lever-and-fulcrum mechanics (elbow domination), not hand/grip strength. Opening the figure-of-four, palm up, wrist curled back, no grip — actually increases your effect rather than reducing it.

## Drilling the sit-back

- Simple test/drill: your training partner explodes up from bottom position in T-kimura, and you practice putting them back down.
- Athletes are drilled specifically in this position — one person comes up, the other practices sitting them back down, repeatedly.
- Sequence within the drill: when the partner first comes up, lift the elbow (hard for him to get up); then let him get up anyway, and practice sitting him back down using lever and fulcrum.
- Purpose of the drill: build real faith in the power of lever and fulcrum from the T-kimura position, rather than just knowing it intellectually.
- Reinforces the earlier structural point: the opponent cannot turn away — he's physically restrained in that direction — and staying still just lets you pin him on top. His only real option is to turn into you, which recreates the elbow battle every time.
- If you use an open kimura grip and focus on lifting the opponent's elbow consistently off the floor, even if he does get his elbow all the way to the mat and gets up, you can always return him to the mat with the same simple extension of lever and fulcrum.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Wrist/hand pain during a hard T-kimura scramble is normal — the fix is to open the figure-of-four, turn the hand palm up, and curl the wrist back rather than gripping harder.
- Control here comes from elbow/lever-fulcrum mechanics, not grip strength — releasing the grip does not reduce your control.
- Drill the "sit-back": partner explodes up repeatedly, you practice putting him back down, building confidence in the mechanism through repetition.
- Even with an open, grip-free kimura, consistently lifting the opponent's elbow keeps him restrained, and you can always sit him back down with lever and fulcrum if he does get up.
