# Failed Kimura To Front Triangle (Mote Sankaku)

## Overview

This lesson formalizes the branch-point logic that runs through the whole kimura system — the moment contact is made with a kimura grip, the situation splits into anterior (hand stays in front) or posterior (hand goes behind the back). Posterior means go straight to the finish; anterior means move to the next step of the system. Here, the "next step" demonstrated is abandoning a stalled kimura from half guard bottom in favor of an inversion into mote sankaku — the front triangle — using the kimura purely as a restraining mechanism to buy the time needed to invert.

---

## The branching system

- The instant kimura contact is made, ask: anterior or posterior?
  - **Posterior** (hand behind the back) — go straight to your submission holds.
  - **Anterior** (hand reinforced in front of the body) — proceed to the next step of the system.
- First option when anterior: use your legs to back up the kimura (very strong).
- When legs can't be brought to bear, the two main alternatives are:
  1. Switch to another position.
  2. Switch to a different submission hold — using the kimura as a restraining mechanism rather than a breaking mechanism. Armbars and triangles both fall into this category.
- This lesson covers one favorite option: exiting a failed or stalled kimura from half guard bottom into sankaku (triangle) — specifically mote sankaku, the front triangle, the most common variant.
- General principle: from a bottom position, get into some form of inversion. The inversion changes the relationship between the top man and bottom man, making the triangle a likely, high-probability attack.

## Primary entry sequence

1. Bottom half guard, adequate shields up, opponent comes in — hold him off and lock up an initial kimura.
2. Opponent hides the hand by biting down on his own thigh — a slogging match, both sides stuck.
3. It's usually in the opponent's own interest to raise his head, since that increases pressure on your kimura (a kind of counter-kimura effect against you). Opponents will often base a hand on your body (or the floor) to help lift themselves — either base point works the same way for your purposes.
4. As space opens up, begin inverting underneath him: shimmy the knee back and bring it in front of his body as a shield; hold him off with the knee; turn your body through and hook your foot all the way underneath his armpit.
5. Move the bottom leg out to begin the inversion, rolling through underneath the opponent.
6. During the inversion, aim your (outside) foot for the opponent's collarbone; as he holds his base, bring the other leg around behind his head.
7. As he turns to face you defensively, drag him in close — knee behind his head as a wedge, leg in a strong attacking position.
8. Release the kimura grip and catch the back of his head as he tries to pull away.
9. Turn the body around the corner, switch to a shin grip, and lock the figure-of-four.
10. As with any front triangle, turn the legs out in the standard direction for a strong strangulation finish.

## Variation — knee-shield entry from the far side

1. From half guard, lock the initial kimura.
2. Use the knee as a shield over the opponent's shoulder to move the body out and away.
3. Swing the (bottom) foot around and underneath, then start coming up into an attacking position.
4. Bring one foot around behind the opponent and step the other leg over his head as he squares up to face you.
5. Catch him by the head as he pulls away, catch the shin, turn the corner.
6. Lock the figure-of-four; legs turn out to the side for a strong finish.

## Variation — for when the opponent stays chest-down

- The knee-shield-in-front-of-the-chest entry can feel awkward if the opponent's head is too far forward, or if he stays chest-down over you instead of posturing.
- Alternative setup, same resulting front triangle:
  1. Lock the initial kimura, then willingly drop the (bottom) leg, giving up the half guard.
  2. As the opponent tries to move, put the knee in front of him and begin inverting underneath his body.
  3. As he straightens up and tries to move away, come all the way up, push off the outside foot, and hook the foot behind his armpit.
  4. Scoop up his second hand — with both arms now controlled, it becomes difficult for him to yank away.
  5. Push the leg around the corner and spin through; the knee ends up wedged behind his head.
  6. Lock the figure-of-four; the legs turn out for a strong strangle.

## Variation — dropping the bottom hook first

1. Bottom half guard, catch the opponent's arm, lock up, hold him off as best you can, then drop the bottom hook.
2. Push off the bottom leg to start pulling the opponent in, catching him elbow-to-elbow — a surprisingly strong grip.
3. As he pulls away, use the leg as a wedge behind his head.
4. Pull the body around the corner; the legs lock into the traditional figure-of-four.
5. Strong strangulation finish.

## Variation — short recap version

1. Initial half guard lock with arm framing; opponent comes forward.
2. Lock the initial kimura, drop the bottom leg, push up to base.
3. Come up into the inverted position, locking the opponent's head and shoulders.
4. As he tries to yank out (not as easy as it looks), turn around the corner into the front triangle.
5. Lock the figure-of-four and close for the finish.

## Key Takeaways

- Every kimura grip immediately branches: hand posterior → go straight to the finish; hand anterior → move to the next system step (legs backing up the kimura, switching positions, or switching submissions).
- Mote sankaku (front triangle) is one of the best "switch submissions" out of a stalled anterior kimura from half guard bottom — the kimura's job shifts from breaking mechanism to pure restraining mechanism.
- The mechanism that makes the triangle available is inversion: getting underneath the opponent changes the top-man/bottom-man relationship and opens the triangle as a high-probability attack.
- The opponent raising his head to relieve kimura pressure is exactly the moment that creates space to invert.
- Multiple entries (knee-in-front-of-chest, knee-in-front-with-dropped-leg, dropped-bottom-hook) all converge on the same finish: knee wedged behind the head, shin grip into figure-of-four, legs turning out for the strangle.
- Once both arms are controlled during the inversion (kimura hand plus a scooped second hand), it becomes very difficult for the opponent to yank free before the triangle locks.
