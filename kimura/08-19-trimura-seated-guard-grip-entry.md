# Trimura: Seated Guard Grip Entry

## Overview

Presents the instructor's personal favorite grip-entry method for controlling many forms of attack, including the hybrid half-kimura/half-triangle "trimura" — and it's also used to enter leg triangles on their own. The core case being made: conventional kimura attacks from closed guard are difficult to finish compared to those launched from a trap triangle, because the trap triangle gives direct control over the opponent's head in a way closed guard never can. The lesson then walks through the full entry from seated guard, through a pronated wrist grip and pass-off, into the trap triangle and the kimura that opens up when the opponent defends the strangle.

---

## Why trap triangle beats closed guard for kimura

- Trimura is an extremely versatile grip, useful for many forms of attack.
- Conventional kimura attacks from closed guard can be very difficult to finish compared to the same attacks launched from a trap triangle.
- The trap triangle directly influences the opponent's head, giving tremendous control that closed guard simply doesn't provide.
- As a result, regardless of the exact leg configuration used (trap triangle or a full figure of four), you'll have much more success from bottom position by controlling the head and the arm together, rather than relying on a simple closed guard.

## Entry from a seated position

1. Take a seated position and reach for a **pronated grip** on the opponent's wrist (palm facing away).
2. Use a pass-off rather than reaching directly: take the opponent's hand toward your center line, which exposes his wrist to your right hand; your left hand passes his wrist off to your right hand.
3. Go into an elbow two-on-one control on the arm.
4. The danger at this point is the opponent pulling his hand down and away — counter this by placing his hand across your center line onto your hip and trapping it there.
5. Result: a completely isolated arm, which makes it easy to kick your legs over into a trap triangle.

## Into the trap triangle and the kimura decision point

- Once you have control of the head, pull in with the knees. A strangulation is available directly from here — but the opponent will often force his arm to the outside specifically to make the strangle difficult.
- That defensive move is exactly where the kimura opportunity opens up (the "trimura"):
  1. Push the hand further to the outside than the opponent wanted it to go.
  2. Control both the wrist and the elbow, bringing the elbow down toward your sternum.
  3. Pull in with the knees and lock the kimura.

## Locking mechanics: the inverted knee and the bottom power line

- It helps to invert your right knee — point the foot up at the ceiling and the knee down at the mat. This creates a better turn and a better angle, and the ankles cross in this position.
- From here, draw the training partner's elbow up to the shoulder line to create the **bottom power line**: your head, his elbow, and both of his shoulders form a straight line — the perfect breaking position.
- The inverted knee (right foot pointing directly to the ceiling, right knee pointing directly down to the mat) is what controls the opponent's head so effectively with the legs, making for very strong, efficient finishes.

## Practical notes on the pass-off and hand trapping

- The pass-off (left hand to right hand across the center line) matters in live practice — it's unrealistic to expect to simply reach across with the right hand and grab the wrist directly.
- Lock the opponent's hand into your hip so that when he tries to pull away, it isn't easy. Everything — head and shoulders — drives forward throughout.
- As you kick your legs over, the feet lock. A straight triangle attempt is available here, but the opponent will typically posture up and take his hand to the outside specifically to prevent the strangle — that's the trigger for the kimura: push the hand outside, pull down so the elbow comes to the sternum, which brings his head down. Lock down with the inverted knee (right knee down, right foot up) so that head, elbow, and both shoulders align in a straight line — the perfect breaking position. Because the head is so heavily controlled, the elbow breaks in a very short space.

## Demonstration: the bottom power line in action

- Demonstrated with a training partner working the grip entry against a resisting partner: as the legs are thrown into position and the knee inverts, the demonstrator's head, the opponent's elbow, and both of the opponent's shoulders follow a straight line — visually, a belt laid across all four points would touch each one.
- When aligned this way, the finish is essentially guaranteed. If the head is off that line, success is much less likely. Getting back onto the bottom power line is what creates a very strong lock.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Kimura from closed guard is comparatively weak; kimura from a trap triangle is comparatively strong, because the trap triangle controls the opponent's head directly.
- Entry sequence from seated guard: pronated grip via a left-to-right pass-off on the wrist → elbow two-on-one → trap the isolated hand against your hip → kick the legs over into a trap triangle.
- If the opponent defends the strangle by forcing his arm outside, that's the cue for the kimura: push the hand further outside, control wrist and elbow, pull the elbow to your sternum, and lock with the knees.
- Invert the working knee (foot to the ceiling, knee to the mat) to create the bottom power line — head, opponent's elbow, and both his shoulders in a straight line.
- Alignment on the bottom power line is what makes the finish work; if the head drifts off that line, the finish becomes much less reliable.
