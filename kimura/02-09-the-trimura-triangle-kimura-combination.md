# The Trimura: Triangle-Kimura Combination

## Overview

The third of the three leg-incorporated methods for solving the "locked hands" problem in the dorsal kimura, and Danaher's stated favorite. Instead of using the legs purely to separate the opponent's hands (cross kimura) or to turn and break (kimura plata), this method uses senkaku — the triangle — to lock up the opponent's head itself. Trapping the head removes the wiggle room an opponent uses to loosen a kimura, and from the locked triangle position two separate kimura attacks become available on top of the strangle the triangle already provides. Jokingly nicknamed the "trimura" — half triangle, half kimura.

---

## The principle: trap the head

- Trapping the opponent's head is one of the keynotes of a powerful kimura — it removes the play and wiggle the opponent would otherwise use to loosen the grip.
- From a dorsal position, yoko senkaku (the side triangle) can be used to lock up the opponent's head and transition into a devastating double-kimura finish. Because the head can't move, the resulting kimura attacks come on very quickly and feel extremely tight.

## Entry sequence (first demonstration)

1. Take the knee inside the training partner's leg and push/separate.
2. Turn; the opponent, defending, takes his arm underneath and re-locks his hands to stop the kimura.
3. Thread the hand through, come down to an elbow.
4. Bring the legs close together, lifting underneath the training partner's armpit.
5. Take the second leg and pass it through.
6. Come back to the kimura grip and lock it down.
7. Turn the body up so the two legs go in a straight line, and relock the triangle.

## What the locked triangle gives you

- First and foremost: a tight stranglehold from the triangle itself.
- In addition, two kimura attacks become available:
  - **Push kimura**: thumb post on the training partner's wrist, push on the arm — because the head is trapped, this produces a fantastically strong kimura.
  - **Push-pull kimura**: switch to make an even tighter kimura via a push-pull action; the far arm can be broken at the wrist alone, or at the wrist and elbow together.
- Opponent's only defensive option is to grip his own hands together. Counter: attack the hands by pulling what appears to be the wrong way, driving the hands down to the floor. Unlock the triangle, switch base, and you end up positioned precisely on the power line, leading quickly into a breaking finish.
- Yoko senkaku locks up the head and shoulders, giving the opponent no space — kimura attacks from here feel shockingly tight and come on fast.

## Second demonstration (alternate angle)

1. From a dorsal position, lock in; get the knee inside and separate the training partner's hands.
2. Turn the leg over the opponent's leg; he locks his hands to prevent his hand going behind his back.
3. Lock in, use an elbow on the floor, put the leg in his armpit — slide the leg all the way through underneath his head.
4. Come back into an initial kimura grip to hold him in place (this alone makes it very difficult for him to come up on top).
5. To tighten the triangle: unlock the legs (often while holding the opponent's elbow or wrist for control), pop up, and thread both legs through so the relocked triangle produces a very tight strangulation.

## Kimura options from this tighter triangle

- A devastating kimura off a simple push on the far arm.
- An even more devastating kimura off a double push-and-pull on the far arm, combined with a separation of the hand behind the back.
- The strongest kimura finish of all: located directly on the power line, achieved by coming up on the near arm.
- Net result: two kimura attack options on the far arm, plus one exceptionally powerful kimura attack on the near arm — all layered on top of the underlying triangle strangle.

## Where this sits in the system

- The three strategies for incorporating the legs into kimura — cross kimura, kimura plata, and the trimura — all share the same purpose: preventing the kimura from becoming an arm-versus-arm strength battle, and instead making it a legs-versus-arm battle.
- This removes strength parity from the equation, allowing smaller, weaker athletes to defeat bigger, stronger opponents using the kimura.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The trimura uses senkaku (triangle) to trap the opponent's head, which is the key to preventing the wiggle room that loosens a kimura grip.
- From the locked (yoko senkaku) triangle: get a stranglehold plus two separate kimura attack options — a push kimura and a tighter push-pull kimura on the far arm.
- If the opponent defends by gripping his own hands, pull the "wrong way" to drive hands to the floor, unlock, switch base, and land on the power line for a quick finish.
- The strongest possible finish from this position comes up on the near arm, positioned on the power line.
- Cross kimura, kimura plata, and the trimura are three interchangeable ways to make kimura a legs-versus-arm contest rather than an arms-versus-arms strength battle.
