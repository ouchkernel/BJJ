# The One-Handed Kimura For A Blocked Hip

## Overview

Taught in the basement with Nicky Ryan in a group class setting. The standard dorsal Kimura finish relies on separating the opponent's hands and establishing the power line (running from the opponent's shoulder to his opposite hip), then covering his head and rotating along that line for a powerful, high-percentage finish. But an intelligent opponent can block that finish by using an arm to block your hip and deny you access to his head. This lesson covers the workaround: the one-handed Kimura, which uses a tripod action and a circular walk around the head to break the arm even without full head control.

---

## The standard two-handed finish

- In a dorsal Kimura situation, the emphasis is usually on separating the opponent's hands and establishing the **power line** — running from the opponent's shoulder to his opposite hip.
- Method: lead with the elbow, cover the opponent's head, and position the body along that power line. The elbow blocks the hip as you step over the head.
- As the opponent's body flattens out, the head moves away and a strong rotational finish is achieved, putting tremendous pressure on the trapped arm.
- This is described as the standard method of finishing and probably the most popular Kimura finish in the sport — extraordinarily effective, and for good reason.
- Mechanically: lead with the elbow (keeps the head high), then take a big, confident step across the opponent's body — from there the arm can be broken in a very short space.
- Sometimes the opponent's hands are locked together very strongly; a knee is often incorporated to help separate them and get the hand behind the back. Once the hand is behind the back, it's always the elbow that leads the movement into the strong finishing position.

## When an opponent blocks the hip

- A smart opponent will sometimes block the hip with his own arm — this is a good defensive move, because it makes it very difficult to cover the head and execute the standard finish.
- The answer is to switch to a different form of Kimura entirely: the **one-handed Kimura**.
- Pass the grip off and get down to the blade of the opponent's hand — never grip at the wrist. Going all the way to the blade of the hand maximizes leverage.
- If the opponent blocks your hip with his arm, block his hip in return with your own free hand.
- If his arm is in the way of the power line, incorporate a **tripod action** — come up high on your base to get past his blocking arm.
- Even without the head being covered, blocking his hips while walking around in a semicircle creates a very strong one-handed breaking effect.
- This is an excellent solution when someone has successfully blocked your hips and stopped the conventional two-handed Kimura finish.

## Live demo detail (vs. Nicky Ryan)

- Nicky Ryan uses his right arm to block the hip, which is what forces the switch into the one-handed variation.
- Sequence: the right hand comes back and blocks the opponent's hip; the tripod action then carries the attacker past the opponent's blocking arm; from there it's a circular walk around the training partner's head.
- Even as the opponent tries to stop the attack with his blocking arm, the tripod action makes it nearly impossible for him to succeed.
- Result: tremendous pressure develops as the opponent's head moves across his own stomach while the attacker's hips walk around the head.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Standard dorsal Kimura finish = separate the hands, establish the power line (shoulder to opposite hip), lead with the elbow, cover the head, step across the body, rotate — the most popular and effective Kimura finish in the sport.
- A strong locked grip on the opponent's hands can be broken open with the knee before working the hand behind the back.
- A smart opponent defends by blocking your hip with his arm, which denies you the ability to cover his head — the standard finish stalls here.
- The fix is the one-handed Kimura: grip the blade of the hand (not the wrist), block his hip in return, then use a tripod action to get past his blocking arm.
- Even without head control, blocking the hips and walking in a semicircle around the head produces a strong breaking effect — the tripod action makes the opponent's blocking arm essentially unable to stop it.
