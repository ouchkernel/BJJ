# Top Knee To Cross Kimura And Trap Triangle

## Overview

Building on the top-knee cross-face control introduced previously, this chapter shows the high-percentage follow-up: converting the cross-face hold into a cross kimura, and from there into a trap triangle (a triangle without a figure-four) that shuts down the opponent's rolling escape and produces a very powerful finish. A low-percentage strangle option from the same position is mentioned and explicitly set aside in favor of this kimura-based route.

---

## Rejected low-percentage option

- From the top-knee cross-face position, some strangle finishes exist that involve scissoring the legs.
- These aren't impossible, but they're low percentage — not a priority to teach when a stronger option exists.

## Entering cross kimura from top-knee cross-face

1. As the opponent struggles back toward his base, use the cross-face leg to actively keep his shoulders down.
2. Put your head on the floor.
3. Use the bottom leg (the working-side foot) to post to the floor and pull your body over, so his hand ends up inside your knee.
4. This transitions directly into the cross kimura position (introduced earlier in the course) — reached straight out of T-kimura rather than as a separate entry.
5. Use the opponent's elbow as a lever to bring his back off the mat.
6. As his back comes off the mat, curl your foot to the outside position and use the kimura leverage to bring him over, throwing your leg over his fist.
7. His arm is now caught inside yours. From here you can lock a triangle or simply close your legs, and apply finishing pressure with a pull.

## Handling the roll-through — the trap triangle

- If the opponent elects to roll through, let it happen and continue the attack rather than fighting it.
- Continuation: take your leg over the training partner's head and roll through with him.
- As he comes back up to his base, he's now locked in a **trap triangle** — a triangle formed with just the two feet locked together, without a figure-four.
- Bring his elbow up to the shoulder line, keep his hand trapped inside your leg.
- Result: a shockingly strong finish.

## Why this works — head control and the trinity of power

- Triangles linked to kimuras satisfy the **head control** element of the "trinity of power" referenced earlier in the course.
- Controlling the opponent's head dramatically increases the power available in the kimura.
- Between cross kimura and this triangle-linked variation, tremendous pressure is generated on the opponent's arm.

## Second demonstration pass (full detail)

1. From T-kimura, opponent turns up into you; use elbow lever to keep him down and top-knee cross-face to hold him — coming up means coming directly into the cross-face.
2. Bring your head to the mat, pop up, and put your knee over the training partner's fist.
3. Use the kimura to drag him up off the floor — this produces **cross kimura**: two legs across the opponent's back, elbow taken all the way up to the shoulder line (a classic way of amplifying kimura pressure).
4. From here you can lock a triangle, work with crossed feet, or other finishing options — but the core mechanism is the pull of the elbow at this angle, which creates tremendous pressure.
5. The opponent's realistic remaining option is to pitch forward and roll — accept the roll rather than resist it.
6. Back-step with your leg and go over the training partner's shoulder, committing down into a second roll-through.
7. This catches him in the trap triangle (two feet locked, no figure-four) — a second roll-through is now effectively impossible.
8. Bring the elbow to the shoulder line again for tremendous breaking pressure from bottom position.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Scissoring-leg strangles from the top-knee cross-face position exist but are low percentage — the cross kimura/trap triangle route is the preferred high-percentage path.
- The entry: cross-face leg pins shoulders, head to floor, bottom foot posts and pulls you over so opponent's hand lands inside your knee — this flows directly into cross kimura.
- Cross kimura mechanics: use the elbow as a lever to lift the opponent's back off the mat, throw your leg over his fist, catch the arm inside your leg.
- If the opponent rolls through, accept the roll and continue the attack rather than resisting — it leads into the trap triangle.
- Trap triangle = triangle without a figure-four, just the two feet locked, applied after the roll-through. It prevents a second roll and, combined with bringing the elbow to the shoulder line, produces a very strong finish.
- The underlying reason this hurts so much: triangle + kimura together satisfies head control, one leg of the "trinity of power," dramatically amplifying kimura pressure.
