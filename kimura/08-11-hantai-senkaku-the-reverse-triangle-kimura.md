# Hantai Senkaku: The Reverse Triangle Kimura

## Overview

There are two ways to employ the trap triangle position: simply crossing the ankles (the trap triangle already covered), or locking a genuine figure-four triangle around the head and one arm — but locked "the wrong way," with no intention of strangling. This is **hantai senkaku**, used purely to control the head and arm and set up a devastating Kimura break. Two critical technical details make it work: where the heel locks relative to the shoulder, and a specific method for exposing a hidden wrist.

---

## Two Ways to Employ the Position

- **Trap triangle**: simply cross the ankles around the head and trapped arm (covered in prior chapters).
- **Hantai senkaku (reverse/"wrong way" triangle)**: lock an actual figure-four triangle, but the intent is different — not to strangle, but to lock up and control the opponent's head and arm.

## Entering Hantai Senkaku

- Start from any of the established grip-fighting sequences and pull the training partner into a trap triangle situation as usual.
- As the hand is forced to the outside, get a hold of the opponent's head — but this time shimmy the hips the *opposite* way from the standard trap triangle finish.
- Instead of inverting the near knee and shifting the hips out to that side, shift the hips out to the *other* side.
- Physically grab your own shin and pull your ankle forward, over the opponent's shoulder.
- Lock the figure-four triangle from there.

### Critical Detail #1: Heel in Front of the Shoulder

- The triangle must be locked with the heel in **front** of the opponent's shoulder, not behind it.
- If the heel locks behind the shoulder, the opponent can still posture up and escape the position.
- Heel in front of the shoulder = the head is truly controlled and can't move.

## Exposing the Hidden Wrist

- Once head control is established, the opponent's wrist still needs to be exposed to build the Kimura grip.
- Problem: when the opponent tucks the hand behind his body, he hides the wrist.
- Solution — reverse the usual triangle mechanics:
  - Put the points of your shoulders on the mat, elbows together, going *backward* to the floor — the opposite direction you'd normally reach for a standard strangle triangle.
  - As the shoulders and elbows go back together, drive the hips up toward the ceiling.
  - This action exposes the opponent's wrist.
  - Go in with the thumb and lock it in.
  - Then reverse direction again, going back the same way you just came — this creates a good bend in the arm and locks up the full figure-four Kimura grip.

## Finishing Mechanics

- Draw the opponent's elbow up to your own armpit — the further his elbow is from your armpit, the less effective the finish.
- Because there's no intention to strangle, the usual triangle mechanics (e.g., covering the shoulder with the leg) don't need to be observed here.
- The finish is efficient specifically because the opponent's head cannot move — normal Kimura escapes rely on head movement, and hantai senkaku denies that entirely.
- With the head locked down, breaking pressure builds rapidly and the finish can come in a matter of millimeters of movement.

## Real-World Application

- This exact sequence — pronated elbow two-on-one into trap triangle, into hantai senkaku, into exposed-wrist Kimura — was used by Ethan Crowenstein at the ADCC trials to produce one of the most powerful Kimura breaks the grappling world has seen from bottom position.
- Why it's so powerful: it positions the attacker precisely on the power line from bottom, and it locks the opponent's head and shoulders down, making the usual defensive head movement against a Kimura almost impossible — so breaking pressure builds fast and dramatically.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Hantai senkaku is a figure-four triangle locked "the wrong way" purely to control the head and arm — there is no strangle intention.
- Non-negotiable detail: the heel must lock in front of the opponent's shoulder, not behind it, or he can still posture out.
- To expose a hidden wrist, reverse the usual triangle instinct — shoulders/elbows go backward to the mat, hips drive up, then reverse direction again to lock the figure-four Kimura grip.
- Draw the trapped elbow all the way to your own armpit for maximum pressure.
- The finish is so powerful because it removes the opponent's main escape tool for a Kimura — head movement — entirely.
- This sequence (grip fight → trap triangle → hantai senkaku → exposed wrist → Kimura) is the same one Ethan Crowenstein used for a landmark ADCC trials Kimura break.
