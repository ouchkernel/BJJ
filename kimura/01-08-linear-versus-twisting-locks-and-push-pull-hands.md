# Linear Versus Twisting Locks And Push/Pull Hands

## Overview

A foundational classification chapter: every joint lock in jiu-jitsu is either a linear lock or a twisting lock, and kimura falls firmly into the twisting category. Understanding that distinction sets up the core mechanical concept used throughout the rest of the series — every twisting lock is applied with a push hand and a pull hand, and kimura is no exception. The chapter closes by introducing side kimura as the starting point for the whole system.

---

## Linear locks vs. twisting locks

- **Linear locks**: the joint is attacked with straight-line force — the limb is taken straight and pressure is applied directly against the joint.
  - Examples: the classic armbar (juji gatame), ude gatami on two knees, kneebar, achilles lock.
- **Twisting locks**: the joint is attacked with a twist applied around it.
  - Examples: figure-four toehold, heel hook, americana — and kimura.
- This is a critical distinction to keep in mind every time you go to apply kimura: it is a twisting lock, not a linear one, and that shapes how force should be applied.

## Push hand and pull hand

- Every twisting lock involves a **push hand** and a **pull hand** working against each other on the joint.
- In kimura: with the training partner down and the arm bent, one hand provides a pushing force on the wrist while the other provides a pulling force on the elbow.
- The degree of arm bend is variable — kimuras can be worked with the arm bent more than 90 degrees, but the stated personal preference is for less than 90 degrees. An arm can be broken from either angle.
- Old-time catch wrestlers reportedly referred to the hand on the wrist as the **"stopper hand."** In truth, rather than actively pushing the wrist, the more accurate description is that this hand holds and immobilizes the wrist while the other hand pulls the elbow.
- Core operating concept for the whole series: create a strong pull on the opponent's elbow while at minimum immobilizing the wrist.

## Why push/pull matters

- Two opposing forces on the joint — a pull on the elbow and a stopping/immobilizing force on the wrist — create tremendous twisting pressure on the joint.
- In extreme cases this twisting pressure can take the opponent's elbow completely out of its socket.

## Starting point for the system

- Side kimura is introduced as the place to begin the kimura journey — described as the clearest and simplest entry point, even though there are dozens of ways to apply kimura covered later in the series.

---

## Key Takeaways

- All jiu-jitsu joint locks split into two categories: linear locks (straight-line force — armbar, ude gatami, kneebar, achilles lock) and twisting locks (twist around the joint — toehold, heel hook, americana, kimura).
- Kimura is a twisting lock — always keep that in mind when applying it.
- Every twisting lock uses a push hand and a pull hand; in kimura, the push/stopper hand holds and immobilizes the wrist while the pull hand drives the elbow.
- The real work is done by pulling the elbow, not by actively pushing the wrist — the "stopper hand" label from catch wrestling captures this well.
- Opposing push/pull forces on the joint generate the twisting pressure that can dislocate the elbow.
- Side kimura is the recommended starting technique for learning the whole system.
