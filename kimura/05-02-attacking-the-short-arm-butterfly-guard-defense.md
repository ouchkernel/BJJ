# Attacking The Short-Arm Butterfly Guard Defense

## Overview

A direct answer to the most common defensive adjustment opponents make once they've been caught by Kimura from butterfly guard a few times: they stop reaching and keep their hands back ("short arms"), denying the easy wrist grab the conventional entry relies on. This lesson covers how to manufacture that grip anyway by manipulating the opponent's head, plus a variant for when the opponent defends by dropping the shoulder instead of the hand.

---

## Why this situation comes up

- Butterfly guard is fast-moving and dynamic, so the attacker needs to be flexible with Kimura entries rather than relying on one setup.
- A common defensive adjustment: once an opponent has been submitted by Kimura a couple of times, he learns that reaching for you gives you a simple Kimura entry — so he keeps his hands back and plays "short arms," making the conventional grip much harder to get.
- If the arms are long (opponent reaching normally), go straight to the conventional Kimura entry — this lesson is specifically for when he denies that.

## Forcing the hand down: the head-throw tactic

1. You can't just grab a short-armed opponent's wrist directly — it's out of reach/guarded.
2. Instead, move his head and force a hand down toward the mat.
3. If he doesn't react and keeps his hands in place regardless, the head movement will force the hand to the mat, and from there you can start playing the half guard Kimura game.
4. In practice: throw the head toward the mat — he bases out with a hand to stop himself falling, and that base hand is exactly what you seize by the wrist.
5. Post on his hand, go up and over the top, and you land right back in the same attacking position used in the standard butterfly Kimura entry.

## From there: general attacking options

- As the opponent turns into you, use one of two general strategies:
  - Go underneath with the bottom knee, or
  - Bring the top leg over and control with the top knee.
- Either option leads into a range of attacking options — e.g., a cross Kimura finish is shown as one example off this position.
- The key mechanism throughout is: create a vulnerability by forcing the opponent's hand to the mat, then cover it and bring your body over the top to reach the same attacking position every time.

## Variant: opponent drops the shoulder instead of the hand

- Some opponents, instead of basing a hand on the mat, will just let the shoulder drop to the mat to avoid giving up the wrist.
- In that case, step to the inside position and simply score the wrist directly off the dropped shoulder.
- Go over the top and you land in the same attacking position as the hand-base version.
- Whichever defensive reaction the opponent chooses (hand to the mat, or shoulder to the mat), the goal is the same: get to that attacking position and go from there.

---

## Key Takeaways

- "Short arms" (opponent refusing to reach) is a learned defense against Kimura — expect it once an opponent has felt the submission a couple of times.
- If the arms are long, go straight to the Kimura; if he's playing short arms, manufacture the grip via a head throw instead of trying to grab a guarded wrist.
- Head throw → opponent bases a hand to the mat → seize the wrist → post and go over the top → land in the standard attacking position.
- If he defends by dropping the shoulder instead of basing a hand, just score the wrist directly off the shoulder and go over the top the same way.
- From the resulting position: bottom knee under or top knee over, both leading into a range of finishes (e.g., cross Kimura).
