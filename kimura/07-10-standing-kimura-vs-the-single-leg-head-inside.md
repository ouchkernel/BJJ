# Standing Kimura Vs. The Single Leg (Head Inside)

## Overview

Having covered kimura from top and bottom position on the ground, this section moves to the standing game — one of kimura's great virtues is that it's equally effective top, bottom, standing, and on the ground, in both gi and no-gi. The single most important standing application is using kimura as a reactive counter to leg takedowns, and above all to the single leg takedown, since it's statistically the most common takedown in modern submission grappling. The first branch covered here is the more common case: the opponent's head ends up to the **inside** when he picks up the single leg.

---

## Why the single leg is the priority target

- Kimura in the standing position works as a counter to single legs, double legs, waist locks, and body locks — but the single leg counter is the most important of all.
- There are several kinds of single leg: the **high single leg** (no knees touch the mat — lower level, step in, grab the leg; statistically the most common in ADCC-style competition), the **sweep single leg**, and the **single leg tackle** (going down to a knee).
- Regardless of which variety is used, kimura works as a counter to all of them, because the most common way people finish a single leg — whether they started standing or on their knees — is to stand back up to their feet and complete the takedown from there. That standing phase is exactly where the kimura counter operates.

## First diagnostic question: head inside or outside?

- The very first question once the opponent has picked up the single leg: is his head to the inside, or to the outside? This materially changes how you operate.
- Head to the inside is the more common case, so it's covered first.

## Head-inside sequence

1. If the opponent's head is in a good position tucked into your chest, it's very hard to lock a kimura — the head is a physical barrier.
2. Use your forearm to bring his head down and "stuff" it out of the way. This opens the route to the kimura.
3. Initially you may just be fighting for wrist control and head position (a wizard-style grip) while he holds the single leg.
4. At some point, come up, stuff the head with the forearm, go over the top, and lock the kimura.
5. Step in such a way that his head either moves out to the side or gets trapped against your hips — step and slide toward his far foot.
6. This produces a high-amplitude sumi-gaeshi-style throw that puts him down on the mat.
7. As he tries to lock his hands, take your legs and lock a triangle around his arms, splitting them apart.
8. From there it's an easy transition into the kimura (ude garami) breaking position.
9. As he comes up, you have two options: lift the trapped elbow and come through with the bottom knee, or return him to the mat and come over the top with the top knee.
10. Either way leads directly into the favorite breaking positions.

## Setting up the throw safely

- You must be able to hold your balance long enough when the opponent first picks up the single leg — don't be naive and let him simply put you down or switch to a double leg.
- First get into a good controlling position: control his far wrist (or similar).
- Then come up, stuff his head with the forearm, and step all the way up to his feet.
- Let your own head fall forward so his head naturally moves out to the side.
- This produces the high-amplitude throw.
- As he tries to come up on top of you afterward, he turns into you — always keep lifting and putting him back down.
- Scissor your legs, come over onto your elbows, step over his head, and arrive in a strong breaking position.

## Key Takeaways

- Kimura is equally effective top, bottom, standing, and on the ground — and works in both gi and no-gi.
- The single most important standing application is kimura as a reactive counter to the single leg takedown, because most single-leg finishes require the opponent to stand back up, which is exactly when the counter applies.
- First question when the single leg is picked up: is his head inside or outside? Head inside is the more common case, and the head is a barrier to the kimura that must be dealt with first.
- Head-inside solution: stuff the head with the forearm to clear the route to the kimura, then step to the opponent's far foot for a high-amplitude sumi-gaeshi-style throw.
- After the throw, trap and split his arms with a leg triangle to transition easily into the kimura/ude garami breaking position, choosing between lifting the elbow with the bottom knee or returning him to the mat and coming over the top knee.
- Secure your balance and grip before attempting the throw — don't let the opponent simply finish the single leg while you're still getting organized.
