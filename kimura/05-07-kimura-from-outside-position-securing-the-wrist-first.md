# Kimura From Outside Position — Securing The Wrist First

## Overview

Up to this point in the volume, every kimura entry against butterfly guard relied on getting some degree of inside position with the lead foot. This chapter poses the problem case: a bottom player with excellent footwork who simply will not allow that inside foot position, forcing the top player to work from outside position instead. It sets up the danger inherent in that situation — putting a leg up from outside position is a direct invitation for the opponent to attack the legs — and frames the need for a controlled, careful approach rather than an improvised one.

---

## The problem: no inside position available

- Every prior entry against butterfly guard depended on establishing some degree of inside position with the lead foot.
- Eventually you'll face an opponent who is very adept with his footwork and will not concede that inside position — he actively threatens your legs, and you're forced to work from outside position instead.
- Demonstrated live: the training partner does a great job maintaining inside position, denying any leg or knee entry no matter what's tried.

## Why outside position is dangerous

- Even without inside position, you can still go on the attack — but the method matters.
- The trap: if you put a leg up while your feet and knees are still in outside position, it's only a matter of time before your opponent pulls that leg into single leg X (Ashi Garami).
- Once he secures that entanglement, he's immediately ready to start attacking you back.
- Conclusion: you must be very careful about how and when you commit a leg from outside position, because a careless entry gives your opponent an easy invitation into your legs.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Outside-position kimura attacks are needed whenever the opponent's footwork denies you the usual inside foot position against butterfly guard.
- Putting a leg up carelessly from outside position is a direct invitation into single leg X / Ashi Garami for your opponent.
- The chapter closes on the question of what to do instead — handled by controlling the wrist early and moving decisively once committed, covered in the following chapter.
