# Grip Fighting Entries Into the Trap Triangle

## Overview

A menu of grip-fighting entries for getting into the trap triangle from seated guard, covering both grips you initiate yourself (two-on-ones) and responses to grips the opponent puts on you first (collar tie, inside bicep tie). Every entry funnels into the same finishing shape shown in the previous chapter: hand forced to the outside, knee inverted, ankles locked, everything aligned on the power line.

---

## Self-Initiated Grips: The Two-on-Ones

### Pronated two-on-one (favorite entry)

- Opponent has hands up in a hand-fighting situation.
- Pass the hand across and take a pronated grip (palm away).
- Connect to the hip for a strong connection.
- This is the go-to grip for attacking the trap triangle from seated guard, though not the only one.

### Supinated elbow two-on-one

- Get hold of the training partner's elbow.
- Pull the elbow toward your center line; bring your hand up with five fingers spread.
- Hug hand to wrist, hugging his knuckles to your chest.
- This is a **supinated** grip — palm back toward your own chest — instead of the pronated, palm-away version.
- Instead of locking his hand into your hip, lock it onto your own sternum.
- Result: shockingly difficult for the opponent to yank his arm free, even harder to escape than the pronated version.
- From here, force the hand to the outside as usual, invert the knee toward the floor, and line up on the power line.

## Responding to Opponent-Initiated Grips

### Collar tie → over tie

- A collar tie is actually a good grip for the opponent to use: if he pushes down on your knees from it, he can clear your legs to the side and pass your guard.
- Counter: when you see the collar tie, drop your same-side knee to the floor immediately (if your knee is up when he pushes, that's a real problem).
- From the dropped-knee position, take an **over tie** — your hand comes through and holds under his elbow.
- This makes it almost impossible for him to yank his arm out.
- From here: foot on his hip, hold tight to the knee, then bring the foot all the way through to lock the same trap triangle.
- Clear everything out to the side, invert the knee, lock the trap triangle, and come up to the power line to finish.

### Inside bicep tie → tricep/elbow-pit control

- Opponent takes an inside bicep tie, controlling the inside of your bicep.
- Counter: hold at his elbow with one hand; bring the second hand over to chop into his elbow pit.
- Now you're holding his tricep and elbow — a shockingly strong grip to break even when he tries to yank out.
- From this control: kick your leg in over the top and lock, pass the wrist to the outside, bring the hand around the corner, invert the knee, lock the ankles, with the knees coming up over the head.
- Result: right on the power line, ready to attack.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Every entry — whether self-initiated (pronated two-on-one, supinated elbow two-on-one) or reactive (over tie off a collar tie, tricep/elbow-pit control off an inside bicep tie) — funnels to the same finish: hand forced outside, knee inverted, ankles locked, alignment on the power line.
- A collar tie is dangerous because it lets the opponent clear your knees and pass; the fix is dropping your knee immediately and countering with an over tie under his elbow.
- The supinated elbow two-on-one, locked to your own sternum, is at least as strong as the pronated hip-connected grip and gives another entry option depending on how the opponent hand-fights.
- The common thread across all these grips: get a strong enough connection that the opponent cannot simply yank the trapped arm free before you can fire your legs into the trap triangle.
