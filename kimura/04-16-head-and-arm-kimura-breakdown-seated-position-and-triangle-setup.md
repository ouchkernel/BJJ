# Head-And-Arm Kimura Breakdown — Seated Position And Triangle Setup

## Overview

A full classroom breakdown of the head-and-arm kimura previewed in the prior basement footage. Danaher walks through the mechanics step by step: what to do when a conventional kimura is stymied, how the seated position frees both feet, how that leads into Ushiro Senkaku (triangle from the back), and why locking a triangle first makes the kimura finish come on fast — plus the Hantai Senkaku alternative when the opponent turns into you.

---

## Starting Problem: The Stymied Conventional Kimura

- Danaher demonstrates attempting a conventional kimura and getting stymied by the opponent's resistance — he can't separate the opponent's hands.
- The answer is to move around to the back and lock up the **head-and-arm kimura** — a kimura grip that also includes the opponent's head.

## Seated Position And The Two Unweighted Feet

- From the head-and-arm kimura, take a **seated position**.
- From seated, the first leg passes across the training partner's hips and goes through to the far side.
- This access to the first leg's crossing enables access to the second leg.
- Result: two unweighted feet.
- With both feet unweighted, use the strength of the legs to separate the opponent's hands and place a foot inside the opponent's armpit.

## Transitioning Into Ushiro Senkaku

- Foot-in-armpit control makes it easy to transfer directly into **Ushiro Senkaku** — the triangle from the back.
- Once that position is locked, the triangle greatly facilitates any kimura attack from here.

## Attacking The Kimura From Ushiro Senkaku

- Attacking the kimura directly out of Ushiro Senkaku puts severe breaking pressure on the opponent.
- Because the opponent's head cannot move, it becomes very difficult for him to prevent a strong breaking action on the shoulder and elbow.
- This is called out as one of Danaher's favorite positions to attack the kimura from — opponents rarely see a kimura coming from such an unconventional setup.
- A second benefit: if the opponent manages to turn inside the Ushiro Senkaku, he comes right up into a **front triangle** — the transition is simply going from one triangle to the other, with no lost control.

## The Hantai Senkaku Alternative

- When the opponent turns into you, switch to **Hantai Senkaku** — a triangle with the legs locked in what appears to be the wrong way.
- The triangle is locked in front of the opponent's far shoulder, which gives strong control of both the head and the arm.
- From here, a strong kimura attack is available from bottom position.
- Because the opponent's head can't move once it's locked in the triangle, defending the kimura becomes exceptionally difficult — described as one of the central themes of this instructional.

## Full Sequence Recap

1. Starting position: opponent's hands are securely locked together — very hard to get a hand behind his back for a conventional kimura.
2. Switch around behind him, lock up the head and the arm together, and take a seated position.
3. The seated position makes it easy to pass the legs across the opponent's body.
4. Use the feet to separate his hands.
5. Make a quick transition into Ushiro Senkaku (the rear triangle).
6. First attack option: go straight to the kimura, hand on the outside of the thigh — a very strong breaking position.
7. If the opponent manages to turn into you, switch the legs and go to Hantai Senkaku (the triangle locked the wrong way) instead — here the intention is the kimura, not a strangle.
8. Lock the feet in front of the training partner's far shoulder, then bend the arm in the usual fashion for the kimura.
9. Because the opponent's head is so securely trapped throughout, extrication is very difficult.

---

## Key Takeaways

- When a conventional kimura is stymied by an opponent who won't let his hands separate, move to the back and add head control — a head-and-arm kimura.
- The seated position is the mechanical key: it unweights both feet so the legs can do the work of separating the opponent's hands.
- Foot in the armpit is the bridge from head-and-arm kimura into Ushiro Senkaku (triangle from the back).
- A locked triangle massively facilitates the kimura finish — the opponent's trapped head can't be used to help him escape the joint attack.
- Two triangle options exist depending on opponent reaction: Ushiro Senkaku by default, Hantai Senkaku if he turns into you — both keep the head locked and the kimura available.
- If he turns inside Ushiro Senkaku, it converts directly into a front triangle rather than losing the position.
