# Getting To Kimura — Neutral Vs. Dominant Position And The Tombstone Defense

## Overview

Once you move from a neutral exchange into a dominant position — side control, mount, or similar — the opponent's behavior changes completely. They now recognize real danger and defend by pulling everything in tight, creating the "tombstone" position that makes it very hard to get in on their arms. This chapter frames that problem and sets up the three methods used to solve it.

---

## Neutral vs. dominant position

- In a neutral position (e.g., open guard), the opponent doesn't feel threatened and reaches for your body freely — this creates natural kimura opportunities (covered previously).
- Everything changes once you get into a **dominant position** like side control or mount.
- In a dominant position, the opponent recognizes there's now a real danger, and typically responds by bringing everything in tight.

## The tombstone defense

- With arms brought in tight, the opponent adopts what's referred to as the **tombstone position** — arms drawn in close to the body, like a tombstone.
- In this position it's very, very hard to get in on the training partner's arms and secure a kimura grip.
- Context matters here: this isn't a beginner opponent with arms "flopping around in the breeze" where you could just grab a wrist and go straight to kimura. At a high skill level, everyone knows what a kimura is, so everyone keeps their arms in close by default once threatened.

## The central problem and its answer

- The question this sets up: when people are in these good, sound defensive (tombstone) positions, how do you get them into a kimura hold?
- The answer: there are three main methods. Mastery of kimura requires being good at all three (detailed in the next lesson).

---

## Key Takeaways

- Kimura opportunities in neutral positions come easily because the opponent doesn't feel threatened; dominant positions (side control, mount) flip that — the opponent now defends hard.
- The tombstone position — arms drawn in tight to the body — is the standard high-level defensive response to a dominant position threat.
- Getting a kimura grip on a tombstone-defending opponent is fundamentally harder than on someone with arms out in the open, and requires deliberate entry methods rather than just grabbing a wrist.
- Three main entry methods exist to solve this problem — a complete kimura player needs to be competent in all three.
