# The Yoko Sankaku (Side Triangle) Setup For A Basing Opponent

## Overview

A judo-derived answer to the specific problem that arises when an opponent successfully bases out his free hand against the earlier turtle-kimura entries (the original method and the Olympic bar variation): a long base of support makes him hard to lever over. The fix is to abandon the lever and instead lock the kimura grip early, then wrap a leg around to build a yoko sankaku (side triangle) — a control position that opens strangle and multiple kimura finishing options regardless of the base.

---

## The problem this solves

- Working from turtle position with an Olympic bar already in place, arm secured, head covered successfully.
- If the training partner puts his free hand out as a base of support (hand comes forward for a long base), it becomes difficult to lever him over — the long base resists the turning action.
- This is exactly the situation the yoko sankaku (side triangle) is designed to solve.

## Entry sequence

1. Start from the one-on-one grip behind the training partner, as always.
2. Secure the one-on-one, bring it to a two-on-one.
3. Bring your knee over the training partner's head.
4. Lever the training partner's arm up — he responds by putting his hand out as a base of support, making him difficult to turn over because of that long base.
5. Instead of continuing to fight the lever, go through and lock a kimura grip right there.
6. Step your foot over and behind the training partner's armpit to get a good hook — don't just place the foot over, make sure it back-heels and hooks in.
7. Fall straight over; this lands you in position to hook your top leg around the training partner's arm.
8. With your frame arm in, catch the training partner with the elbow, turn your body through, and lock the figure of four — this completes the yoko sankaku.

## Attacking options from the yoko sankaku

- A strangle is directly available from here.
- Kimuras on the far arm:
  - A pushing kimura.
  - A mixed pull-and-push kimura.
- Alternatively, release the far arm, focus on the near arm, and come up onto the power line for a strong breaking attack.
- General rule: when you see the opponent base out against the earlier entries, step in behind the arm and go into the yoko sankaku instead.

## Second demonstration (different angle)

1. Opponent in a good, tight defensive position.
2. Score the initial one-on-one, punch in, turn it into a two-on-one.
3. Come up, try to turn him in — he resists well.
4. Go through and lock up the initial kimura grip.
5. Foot goes over the top and back-heels in.
6. Sit straight back.
7. Catch the training partner's arm, secure the elbow.
8. Come up to relock the triangle.
9. From here: a very strong strangulation is available, plus a one-handed push kimura, a two-handed combined push-and-pull kimura, and the near-arm attack coming all the way back to the power line for a breaking finish.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The yoko sankaku (side triangle) is the answer when an opponent bases his free hand out long enough to defeat a straight lever from the Olympic bar or original kimura entry.
- Trigger: whenever levering the arm up causes the opponent to base out with a long support arm and you feel you can't turn him over.
- Mechanics: lock the kimura grip first, hook the foot behind the armpit (back-heel it, don't just lay it over), fall straight over, hook the top leg around the arm, catch with the elbow, turn through, lock the figure of four.
- From the completed yoko sankaku you have a strangle, a pushing kimura, a mixed push/pull kimura on the far arm, or a near-arm breaking attack up to the power line — pick based on what the opponent gives you.
- This is explicitly the counter to an opponent resisting the previous two turtle-kimura entries (the original method and the Olympic bar) by basing out.
