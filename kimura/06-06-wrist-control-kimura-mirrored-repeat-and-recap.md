# Wrist Control Kimura — Mirrored Repeat And Recap

## Overview

A consolidating recap of the wrist-control closed guard kimura, restating the diagnosis (setup failure, not a kimura failure) and re-running the full sequence — including a mirrored repetition on the other side — with a couple of finishing refinements added: a one-handed finish demonstration and an emphasis on lifting the hips for a stronger two-handed, arched-body finish.

---

## Restating the diagnosis

- Traditional closed guard kimura has historically been a massive statistical failure at the championship level — unlike virtually every other beginner move, which shows up constantly in elite competition (potentially tens of thousands of successful examples for other basics vs. a handful for closed guard kimura across decades).
- The conclusion stands: it's not the kimura hold that's broken (plenty of other kimura setups succeed constantly), it's the setup. The chapter also flags that the finish itself has typically been weak too, because most people use a push instead of a pull.

## The full sequence, recapped

1. Instead of using momentum to fight through hands, biceps, and head, get wrist control.
2. Come to inside position, get two hands on one (two-on-one grip), and bring everything down to the hip.
3. Once at the hip, apply a thumb grip on the training partner's wrist.
4. As the opponent tries to hold his base, bring him forward with your legs over your shoulder and make him post/base out.
5. Turn the corner and lock the kimura — this does not require sitting up; the arm is already in place.
6. As he tries to recover: shimmy the hips out and invert the knee so the knee points down at the floor (not up at the ceiling) — if the knee points up, the opponent can posture up and pressure your arm.
7. Cross the feet into a strong, resistant body shape as he posts his hand and pushes.
8. Turn so both shoulders and the elbow line up on the shoulder line.
9. Maximize the shoulder line by bringing his elbow to your armpit.
10. At this point the opponent is nearly finished before you've even started pulling.

## Finishing refinements

- **One-handed finish**: a short pull with just one hand is enough to finish once the shoulder line and armpit connection are established — demonstrating how little force is actually needed once the mechanics are right.
- **Two-handed, arched-body finish**: using two hands with an arched body that puts all your weight onto the opponent is described as devastating. Key detail — lift the hips off the floor. If the hips stay on the mat with the body bent, the technique degenerates into a push; lifting the pelvis lets you focus purely on the pull of the arm.

## Repetition on the mirrored side

- The whole sequence — wrist control to the hip, thumb grip, bringing the opponent forward and off-balance, turning the corner into the kimura lock, inverting the knee, aligning the shoulder line, elbow to armpit, hip-lifted pull-dominant finish — is repeated on the opposite side to reinforce it works symmetrically.

## Why the setup matters more than raw athleticism

- Closed guard kimura remains poorly represented in world championship jiu-jitsu despite being one of the most basic moves taught — every other beginner move has roughly ten thousand or more successful championship examples; closed guard kimura is lucky to reach double digits.
- The failure is not the kimura (which works fine from other positions) — it's specifically the traditional setup, which forces you to fight through hands, biceps, and head using momentum alone.
- Getting two-on-one wrist control and driving down to the hip solves all three problems at once: the hand can't stop you (it's controlled), the bicep can't stop you (connected to the controlled arm), and the head is taken out of position over your shoulder.
- Once the hand is out, extraction/off-balancing (weight on the body, hips off the floor, head lined up with the opponent's head) sets up the angle.
- Angle-finding is about reaching the shoulder line: a straight line through the opponent's far shoulder, near shoulder, and elbow, with your own head also on that line. All four elements aligned = a winning breaking position.
- The final piece is always bringing the opponent's elbow to your armpit — at that point, whether you kept a thumb in the grip or not becomes irrelevant, since the mechanical position itself is what breaks the arm.
- Finish with everything off the mat, including the hips — if the hips fold, the opponent's head can rise, which is a problem — so keep everything elevated and pull.

## Key Takeaways

- The wrist-control setup solves the closed guard kimura's historical failure by neutralizing hands, biceps, and head simultaneously, rather than relying on speed to beat all three separately.
- Sequence: two-on-one to the hip, thumb grip, drag opponent forward/off-balance, turn the corner into the lock — no sit-up required.
- Invert the near knee so it points down (never up) to prevent the opponent from posturing and pressuring the arm.
- The shoulder line — far shoulder, near shoulder, elbow, and your own head all aligned — combined with pulling the elbow to your armpit is what turns a locked kimura into an actual break.
- Keep the hips elevated off the mat throughout the finish; a flat, bent-body position degenerates into an ineffective push and lets the opponent's head rise.
- The technique works identically on both sides — this chapter repeats the full sequence mirrored to prove it.
