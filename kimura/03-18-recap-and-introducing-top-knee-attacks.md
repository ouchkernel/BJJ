# Recap And Introducing Top Knee Attacks

## Overview

A recap of why T-kimura is treated as a cornerstone of the whole kimura system, followed by the introduction of a second major branch of attack from that position: using the top leg to pin the opponent's shoulders to the mat, as an alternative to the bottom-knee-penetration attacks already covered.

---

## Why T-kimura is a cornerstone

- T-kimura lets a good athlete take an uncontrolled scramble situation and turn it into a win in a reliable, systematic fashion.
- Once you're in T-kimura, the opponent's options are reduced to one:
  - He can't turn away — physically impossible from this position.
  - He can't stay where he is — tactically self-defeating, since you can simply come up and pin him.
  - He has to turn *into* you.
- Turning into you creates a clear battle for elbow position/leverage — a battle you're favored to win.
- Even when you lose that specific battle, the opponent only manages to get up to his knees, and there are still good attacking options from that outcome.

## Two directions of attack from T-kimura

- **Bottom-knee attacks** (covered previously): your bottom knee scoots underneath the opponent as space opens from the elbow-lever battle, and a large number of attacks flow from that penetration.
- **Top-leg attacks** (new direction): instead of bringing in the bottom knee, use the top leg to keep the opponent's shoulders pinned to the mat.

## Introducing the top-leg method

1. From T-kimura, the ongoing battle is for the lever of the opponent's elbow as he tries to turn into you.
2. Rather than let that space go to the bottom knee, bring the **top knee over as a cross-face**.
3. When the opponent tries to get up, he is now fighting your leg with his own head — a difficult battle for him.
4. Result: it becomes easy to keep his shoulders pinned down to the mat.

## Demonstrated repetition

- T-kimura position; as the opponent explodes up, use the elbow lever to keep him down, then throw the top knee over the top so it functions as a cross-face.
- With the cross-face knee in place, any attempt by the opponent to get up means fighting directly against the strength of your legs.

---

## Key Takeaways

- T-kimura forces a binary choice on the opponent (turn into you or get pinned), and the resulting elbow battle is one you're built to win — this is why the position is a cornerstone.
- Even losing the elbow battle isn't a disaster; the opponent only reaches his knees, and attacking options remain.
- There are two branches of attack from T-kimura: bottom-knee penetration (already covered) and top-leg cross-face control (introduced here).
- The top-knee cross-face works by making the opponent fight your leg with his own head when he tries to stand — a losing battle for him that keeps his shoulders pinned.
