# The Half Guard T-Kimura Entry

## Overview

Bottom half guard is one of the easiest positions from which to enter a kimura — easier than closed guard — but it is also riskier and less reliable when it comes to actually finishing. This lesson shows how to use the restraining function of kimura (locked with the hand still in front of the body, not yet behind the back) to willingly give up half guard, use the opponent's own passing momentum against him, and land in a strong T-kimura finishing position.

---

## Two functions of a kimura grip

- **Submission hold** — when the hand is worked behind the back.
- **Restraining hold** — when the hand is still in front of the body. This lesson uses the restraining version: lock the kimura with the hand in front, then use that restraining leverage to get to the T-kimura position and finish from there.

## Entry sequence from bottom half guard

1. Start in bottom half guard with a shield in place — knee and forearm — so the opponent can't get chest-to-chest.
2. Dominate the inside position as he reaches for the cross face; never let him win the cross face.
3. Work to the wrist, go around the corner, and lock up the kimura grip.
4. Often the opponent will successfully reinforce his hand against his own thigh, and it can't be extracted.
5. At this point, **willingly give up the half guard**: place your leg inside the opponent's feet and let him bypass your legs entirely.
6. This looks like a huge risk — no hand behind the back, and he's just passed your legs — but the kimura restraint is still doing work.
7. Shimmy the hips out with both feet as the opponent tries to hold his base.
8. Whip the legs in one motion around the opponent's body. He has no ability to base out with the trapped arm, because it's still locked in the kimura.
9. Use the momentum of the legs to throw your body forward and take the opponent down with the kimura — this puts you in the T-kimura position, because the opponent's body momentum is moving away from you.
10. Scissor the legs, step over the opponent's body, come up to the elbow, and land directly on the power line, ready to finish.

## Key principle: follow the moving body

- As a general rule, when a body is moving in a given direction, always follow the body. If you've thrown the opponent to your right, come up to your right and exploit that moving body weight rather than fighting against it.

## The unwinding/throwing mechanic in detail

- Starting from the locked kimura with the opponent making a strong base, the entire action is a **wide sweep of the legs with momentum around the body**.
- The key idea: your head must end up inside and underneath the opponent's chest. If the head stays outside, the throw will never work — the legs are what drive the head underneath.
- Even if the opponent tries to come back up into you, he comes up directly into a cross face, which is easy to roll through into a strong breaking position.
- Any of the T-kimura finishers already covered in the course will work from the resulting position.

## Second demonstration (guard-pass variation)

1. Half guard bottom, knee-and-forearm shield — one controls the hip line, one controls the shoulder line.
2. Opponent drives forward; hold him off, work to the wrist.
3. Opponent successfully hides the hand — nothing to do with it directly.
4. Put your leg inside his and give him a simple guard pass.
5. Shoot the hips out, move all the way around to the side, using the gathering body momentum to throw the man down.
6. Follow the momentum — leg goes across, come up to the elbow — landing in a perfect breaking position.

## Why kimura works so well as a restraining mechanism here

- Once used in this fashion to lever the opponent over, the kimura's value as a restraining mechanism becomes clear: it restrains the opponent's movement and gives access to a very strong lever and fulcrum, which lets you put a man down from bottom position and attack with kimura even when you never got the hand behind the back.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Bottom half guard is an easy entry into kimura but a riskier finish than closed guard — know the tradeoff going in.
- The restraining version of kimura (hand still in front) is enough to control an opponent's base even before you've secured the hand behind the back.
- Willingly surrendering the half guard leg position is a deliberate tactic here, not a mistake — it sets up the whipping/throwing motion.
- The throw only works if your head ends up inside and underneath the opponent's chest; head-outside throws fail.
- Always follow the direction the opponent's body is moving after the throw — that's where you come up to finish.
- Even if the opponent tries to come back up into you, he comes up into a cross face you can roll straight through.
