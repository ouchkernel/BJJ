# The Olympic Bar Variation For Turtle Position

## Overview

A modification of the classic turtle-position kimura entry, borrowing a control mechanism from freestyle wrestling — the "Olympic bar" across the back — that Danaher favors for its stability and mechanical efficiency. Where the original method (attributed to Kimura himself, using the ude garami / kimura lock to finish many opponents from turtle) directly levers the arm up and over, the Olympic bar variation first pins the opponent flat to the mat with a bar across his back before working through to the same breaking position.

---

## Context: the original method

- The method most of the historical evidence suggests Kimura himself used to finish many opponents from turtle position.
- Highly effective for taking a strong defensive turtle, putting the man down, and arriving at one of the strongest breaking positions in the sport.

## The Olympic bar entry

- Derived from a common wrestling move designed to pin people — repurposed here to set up the kimura submission instead.
- From training partner in defensive turtle:
  1. Head position comes forward — score the initial one-on-one grip.
  2. Move around, convert the one-on-one into a two-on-one.
  3. Cover the training partner's head, get your knee over the top.
  4. Lever the training partner's arm upward.
  5. Come in elbow to elbow; your hand goes across to the opposite shoulder so your forearm and upper arm form a bar across the training partner's back — the "Olympic bar."
  6. Key detail: keep the barring hand up near the shoulder, never down low.
- Once the bar is in place, if the opponent tries to bring his hand back down defensively to the floor, it cannot get through your arm — "no one's that strong."

## Turning the opponent down

1. With the Olympic bar set, put your own hand on the floor and pin/break the opponent down to shoulder and hip, turning him down into a dorsal (chest-down) position.
2. From dorsal position, swim your hands through and come back to an elbow-to-elbow position.
3. Break his grip, get his hand behind his back.
4. Put your elbow down on the floor.
5. Step boldly across the training partner's body.
6. Arrive on the diagonal power line, ready to attack with the kimura.

Alternative phrasing of the same finish sequence: pin the opponent down to shoulder and hip, draw the elbow up, punch in with the second arm, and arrive in the classic frame position with the kimura in place — elbow up to the shoulder line, opponent's hand behind the scapula. From there, convert down to the side kimura: elbow leads, step boldly across the training partner's body, head moves backward, and you arrive on the power line in a strong breaking position.

## Why Danaher favors this method

- Very stable.
- Incredibly mechanically strong and efficient.
- Difficult for the opponent to upset your balance or resist as you move through the sequence.

## Handling a bare hand fed away from the body

- If the opponent's hands are so far away that you can't score the wrists at all, that's actually a favorable sign — it typically means it becomes very easy to go into alternative attacks, since he's usually balled up tightly to prevent back attacks or the crucifix, which in turn makes the initial one-on-one grip easy to obtain.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The Olympic bar borrows a wrestling pinning mechanism (forearm-and-upper-arm bar across the back, elbow to elbow) and repurposes it as a kimura setup from turtle position.
- Keep the barring hand up by the shoulder, not down low — this is what makes it impossible for the opponent's hand to fight back through.
- Sequence after the bar is set: pin down to shoulder/hip → turn to dorsal (or draw elbow up and punch second arm through) → classic frame position with kimura in place → elbow leads, step boldly across, head moves back → power line breaking position.
- Danaher rates this method highly for its stability, mechanical efficiency, and resistance to the opponent's counters.
- An opponent balled up too tightly to reach for the wrists is not a problem — it's what makes the initial one-on-one grip easy and opens alternative attacks.
