# Ushiro Senkaku: Triangle Strangle From The Back & Closing Thoughts

## Overview

A second finishing option off the same kimura-to-back transition covered in the previous lesson: instead of the straight strangle out of the body triangle, use ushiro senkaku — a strangle hold reinforced with a triangle from the back. The lesson closes the volume's kimura-to-other-systems material with Danaher's broader point: kimura is one of the best gateways in jiu-jitsu to other systems, and getting tunnel vision on the kimura submission itself means missing the far larger set of options it opens up.

---

## Entry (first demonstration)

1. Start from the same dorsal position used for the back-position transition — unable to get the hand behind the back.
2. Chin level drops, initial turn, drive through in that direction, bicep in behind the head.
3. Getting ear-to-ear makes it easy to get a hold of the training partner's wrist.
4. Sit down; bring one leg across, bring the other leg out — two unweighted legs. At this point it no longer matters how tightly the opponent holds on with his arms.

## Building the ushiro senkaku strangle

1. Put two feet around the training partner's wrist and separate the hands.
2. Put a foot into the armpit.
3. Bring his elbow to your armpit, bring his knuckles onto your chest, and sit back.
4. Fall with the legs kinked. Even without a triangle locked yet, spinning out from here is incredibly difficult for the opponent.
5. Lock an initial triangle, hand-assisted, and place the shoelaces behind the training partner's lat muscle.
6. Keep the knuckles connected to your chest throughout — as the opponent tries to work free, it remains very difficult.
7. A simple squeeze of the legs creates a very strong strangulation effect.

## Entry (second demonstration)

1. From an initial kimura start where the hands can't be separated: switch from kimura system to back system.
2. Chin down, turn, lock the back of the head, come up to the knees, get ear to the opponent's ear.
3. Lock the figure-of-four; sit down; take the first foot across the line of the belt; take the second foot out — two unweighted feet.
4. These feet move easily onto the opponent's wrist to separate the hands; put a foot into the armpit.
5. First priority from here: connect the knuckles to the chest, so that when you fall to the side and kink the legs, spinning out remains very difficult even if attempted.
6. Lock an initial figure-of-four, then hand-assist it off; the foot goes behind the back, connecting both arms so the knuckles stay at the chest.
7. As the opponent tries to turn out, it's very difficult; the squeeze of the legs produces the finish.

## Closing thoughts on the kimura system

- Using these methods, a failed dorsal kimura — where the hand simply could not be gotten behind the back — can be converted into an entirely different system: the back system.
- Kimura, across all the moves and holds in this course, is one of the very best gateways to other systems. It leads to: kimura → legs, kimura → front headlock, and kimura → back (Danaher's personal favorite of the gateways).
- The closing advice: don't get tunnel vision when positioning a kimura, fixating only on the kimura submission itself. Any time you have a kimura locked, you also have just a few simple steps available into another highly effective system. Always keep that in mind while working.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Ushiro senkaku is the back-triangle strangle alternative to the straight strangle finish out of the same kimura-to-back transition.
- Sequence: separate hands with two unweighted feet, foot to armpit, elbow to armpit, knuckles to chest, sit back with legs kinked — this alone (even pre-triangle) makes spinning out very difficult.
- Lock the triangle hand-assisted, shoelaces behind the lat muscle, keep knuckles connected to the chest, then finish with a simple leg squeeze.
- Connecting the knuckles to the chest before falling and kinking the legs is the first priority in both demonstrated entries — it's what kills the spin-out escape.
- Big-picture closing point: kimura is one of the best gateways in jiu-jitsu to other systems (legs, front headlock, and especially the back) — don't get tunnel-visioned on finishing the kimura itself when a few simple steps open up a much stronger system.
