# Turtle Kimura Finish — The Diagonal Power Line And Breaking Mechanics

## Overview

Picking up from the one-on-one-to-bar-arm setup, this chapter shows the complete finishing sequence for the turtle-position kimura, demonstrated from two different angles, and explains the mechanical principle that makes it so devastating: aligning the whole body along a diagonal line of power. It closes with a demonstration of the hold's breaking potential, and ties the mechanics back to the historical man Kimura's reputed ability to break arms with this exact method.

---

## From bar arm to locked kimura

- Starting behind the training partner in a decent attacking position: secure the initial one-on-one and pull everything in tight, so it isn't easy for him to move away.
- Come around the corner into two hands on one; take the knee over his head and pinch in, elevate, and throw in the initial bar arm.
- As the partner makes a strong base from turtle position, his arm is exposed — go through elbow to elbow and catch the kimura.

## Taking the hand to the spine

- Take the trapped hand to the opponent's spine — this gives you the "hand behind the back" advantage, also called the posterior hand position.
- From here, bring him down to his shoulder: step your foot up, put your knee on the mat, turn, and put your elbow down on the floor. This beats his shoulder to the ground.
- Spread your feet 90 degrees and dominate the power line by bringing your head down to his hips as your elbow drives to his elbow — this produces a punishing kimura all the way down on the floor, landing exactly on the power line and amplifying every mechanical detail already established.

## The finish, shown from a second angle

- Start behind the training partner on his back, two arms in behind his arms. This is not seatbelt gripping — you are not strangling and not going after the back in the usual sense; you stay behind both arms with your head coming over his head.
- If your head hangs back, it's hard to reach the wrist — commit with your head to snatch the wrist and feed it in, establishing the initial one-on-one.
- Go around the corner as he makes a strong base (he doesn't want to fall over or be pinned). Bring the knee over his head and lock in the first bar arm — once this is set, it's almost impossible for him to get his hand back down to the floor.
- From the bar arm, go through elbow to elbow and lock the kimura from this position. Drive the training partner over and down to the mat.
- Landing position: knee comes forward, elbow goes down to the mat. Step your legs across his body, move your head over his hips, elbow to his elbow, and pull the dominant kimura for the finish.

## The diagonal line of power

- The key to a good kimura lock: the whole body lines up on the diagonal line of power running from your head, through your hips, to your locked elbow.
- Demonstrated live: this is shown to be the breaking point for the training partner — going further would begin to damage the arm — yet the attacker's whole body can still continue moving and extending far past that breaking point, illustrating how much mechanical advantage is in reserve.
- This mechanical alignment explains how the historical man Kimura was able to apply such impressive bone-breaking pressure on opponents: a controlling, strong, rigorously applied entry that landed him exactly on the diagonal line of power, letting him snap arms "like matchsticks" simply by obeying these mechanical principles.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The finishing sequence flows directly from the bar-arm setup: elbow to elbow, hand to the spine (posterior hand position), then drive down to the mat.
- Beat the opponent's shoulder to the floor, spread your feet 90 degrees, and bring your head down to his hips as the elbow closes — this is what "dominates the power line."
- Landing position after the takedown: knee forward, elbow down, legs stepped across his body, head over his hips, elbow to his elbow — then pull the dominant kimura.
- The single mechanical key to a devastating kimura: align the entire body along the diagonal line of power from head through hips to the locked elbow.
- Correct alignment means your body can keep moving well past the opponent's actual breaking point — this is the mechanism behind Kimura's historical reputation for breaking arms from this exact position.
- This is an extremely effective way to take a strongly resisting opponent from defensive turtle, tilt him onto his back, and go directly into one of the most punishing breaks in the sport.
