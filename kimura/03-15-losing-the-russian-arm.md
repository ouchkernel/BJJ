# Losing The Russian Arm

## Overview

A realistic look at what happens when the Russian arm control from the T-kimura position fails against a strong, sweaty, or explosive opponent. Rather than treating this as a dead end, the chapter identifies the exact structural condition that allows an opponent to pull the arm free — and shows that this same condition always hands you access to his other arm. The chapter ends by pointing toward the specialized arm lock (udigatami) that picks up where the lost Russian arm leaves off.

---

## The mechanical truth about losing the Russian arm

- Starting position: T-kimura lock-up, penetrating with the bottom knee as the opponent explodes up from bottom, butterfly hook in to hold him.
- Against a sufficiently strong, slippery opponent, he can and sometimes will pull the arm free even from a tight-feeling Russian arm control.
- **The only way the Russian arm can be lost is if the opponent posts his second arm on the floor.**
  - If that second hand stays off the floor, he has no way to generate a pull against the trapped arm — no matter how strong or slippery he is, the arm can be held.
  - The instant he posts the second hand on the mat, he can push with that hand while pulling with the trapped arm, generating enough force to rip free.

## The trade you always get

- Because escaping the Russian arm *requires* posting the second arm on the floor, losing the Russian arm always means gaining access to the second arm — it has to be that way, or the opponent could never have escaped the first grip in the first place.
- So when the opponent explosively pulls away and frees the Russian arm, immediately shift attention to the arm he just posted.

## Transition sequence

1. From T-kimura, opponent explodes up hard from bottom; hook the butterfly hook in and hold.
2. Opponent violently pulls the Russian arm free by posting his other hand on the mat.
3. Post your own bottom knee's foot on the mat and turn your whole body out to the other side.
4. From here, transition into a specialized arm lock called udigatami, attacking the arm he was forced to post.

---

## Key Takeaways

- You can never lose the Russian arm to a slippery/strong opponent unless he posts his second hand on the floor — no post, no escape.
- Losing the Russian arm is not a failure state — it is a guaranteed trade for access to the opponent's other arm.
- The recovery sequence is simple: post your bottom foot, turn your body to the other side, and transition into udigatami on the newly posted arm.
