# Switching To Armbar And Triangle

## Overview

The central problem of the kimura system is stated plainly: how do you take the opponent's hand from a strong anterior (front) position to a weak posterior (behind-the-back) position when he has a very strong grip (hand-to-hand, hand-inside-thigh, hand-on-belt)? The first response was incorporating the legs (cross kimura, kimura plata, trimura). This lesson introduces a second response: give up on the kimura submission itself and use it purely as a restraining hold to switch to an alternative submission — juji gatame (the armbar), which can further flow into a triangle.

---

## The core idea: kimura's two faces

- Kimura has two faces: it can be an incredible means of breaking someone's arm, and it can be an incredible means of restraining someone's movement.
- This lesson uses the second face — restraint — as a bridge to a different finishing hold rather than forcing the kimura submission itself.

## Switching to three-quarter juji gatame

1. Start in a dorsal kimura position where the opponent is giving extreme resistance.
2. Switch arms: come through with the left arm elbow-to-elbow, then the right arm elbow-to-elbow.
3. Put a hand on the floor in line with the training partner's hips.
4. Swivel the feet to the outside so a foot can come up.
5. Bring the two knees up and walk in, then immediately re-lock the kimura hand grip.

### Why the arm must stay locked

- Re-locking the kimura here without care risks a turning escape: if the opponent's arm can extend, it's easy for his body to turn away and get up.
- With the kimura locked, his arm cannot extend. Even if he works all the way up to his knees, it means nothing — the kimura will always pull him back to where he came from.

### Finishing the armbar

1. Once the opponent is forced to lock his hands defensively, thread your foot through underneath his neck.
2. This produces the three-quarter juji gatame: the foot reinforces at the head rather than sitting extended.
3. When you separate the training partner's hands and he tries to turn his body out, he can only turn so far — even getting up toward his knees doesn't free him, because head-and-shoulder control lets you straighten the arm out into a strong finish.

### The common counter-response, and the answer

- Once opponents catch on to this sequence, the common response is to come up hard into you.
- Counter: draw in the second arm and kick the legs into place. Put a knee behind the training partner's head as a wedge. As he comes up onto his knees, lock the figure-of-four and transition into senkaku (the triangle) from the front for the finish.

## Switching to a front triangle (senkaku) directly

1. Start in front of the training partner in a conventional kimura position where the arm can't be secured.
2. Try to separate the arms via a switching-elbow method. Keep the hands up throughout — the lower your hands, the more the opponent can swim his arms around.
3. Bring the thumb up to your own sternum, put the hand down on the floor, pick the knee up, and sit down onto the training partner with good control.
4. Go elbow to elbow and pin his hand to his own ribcage — even without going for a submission yet, this alone functions as a restraining hold (kimura's "restraining" face again).
5. Pass a foot through. Options: a regular juji gatame, or the preferred three-quarter juji gatame by feeding the leg through and drawing the feet back onto the neck rather than extending the legs.
6. From the three-quarter position, when the opponent tries to turn out of the lock, his head and shoulders are well constrained — unlike a regular juji gatame, where it's comparatively easy for him to get up.
7. If the opponent is smart and locks his hands to defend the armbar, finishing by that method becomes difficult. Response: hold the opposite elbow and give him the opportunity to come up on top. As he does, he finds everything wedged in by the tight senkaku, and from here the figure-of-four locks up easily for the finish.

## Why this matters

- This is presented as the second major strategy for the central kimura problem, alongside incorporating the legs: rather than forcing the kimura through, use it as a restraint and pivot into juji gatame and/or the front triangle.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Kimura's restraining function (not just its breaking function) is the key to this strategy — use it to hold the opponent in place while you switch to a different submission.
- To set up three-quarter juji gatame: switch arms elbow-to-elbow, walk the knees in, re-lock the kimura grip before threading the foot through — the locked kimura prevents the arm extension that would otherwise let the opponent turn-escape.
- Three-quarter juji gatame (foot reinforcing at the head, feet drawn back rather than extended) constrains the head and shoulders far better than a regular armbar, stopping the opponent from simply standing up out of it.
- When the opponent comes up hard in response, draw in the second arm, wedge a knee behind his head, and transition to a front triangle (senkaku) as he rises to his knees.
- If the opponent locks his hands to defend the armbar, let him come up on top deliberately — the tight triangle wedges him in place and the figure-of-four finish becomes easy from there.
