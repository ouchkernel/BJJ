# Rolling Through The Kimura Scramble

## Overview

One of the essential skills of the entire kimura game: what to do when you've successfully trapped the opponent's hand behind his back but haven't pinned him down, and he starts scrambling his hips and back up off the floor. The central idea is that the hand-behind-the-back grip is such an enormous mechanical advantage that it outweighs positional concerns entirely — you can willingly give up top position, let the opponent roll you over, and still come out the other side in the same dominant pin with the kimura intact.

---

## The scenario

- Starting point: side kimura position, working to take the opponent's back off the floor as part of the attack (per the "beating the shoulder" sequence).
- Risk: as the opponent's back comes off the floor, if his head isn't kept down, he can sit up rapidly with you and you lose control of the pin — but the hand may still be trapped behind his back.
- This is a very common scenario — the more you practice kimura, the more often you'll find yourself here.

## The core principle: hand behind the back beats the pin

- As long as the opponent's hand stays behind his back, you dominate the scramble — have confidence in this.
- If you lose the pin but keep the hand behind the back, you are still fine. The hand behind the back takes precedence over the pin.
- Common mistake: panicking when the pin is lost and scrambling back to a neutral position. In a truly neutral position neither person has an advantage, and it becomes a battle for head height — if his head gets higher than yours, you end up going backwards and his hips move away, landing you back in a genuinely neutral (i.e., wasted) position.
- It didn't have to be that way — the hand behind the back was never actually lost.

## What to avoid

- Don't let go of the arm to fight for the pin — this puts you in an unstable position, and even if you end up on top, the opponent likely recovers his hips and you're back to neutral.
- Don't stay on the same side of his body as he rises — he can cover you there and work his hand back around to the front, which is a disaster (a hand that gets back in front is no longer trapped).

## The roll-through sequence

1. As the opponent's head/shoulders rise and you start losing the pin, don't panic and don't fight to stay on the same side.
2. Bring your head to his elbow; exaggerate the loss of the pin — jackknife your hips and put your head down, making it easier for him to come up. Willingly give up bottom side control.
3. Go around the corner and out of his path rather than staying on his side — this keeps the hand from swinging back in front of him.
4. Turn everything to your left (guide the roll in your desired direction, not his) and take him over.
5. Come up with his head covered by your leg, so when he tries to continue rolling, his head can't move further.
6. Land back in the same pin, positioned right on the opponent's diagonal line of power, with the kimura intact — hand still behind the back, leg over the head, shoulder beaten.
7. From here, apply the pull-dominant finishing mechanics covered earlier.

## Why this works

- With the hand behind the back, the opponent is in such a mechanically weak position that normal positional hierarchy in jiu-jitsu stops applying.
- You can willingly give up bottom side control, or even throw your own back to the mat and let him land in top side control — situations that would normally be disastrous — because his trapped hand neutralizes his advantage.
- If the opponent tries to come up to his knees and pin you from this "bad" bottom position, you can simply break his arm from underneath — he has to keep rolling through, at the cost of his arm, rather than stopping to consolidate.
- When he does roll through, you're already positioned with your leg tripodded over his head, located right on his power line — ready for the pull-dominant finish.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The hand behind the back is worth more than the pin — as long as you keep it, you're winning the scramble no matter how the positional picture looks.
- Don't panic and let go to fight for a neutral position when the pin is lost — that trades a winning position for a genuinely even one.
- Don't stay on the same side as the opponent rises — his hand can swing back to the front from there.
- Roll-through steps: exaggerate the loss (head to his elbow, jackknife hips), go around the corner and out of his path, turn him to your guiding direction, cover his head with your leg as you come up, land back in the same pin on his diagonal line of power.
- Because the trapped hand nullifies normal positional rules, you can safely surrender bottom side control (or worse) mid-scramble — if he tries to consolidate on top instead of rolling through, you can just break the arm from underneath.
- This roll-through skill recurs across many kimura positions, not just the side kimura — expect to need it constantly as you develop the system.
