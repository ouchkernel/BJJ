# Trimura — The Triangle-Kimura Hybrid (Trap Triangle And Hantai Sankaku)

## Overview

Taught in the basement with Ethan Crelinson and Frankie Rosenthal. "Trimura" is the hybrid between the triangle and the Kimura: using a triangle configuration from bottom position to lock up the opponent's head and arm together, which makes the Kimura significantly easier to finish once the head is heavily controlled. Two main leg configurations are shown from an elbow two-on-one grip — the trap triangle and the Hantai Sankaku (a triangle locked the "wrong way," with no strangling intent) — both of which line the opponent's arm up on the power line for a strong break.

---

## The core idea

- Triangles from bottom position aren't only used to lock up the opponent's head and arm for their own sake — doing so creates a much stronger Kimura attack.
- Once the head is heavily controlled by the leg configuration, the Kimura becomes significantly easier to finish from bottom position.
- Both variations start from the same grip: an **elbow two-on-one** — the legs come over the top and trap the head and the arm together, similar to having a closed guard wrapped around the opponent's head and arm.

## Option one: the trap triangle

- Ankles are simply crossed.
- Invert the right knee, pointing it down toward the floor with the foot pointed up toward the ceiling.
- Result: excellent control of both the head and the arm, producing a very strong breaking finish.
- Live demo (Frankie Rosenthal): starts in what looks like a trap triangle off the elbow two-on-one grip trapping the head and arm, inverts the right knee, crosses the ankles — a clean trap-triangle finish follows, with an extreme bend visible in the defender's arm that creates a very strong break.

## Option two: Hantai Sankaku (triangle locked the wrong way)

- From the same elbow two-on-one starting grip, the leg configuration changes: instead of the trap triangle, the legs are reconfigured to lock the Hantai Sankaku.
- Grip the opponent's head so you can pivot off his head and shoulders — this is what allows the Hantai Sankaku to be locked.
- There is no intention to strangle in this configuration — it's used purely for head-and-arm control.
- Important detail: the feet lock in front of the opponent's (near) shoulder — this maximizes control of his head.
- Once the Kimura is locked from here, everything lines up along the power line, producing a very strong finish.
- Full sequence: initial grip → legs fire over the top → control the head → pivot off the shoulders (feet in front of the shoulder is the key detail) → transfer the hand to the outside → lock the Kimura. Result is described as a punishing breaking position that's very hard for the opponent to stop.
- Live demo (Frankie Rosenthal, second alternative): locks a full Hantai Sankaku instead of the trap triangle, giving stronger control of the head and arm and a very powerful break.
- Ethan Crelinson is noted as often using this move in competition — an easy, relaxed lock of the legs that produces a very tight Kimura.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Trimura = triangle-from-bottom used as a head-and-arm control tool that sets up a much easier, stronger Kimura finish, not just a strangle attempt.
- Both variations share the same starting point: an elbow two-on-one grip with the legs coming over the top to trap the head and arm, like a closed guard around them.
- Trap triangle: ankles simply crossed, right knee inverted (foot to ceiling, knee to floor) — strong, straightforward control and break.
- Hantai Sankaku: a triangle locked the wrong way, with no strangling intent — grip the head to pivot off the shoulders, and critically, lock the feet in front of the opponent's shoulder to maximize head control before finishing the Kimura along the power line.
- Ethan Crelinson uses the Hantai Sankaku version regularly in competition, describing it as an easy, relaxed leg lock that produces a very tight finish.
