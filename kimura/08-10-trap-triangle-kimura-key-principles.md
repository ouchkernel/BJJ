# Trap Triangle Kimura: Key Principles

## Overview

One more grip-fighting entry (elbow post and wrist), followed by the underlying principles that explain why the trap triangle Kimura is such an effective attack from bottom position — and why it fits naturally into the broader Kimura system's rules about head control, the power line, and arm-bend mechanics.

---

## Additional Entry: Elbow Post and Wrist

- Sometimes the simplest option is to just hit an elbow post and control the wrist directly.
- Control the elbow post, control the wrist.
- From this initial control, put feet on the hips, kick the legs all the way up, and drag the opponent forward.
- Pass the wrist to the outside — the hand-fighting starts with the right hand and passes to the left hand.
- Shimmy, turn, and lock — everything comes up to the power line for the finish.
- This is another example of how simple gripping sequences from seated guard funnel easily into the trap triangle position.

## Why the Trap Triangle Position Is So Effective

- **It puts you directly on the power line** — the preferred bracing position from bottom position.
- **It gives direct control of the opponent's head**, unlike a closed guard, where head control is minimal and the opponent can easily posture up and escape.
- These two factors together are what make the trap triangle Kimura sequence from bottom so effective.

## Alignment With the General Kimura System

The trap triangle entry is fully consistent with the broader principles used throughout the Kimura system:

- Controlling the head.
- Bracing/finishing from a specified power line.
- Mechanically getting the arm into a good bend position to work from.

Following these principles consistently is what drives up finishing percentage from bottom position.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Elbow post and wrist control is another valid entry: control elbow + wrist, kick legs up, drag opponent forward, pass wrist outside, shimmy/turn/lock to the power line.
- The trap triangle's effectiveness from bottom comes down to two structural advantages: it puts you on the power line, and it gives real head control that closed guard lacks.
- These advantages are not unique tricks — they're direct applications of the Kimura system's general rules: control the head, brace on the power line, and get a good bend in the arm.
- Consistently applying these principles is what raises finishing percentage from bottom position across the whole system, not just this one move.
