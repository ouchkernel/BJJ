# Beating The Seat Belt — The Hand-Race Cheat

## Overview

Kimura from bottom turtle is normally a bad matchup once an opponent has fully locked a seat belt (one of the most common and effective back-control positions in jiu-jitsu) — the position is too dominant to fight out of with a kimura attempt. But there is a brief window before the hands actually connect and lock, and that window can be exploited. The method is to "cheat" the race to grip a moment before it starts, using the fact that you already know exactly where your opponent wants his hands to go.

---

## The problem

- Once an opponent's seat belt is fully locked — head over your head, hands connected in front of your clavicles — it is not a good time to attack with kimura. The position controls too well.
- There is, however, a short time frame before the opponent gets his hands locked where you can still work.

## The cheat: starting ahead of the race

- In any competitive race, it's easier to win if you start ahead of your opponent.
- You already know what your opponent wants: to connect his hands in front of your clavicles.
- So before he gets there, put your own hand on your own clavicle first — occupying the exact spot his hand wants to reach.
- Protect your chin like a boxer: chin down, jaw connected to your own fingers. If your head were up naively, he could shoot straight through to a stranglehold.
- When he goes to connect his hands, his hand runs directly into yours instead — you direct his hand into your grip and get wrist control almost automatically.

## From wrist control to a locked kimura

1. The instant you get wrist control, climb for height so your head rises above his and your elbow rises above his elbow.
2. From that height advantage, lock in a one-handed kimura.
3. Put your hand down to the floor and whip his elbow around in front of you.
4. This lands you in the perfect position across your training partner's side.
5. Get a hold of his wrist as he comes up to try to complete his roll-through — he comes up directly into simple leg control.
6. Bring everything up to the shoulder line, turn your base, and you're in the perfect roll-through kimura, ready for a bone-breaking finish.

## Second scenario — mirrored side, opponent tries to lock hands anyway

- Same trick: protect the chin, thumb on your own clavicle, direct the opponent's hand into your grip before he can connect his own hands.
- If he goes to collect his hands together, bring them apart instead, then climb for height (head above his, elbow above his elbow) into the one-handed kimura.
- **If he's naive and lets go of his hands**: you already have the height advantage — lock up and immediately put him down to the floor, attacking from there.
- **If he does lock his hands anyway**: turn back into him and work him into a severe position; there's no reason this defense fails just because he commits to the lock.
- From either branch, put your hand down on the floor, turn your knees up, and whip your training partner over. As he goes over, put your leg over his leg and get your hand behind his back, following him through the roll. You end up directly on the power line, ready to apply the bone-breaking finish.

## Key Takeaways

- Kimura versus a *fully locked* seat belt is a bad matchup — this technique only works in the narrow window before the opponent connects his hands.
- The core cheat: pre-position your own hand on your own clavicle (where his hand is headed) and protect your chin like a boxer, so his hand runs into your grip instead of connecting his own.
- The moment you get wrist control, climb for height — head above his, elbow above his elbow — before locking the kimura.
- The technique works whether the opponent is naive (lets go of his hands, gets taken down immediately) or commits to locking his hands anyway (you turn in and roll him regardless).
- The finish is a roll-through kimura, ending directly on the power line for a bone-breaking finish.
