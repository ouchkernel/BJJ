# The Head And Arm Kimura

## Overview

States the organizing theme of the whole kimura system — the position of the opponent's hand (in front of the body, or behind the back) determines the direction everything takes — and then introduces the head and arm kimura, a move used when the opponent's hand simply cannot be physically forced behind the back. It's simple, highly effective, and doubles as a bridge from the kimura system into the back system, since it generates back exposure. The lesson runs through a full sequence starting from a stalled side-control kimura, through unweighted legs and hand separation, to a rear triangle configuration that is simultaneously a devastating kimura.

---

## The organizing principle: front hand vs. back hand

- What creates the direction of the kimura system is the position of the opponent's hand: is it in front of the body, or is it behind the back?
- If the hand goes behind the back, you typically go straight for the finish — kimura as an actual submission hold.
- If the hand stays in front, kimura is typically used as a controlling method — pinning the opponent's arm and setting up other positions or submissions — or the legs are incorporated to actually force the hand behind the back.

## When to use the head and arm kimura

- Used when you feel you cannot physically get the opponent's hand behind the back — for example, when you can't control the head properly, or the opponent has a "death grip" locking his own hands together that you can't separate no matter what method you try, or you're worried about stepping over into a leg-based kimura because of limited flexibility.
- It's incredibly simple to use and stunningly effective — one of the most important additions to the overall kimura game.
- Bonus: it generates back exposure and lets you switch from the kimura system into the back system entirely.

## Sequence from side control

1. Starting point: a basic kimura on the training partner, arm controlled but you can't get it behind the back.
2. Take your pulling hand, lock around, and take a reverse grip on the opponent's arm so your shoulder lands directly behind his elbow. The closer your shoulder is to his elbow, the harder it is for him to turn the elbow back in and slip inside your arm — the further away, the easier it is for him to escape that way.
3. Drive everything forward: put your hand on the floor and rotate around, placing your bicep behind his head.
4. Drive forward toward the opposite elbow. This locks a kimura that now incorporates the head as well as the arm — significantly harder for the opponent to turn into you.
5. If he tries to turn in, extend your elbow forward and keep your head in front of his. Sitting back on your heels lets him turn into you and lets you lose the head — so keep both elbow and head driving forward.

## Getting to unweighted legs and separating locked hands

1. With the head and arm locked, you can easily sit behind the training partner even if his legs are up defensively — it means nothing.
2. Shift your weight and pass your foot all the way through, hooking it on the training partner's far hip.
3. Lift and bring the second leg out to create **unweighted legs** — both feet free of weight-bearing duty.
4. Even if the opponent locks his hands tight defensively, it doesn't matter: put your feet around his wrist and separate his hands, then put a foot into his armpit.
5. From here, bring the training partner's hand overhead and lock a rear triangle configuration (ushiro senkaku) around his head and arm.

## The rear-triangle-kimura hybrid

- What looks like a strangle hold from this position is also a devastating kimura position: put his knuckles outside your quadricep, then lift the elbow for a very strong kimura finish.
- Because the head can't move at all in this configuration, the kimura has a much greater breaking effect over a much smaller distance.

## Alternate entry recap (starting from a stalled kimura)

1. Start with a kimura that's stalled — no confidence left that the legs can enact the kimura or that the hands can be separated to get the arm behind the back.
2. Turn, put the bicep in behind the head, lock a figure-four grip, and sit down to the mat.
3. Pass a foot across, bring the second leg in — two unweighted feet.
4. Even as the opponent locks his hands as tight as possible, use the strength of your legs to separate his hands.
5. Incorporate the legs as part of the kimura lock: fall over to your side, lock the figure four. This produces essentially a half-triangle, half-kimura (a "trimura") — hands outside the training partner's quadricep, generating very strong kimura attacks.

## Why this sequence matters

- It's a complete demonstration of failure-recovery: you start with a failed kimura (couldn't get the hand behind the back), switch to a head and arm kimura, use that to enter the back system, lock up a triangle (you could finish with a strangle from here), and then take it back to a kimura — now a kimura with the head and arm controlled by the legs.
- This runs through the keynote themes of the whole system: using the legs to generate breaking power, and controlling the opponent's head throughout.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The single organizing principle of the kimura system: hand behind the back → go for the finish; hand in front → use kimura as control/pin, or use the legs to force it behind the back.
- The head and arm kimura is the answer when you physically cannot get the opponent's hand behind his back — it's simple, effective, and opens a path into the back system.
- Keep your shoulder tight behind his elbow and drive your elbow and head forward — sitting back lets him turn in and lose the position.
- Unweighted legs (both feet free) let you separate even a tightly locked defensive grip and set up a rear-triangle configuration that is simultaneously a strong kimura.
- This sequence is a model for the whole system's philosophy: when one kimura entry fails, use the legs and head control to convert into another attack rather than abandoning the position.
