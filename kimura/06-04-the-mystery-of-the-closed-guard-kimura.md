# The Mystery Of The Closed Guard Kimura

## Overview

An opening argument, not a technique breakdown: why the classic first-day-of-jiu-jitsu closed guard kimura — despite being mechanically simple enough for a complete beginner to hit — is almost never seen finished at world championship level. The chapter frames this as "the great mystery" of the position, walks through the traditional beginner setup, and diagnoses exactly which three elements defeat it, setting up the better setups taught in the following chapters.

---

## The mystery, framed

- Nearly every move taught in a beginner's first two or three weeks of jiu-jitsu — the elbow escape, the upa, basic guard passes, the armbar from guard, basic strangles/collar strangles from the back — remains a staple used for an entire career, appearing constantly at every level from the gym to the black belt world championships.
- The closed guard kimura is the one glaring exception. It is taught on day one as a classic example of using leverage from bottom position, yet it is almost never seen finished successfully at the elite level — maybe five to ten times across 25 years of top competition, compared to literally tens of thousands of successful examples of the other basic moves.
- The one clearly remembered elite example cited: Jacaré defeating Marcelo Garcia with a closed guard kimura — notable specifically because of how rare a successful instance is, not just because of the athletes involved.
- The diagnosis offered up front: kimura itself is not the problem — it works constantly in every other context (gi, no-gi, MMA). The problem is specifically the traditional closed-guard *setup* (and, by extension, a push-based finish), not the hold itself.

## The traditional beginner setup

1. Opponent has inside position on your hands (hands on your biceps), holding you down.
2. Pommel through to beat his hands.
3. Bring his hands to the floor.
4. Open your guard to start moving.
5. Fake his head one direction so he follows, then use the momentum of your arms to move up past his head on the other side.
6. Lock the kimura grip, taking advantage of his hands now being on the floor.
7. Move out to the side and attempt to finish.

## Why it fails against a resisting elite opponent

Three separate elements are each independently capable of stopping the whole sequence:

- **Hands** — if the opponent gets a hand back on your chest, you cannot get up at all.
- **Biceps** — even if you beat his hands, once you come up to the side he can walk around your waist and return you straight back down to the mat by controlling the bicep/arm.
- **Head** — if he follows your head movement with his own head, his head arrives in front of you and physically stops you from sitting up.
- The traditional method relies on momentum and speed to beat all three simultaneously — but a smart, aware opponent only needs small movements to shut down a fast-moving attacker, no matter how quickly you try to move. Speed doesn't solve a three-front problem.

## The reframed diagnosis

- It is not the kimura hold that's ineffective — it breaks arms constantly in every other setting (MMA, gi, no-gi).
- It is the traditional momentum-based setup that is failing, because it forces the attacker to fight through hands, biceps, and head all at once, using only speed as the answer.
- Conclusion: closed guard kimura needs a fundamentally better setup than the traditional whip-the-body-up-and-lock approach — that is the subject of the following chapters (wrist control and power probing).

## Key Takeaways

- Every other beginner-level jiu-jitsu move (elbow escape, upa, armbar from guard, basic back strangles, basic guard passing) remains a lifelong, high-percentage tool used at every level of competition — closed guard kimura is the sole exception, landing successfully only a handful of times across 25 years at the elite level.
- Kimura as a submission is proven and effective everywhere else; the failure is isolated to this one setup.
- Three separate defenses — hands, biceps, and head — can each independently stop the traditional momentum-based setup, and speed alone cannot beat all three at once against an aware opponent.
- The fix has to be a better setup method, not a different finishing hold — this sets up the wrist-control-based approach covered next.
