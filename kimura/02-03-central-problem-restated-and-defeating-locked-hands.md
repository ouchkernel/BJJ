# Central Problem Restated & Defeating Locked Hands

## Overview

Restates the central problem of the kimura system in its clearest form, catalogs the ways an opponent reinforces a threatened arm, and gives the first concrete counter: two variations for defeating an opponent who locks his own hands together to protect the kimura arm.

---

## The central problem, restated

- For any position in jiu-jitsu there is at least one central problem motivating both athletes' actions. For the kimura, it is: how do you take the opponent's hand and put it into a mechanically weak position behind his back?
- The opponent knows that his hand going behind the back is a serious problem, so he will do everything he can to keep his hands in front, in a mechanically strong position.

## How opponents reinforce the threatened hand

- Locking the hands together.
- Grabbing their own clothing.
- Putting the hand inside their own thigh.
- Simply placing the hand on their own opposite hip.
- All of these are effective ways of reinforcing the threatened arm.
- If the arm is unsupported, it's easy to put it behind the back. If it's supported by a thigh, clothing, hip, stomach, or another hand, it becomes very difficult.
- Reality check: as you face better and better opponents, you will frequently be unable to get the hand behind the back at all — that scenario needs its own set of tools (addressed later). Even so, there are simple early tricks that can convert a reinforced hand into a hand behind the back.

## Defeating locked hands

- Starting point: dorsal kimura position, opponent locks his own hands together tightly.
- First diagnostic: check the position of the opponent's bottom elbow.
  - **Elbow locked tight to the body** (no space between bicep and ribs) — one problem, one solution.
  - **Elbow exposed** (space between elbow and ribs) — a different problem, different solution.

### Elbow tight, no space (method one)

1. Hold elbow to elbow with one hand; pinch the opponent's elbow tight to your armpit and chest.
2. You can periodically release the "push hand," but only if the "pull hand" locks things in and frames — don't leave space, or the opponent can withdraw the whole arm and escape.
3. Post the free hand on the floor and place your knee in and over the training partner's bicep.
4. Relock, then use the wedge of your shin to lift the training partner's elbow up and over the shoulder line.
5. Put the knuckles in behind the back — perfect position.
6. Post the elbow on the floor, beat the shoulder, and arrive in a strong finishing position.

### Elbow tucked in, no room for the knee (method two — the obvious counter)

- Obvious counter to method one: the opponent tucks his elbow in tight so there's no space for your knee to work.
1. Unlock your hand, thread it through and around, and place it on the opponent's thigh.
2. Lever his arm out to deliberately expose the bicep.
3. Once exposed, apply the same mechanism as before: shin wedge pushes down, pulling hand lifts up, taking the hand behind the scapula.
4. Come down to an elbow, sit through, and arrive in a strong finishing position.

## Reading the opponent's sophistication

- A naive opponent who leaves the arm exposed requires little work — just come in, unlock, place the knee, and go.
- A more tactically aware opponent who keeps everything tight requires threading the hand through the kimura grip itself to open the space needed to work.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Central problem of the kimura system, precisely stated: get the opponent's hand into a mechanically weak position behind his back, against his determination to keep it reinforced in front.
- Four common reinforcement tactics: locking own hands, grabbing own clothing, hand inside own thigh, hand on own hip — all work by supporting the arm so it can't be pulled free.
- Against locked hands, first check the bottom elbow: tight-to-body vs. exposed dictates which counter to use.
- Elbow tight → knee wedge over the bicep, shin lifts the elbow over the shoulder line, hand to scapula.
- Elbow tucked with no knee room → thread the hand through, lever on the thigh to expose the bicep, then apply the same shin-wedge mechanism.
- Never leave space when you release a hand to work — the pull hand must lock and frame, or the opponent withdraws the whole arm.
