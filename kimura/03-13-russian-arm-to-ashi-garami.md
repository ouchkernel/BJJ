# Russian Arm To Ashi Garami

## Overview

Shows how the same Russian arm control built out of T-kimura can be redirected into an ashi garami entry instead of a pin. The chapter is built around two load-bearing control details — never coming up head-to-head, and holding at the shoulder rather than lower on the arm — both of which exist specifically to stop the opponent from yanking the trapped arm free.

---

## Why this works from T-kimura

- T-kimura gives excellent control over the opponent — enough that turning up into you is really the only thing he can do.
- The same Russian arm used for pinning can instead be used to enter ashi garami.

## Entry sequence

1. As your opponent turns up into you, it's a battle for elbow position — bring your knee inside.
2. As he starts coming up, or gets all the way up to his base, take your outside hook and let it become a butterfly hook.
3. Even if he brings his elbow inside, you still have forearm-to-forearm contact/control.

## The critical control details

- **Don't just stay on your back.** If you sit passively and he viciously pulls his arm, he's gone.
- Instead, immediately hook behind his leg — this is part of what stops the escape.
- As he pulls away, bring your head to the **opposite shoulder**. Never come up head-to-head — that's a training disaster.
- Grip placement matters: holding low (near the hand) lets him yank out easily. Holding **at the shoulder** — pulling his shoulder under your armpit — makes it shockingly difficult for him to yank out.

## Finishing the entry

1. Bring him up with two legs.
2. Once his hips are over your hips, relinquish one leg.
3. Start turning his body so he turns out in the direction you want.
4. Catch your training partner and end up in the perfect position to hold and block.
5. From there, transition into your finishes (the ashi garami entry itself).

---

## Key Takeaways

- The Russian arm from T-kimura is a branch point: it can lead to a pin (previous chapter) or to an ashi garami entry (this chapter).
- Outside hook → butterfly hook is the leg mechanic that starts the transition.
- Never sit passively on your back holding the arm — an opponent who pulls violently will simply rip free unless you're actively hooking behind his leg.
- Never come up head-to-head; always bring your head to the opposite shoulder as he pulls away.
- Grip location is what actually prevents the escape: hold at the shoulder (pulling it under your armpit), not down near the hand.
- Finish by lifting with two legs, relinquishing one leg once his hips clear yours, and turning his body into a hold/block position that opens into your finishing sequence.
