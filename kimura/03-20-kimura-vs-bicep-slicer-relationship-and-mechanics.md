# Kimura Vs. Bicep Slicer: Relationship And Mechanics

## Overview

A dedicated look at the relationship between the kimura (a joint attack) and the bicep slicer (an attack on muscle fibers, and in extreme cases the bones of the forearm), since the two moves are close enough in mechanics and position that any serious kimura instructional needs to address the connection. The bicep slicer is framed as the answer for situations where you have a kimura grip but can't generate a pulling-dominant kimura — most often because the opponent has kept his hands locked together in front of him and you can't get his back off the floor.

---

## When to reach for the bicep slicer

- Starting point: side kimura position, opponent down, kimura grip secured — but you're struggling to bring his back off the ground to apply a pulling-dominant kimura.
- Common cause: he's strong and keeps his hands locked together in front of him, so you can't strip the hands to the floor.
- In this situation, punch through and go over the top to lock your own bicep instead of forcing the classical kimura finish.

## Core grip mechanics of the bicep slicer

1. Take your pulling/frame arm and bring the wrist up so it goes **over the top** of the opponent's forearm — never let your wrist sit lower than his forearm.
2. Curl the wrist around and over so it sits directly on top of his forearm. This wrist position alone already creates significant pressure.
3. Instead of taking the hand to the opponent's wrist (the conventional "push hand" position), flip the hand through and reach for your own arm.
4. Grip choice matters: the conventional teaching is to grab your own elbow — this works but is not optimal. The stronger option is to lift the elbow and go further through to grab your own **shoulder**. The moment you switch from an elbow grip to a shoulder grip, the hold tightens dramatically.

## Why elbow-to-elbow and the shoulder line matter

- Moving your elbow forward creates an elbow-to-elbow relationship with the opponent — already established as central to kimura success, and equally central to bicep slicer success.
- Reaching up to the opponent's shoulder line is likewise a general position of strength.
- Practical sequence: release your grip on your own elbow, re-establish elbow-to-elbow position, then reinforce by grabbing his elbow.

## Walking around the corner

1. Your hips typically start on one side of the opponent's head.
2. Walk your body around the corner to place your hips on the *other* side of his head.
3. From this position, pressure is applied directly onto the bones of the forearm.
4. Even if the opponent locks his hands together defensively, it's extremely difficult for him to prevent the finish — this is a highly intimidating submission, felt as the forearm bones bending under stress, with real danger of breaking.

## The lever principle — why walking past the centerline matters

- If you stay on the near side (elbow grip only, not walked around), you can still put "pretty scary" pressure and finish plenty of opponents — that's a perfectly good stopping point.
- But your own elbow is far from the end of the lever in that position.
- Moving around the corner, sweeping your elbow to meet his, and filling the gap with your hand on his shoulder brings you much closer to the end of the lever.
- Walking all the way past his centerline produces even more pressure — a severe load on both the forearm bones and the surrounding muscle.

## Application from a different entry (never releasing the grip)

1. Scenario: strong opponent, kimura locked, but you can't separate his hands (e.g., from a dorsal/back-mount-adjacent position).
2. Punch through and lock over the top onto your own bicep, using the same over-the-top wrist position.
3. Critical detail: never naively let go of the grip mid-transition and let the opponent yank his arms toward the floor to escape.
4. Instead, thread your hand straight through without ever releasing his arm — post your hand on the floor, move your body around the corner, bring your elbow to his elbow, and grab his shoulder.
5. Once at the shoulder line, breaking pressure comes quickly — and even more so once you've walked past his centerline.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Bicep slicer is the answer when a kimura grip is secured but you can't get the opponent's back off the floor for a pulling-dominant kimura — typically because his hands are locked together in front.
- Wrist mechanics: your wrist must go OVER the top of his forearm, never underneath — that alone creates significant pressure.
- Grip upgrade: grab your own shoulder, not your own elbow — the elbow grip works but the shoulder grip is significantly tighter.
- Elbow-to-elbow and reaching the shoulder line are the same power principles that drive kimura success, and they drive bicep slicer success too.
- Walking your hips around the corner to the far side of the opponent's head, and past his centerline, meaningfully increases leverage and pressure — staying on the near side still finishes many opponents but leaves leverage on the table.
- Never release the grip mid-transition when threading through to lock the bicep slicer — thread straight through so the opponent can never yank his arm free and escape.
