# Why Half Guard Kimura Outperforms Closed Guard

## Overview

Switching topics from closed guard to bottom half guard kimura: despite closed guard kimura being a statistical failure over the last 20 years, half guard kimura has been strikingly successful — both in MMA and in grappling. This lesson explains the two structural reasons half guard outperforms closed guard, then covers the entry mechanics: knee shield selection, dominating the inside space, and the "hips to the hand" principle for getting the hand behind the back.

---

## Why half guard kimura works better than closed guard

Two reasons are given, one about angle and one about the opponent's psychology/tactics.

### 1. Angle

- In closed guard, both players are almost completely square to each other. The attacker has to travel a long distance to reach any kind of kimura attack.
- In half guard, the attacker very often has a favorable angle on the opponent, whose lead arm is relatively close. That angle favors going around the arm and getting a bite on the kimura.

### 2. The opponent's confidence/tactics

- When the opponent has passed one leg in half guard (knee driven forward), they can tell they're doing well — they've already beaten one leg, and freeing the other would complete the pass.
- This makes them more confident with their lead arm. They will often reach to control the head and drive passing pressure — which means they're reaching toward you.
- That reaching creates easy access to kimura attacks.

Because of this shift in angle and opponent attitude, Danaher now typically teaches kimura from bottom position starting from half guard rather than closed guard, reserving closed guard kimura entries for more advanced athletes.

## Entry mechanics

### Knee shield selection

- Preferred base: **knee shield half guard**. If the opponent achieves chest-to-chest contact, it crushes any ability to get into a successful kimura, so preventing chest-to-chest is priority one.
- No gi: keep the knee shield conservative/shallow, on the hip — reduces exposure to leg lock counters.
- With a jacket/gi: a much higher knee shield can be used, combined with lapel control and a reinforced elbow and knee.

### Controlling the inside space

1. As the opponent tries to come chest to chest, the knee wedged in the hip pocket makes it difficult.
2. Take an inside hand grip — as the opponent brings the head forward, put the elbow on one shoulder and the hand on the other, so the forearm crosses the opponent's shoulder line. This holds off incoming weight.
3. The second hand also takes an inside position. Together, the knee, elbow, and both hands tie up the entire inside space, so the opponent can't fight forward effectively.
4. From here, monitor distance with the knee shield.

### Getting to the kimura grip and the hand behind the back

1. Establish wrist control and come up to a "power prop" position — go away from the opponent's head (not over the shoulder toward it), around the corner, and lock the beginnings of a kimura.
2. **Do not just fall backward from here** — if you do, the opponent's hand goes straight back inside their own thigh and you'll have to fight it back out.
3. Instead, **bring your hips to the opponent's hand**. This makes it significantly harder for them to retract the hand back inside their thigh, and lets you quickly get the hand behind the back.
4. Getting the hand behind the back is described as a huge advantage in any kimura situation — **posterior kimuras are generally much easier to finish than anterior kimuras**; once the hand is behind the back, even strong opponents become weak.

## Practical notes

- This is the same knee-shield / inside-space-control approach repeated as a drilled sequence: dominate the inside space first, only then come up for the kimura grip.
- The "hips to the hand" adjustment (rather than falling backward) is the single detail that determines whether the hand ends up trapped behind the back or recovered safely inside the opponent's own thigh.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Half guard kimura outperforms closed guard kimura for two structural reasons: a more favorable angle (opponent's lead arm is closer, less distance to travel) and a psychological/tactical one (an opponent who feels they're winning the pass reaches more confidently, giving up the arm).
- Preventing chest-to-chest contact with a knee shield is the first priority — it's the thing that would otherwise crush the kimura attempt entirely.
- Control the entire inside space (knee, elbow, both hands) before attempting to come up for the kimura grip.
- Never just fall backward after locking the initial grip — bring the hips to the opponent's hand instead, so it can't retreat back inside the thigh.
- The goal of every kimura entry is the hand behind the back: posterior kimuras finish far more easily than anterior ones.
