# The Trinity Of Power Overview

## Overview

The transition from principles into mechanics. This lesson introduces the "trinity of power" — the three things to focus on any time you actually apply a kimura to break an arm — using a side kimura (applied from side control) as the clearest illustration. The same three elements are said to recur throughout the entire video regardless of which of the seven kimura variations or which position is being used.

---

## Starting point: the side kimura

- Kimura can be applied from a vast number of positions, covered across the rest of the video, but the inquiry starts with the **side kimura** — applied from a classic side-control cross-body position — because it's the clearest, most obvious way to illustrate the underlying principles.

## The trinity of power

1. **Control the opponent's head.**
   - Almost all of an opponent's escapes from kimura involve moving the head.
   - If the opponent frees and moves his head, he substantially reduces the amount of breaking power you can apply and can ultimately escape.
   - Controlling the head is one of the central themes worked on throughout the series.

2. **The shoulder line.**
   - The shoulder line is the straight line joining the opponent's two shoulders.
   - The further the opponent's elbow is from the shoulder line, the less pressure/tension he feels.
   - When the elbow comes up to the shoulder line — elbow, shoulder, and shoulder all in a straight line — there is radically more tension in the arm, and the break requires much less work from you.

3. **Use the legs to create pressure.**
   - This can be done many ways. One example demonstrated: when the opponent has his hands locked together and you can't get the hand behind his back, bring a leg into the kimura attack itself so the legs — not just the hands — supply the power.
   - This is one of many ways legs can contribute; even when only the hands are actively holding the kimura, the legs can be used creatively to supply power.
   - Core reframe: get away from thinking of kimura as just your hands against the opponent's arm. Instead, your hands are transmitters — the horsepower of the kimura, regardless of variety, comes from your legs and hips.

## Why the trinity matters

- These three elements — head control, shoulder line, and leg/hip power — form the underlying background to true breaking power and recur throughout every kimura variation and position covered in the rest of the course.
- Understanding this trinity from the outset is meant to deepen understanding of everything that follows.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Introduced from the side kimura (side control) because it's the clearest illustration of the underlying mechanics.
- Trinity of power: (1) control the head — nearly all escapes rely on head movement; (2) bring the elbow up to the shoulder line — elbow/shoulder/shoulder in a straight line means radically more tension for less work; (3) use the legs to supply pressure, not just the arms.
- Reframe kimura entirely: the hands are transmitters, not the power source — the real horsepower comes from the legs and hips.
- These three themes recur throughout the whole course regardless of which kimura variation or position is being used.
