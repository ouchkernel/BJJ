# Kimura Entry Vs Butterfly Guard — Height Advantage And Inside Position

## Overview

The opening application of Kimura against open guard, specifically a classic squared-up butterfly guard where the opponent has both feet in the inside position. Rather than jumping straight for the Kimura from a neutral stance, the emphasis is on first stealing inside position with one leg and establishing a height advantage — the two structural prerequisites that make the entry work. Once those are in place, the Kimura is entered off a roll and chains directly into back control.

---

## Prerequisites for the entry

- **Head position**: your head must be higher than your opponent's head.
- **Elbow position**: the elbow you're attacking with must be higher than your opponent's elbow.
- If you're level with your opponent's head height, it is almost impossible to get to the Kimura attack — you need a height advantage first.
- If your opponent has both feet in the inside position on you (as in a square butterfly guard), jumping haphazardly for the Kimura risks getting elevated and swept.

## Getting the height and stealing inside position

- Use your forearm as a buffer and come up off your ankles (don't stay seated back on your heels).
- Move to a head-to-head position with your opponent.
- Take a **thumb post** — hand inside both of the opponent's arms — as he reaches for you, so your hands/thumbs dominate the inside space.
- Pommel your knee to the inside in the same motion, switching from the thumb post to a forearm grip, establishing an inside foot to match his inside foot.
- **Height check**: is your armpit higher than the crown of your opponent's head? If not, you cannot get to the Kimura yet.
  - If it isn't, pause (call time in drilling), rise up until your armpit clears his head height.
- Once your armpit is above his head, bring your elbow across the centerline — this puts you in position to roll through.

## The roll and finish sequence

1. Complete the roll; the Kimura snaps into place as your opponent comes up on top of you.
2. Keep his elbow elevated as he fights to stand back up.
3. Bring your knee into position and thread your foot through as a hook on his hip.
4. As he continues fighting to get on top, switch and end up on his back.
5. Result: from a neutral open-guard exchange you arrive at seatbelt control and take the back.

## Alternate-angle walkthrough (same mechanics)

- Start with the thumb post, dominating the inside space so the opponent can't easily reach in.
- Step your knee to his hip.
- Take your elbow high so your armpit passes over his head, then use his reaching arm to roll through.
- By the time you land, the Kimura grip is locked in place.
- As he fights to come up, your bottom knee penetrates and your foot goes in as a hook.
- Come up behind him as he tries to move away, arriving at a position to get your hooks in.

## Practical notes

- This is described as one of the most common ways this instructor's students attack open guards, particularly butterfly guard.
- Flexibility matters — butterfly guard is fast-moving and dynamic, so this specific entry is only one of several situational tools needed (later lessons cover variations for different opponent reactions).

---

## Key Takeaways

- Two hard prerequisites before attacking Kimura from open guard: your head must be higher than your opponent's, and your attacking elbow must be higher than his.
- A simple, reliable height check: is your armpit higher than the crown of his head? If not, rise before attacking.
- Use a thumb post to dominate the inside space and steal an inside foot position rather than jumping straight for the Kimura from neutral.
- The finishing chain is fluid: Kimura snaps in off the roll, elbow stays elevated, foot hooks the hip, and the whole sequence can end in back control rather than a finished submission.
- This entry is one tool among many — expect to adapt it as the opponent's reactions change butterfly guard into a fast-moving, dynamic scramble.
