# Kimura Vs. The Double Leg — Attacking The Long Arm

## Overview

Kimura is surprisingly effective not just against the single leg but against the classic double leg takedown on two knees. The chapter introduces the long-arm/short-arm distinction created by any double leg entry, explains why the long arm — not the short arm — is always the correct side to attack, and shows the body-turning mechanic needed to survive the takedown attempt long enough to get there.

---

## The long arm / short arm concept

- Whenever an opponent enters a double leg, one of his arms will always be longer than the other, regardless of whether he's shooting below the knees, at the knees, or high above them.
- The **short arm** is on the same side as his head.
- The **long arm** reaches across your body to the opposite side.
- This is the arm you always attack — never the short-arm side.

## Why you always turn toward the long arm

- The head is already established as an impediment to the kimura — attacking the short-arm side means going straight through the opponent's head, which doesn't work.
- The opponent's driving motion pushes you in a specific direction (toward the short-arm side); turning that way plays directly into his strength.
- Turn toward the long arm instead.

## The danger of not turning in time

- If the opponent's drive is fast, it will drive your hands straight down to the floor.
- Very often he will also pull your knee and put you down to a hip — an even worse outcome.
- The fix: actively turn your body to prevent this — open your body out so your knee comes across, rather than letting the drive collapse you forward.

## Sequence: turning into the long-arm kimura

1. As the opponent drives, turn your body open so your knee comes across.
2. Access the long arm and pull his hand to your hip.
3. This makes it much harder for him to finish the double leg — his finishing strength comes from being down low, pulling the knees inward, and now you've denied him that position by bringing everything up to the hip.
4. Step so your knee goes just outside his hip.
5. Take a big step with your outside foot to line up with his feet.
6. Sit down to the mat and send him over with the same hip-throw (sumi gaeshi) action used elsewhere in the standing kimura system.
7. Bring the legs through in a scissoring motion across his body.
8. End up positioned on his power line — the standard breaking position for the finish.

## Alternate demonstration (deep double leg)

- When the opponent gets deep on the leg: turn everything to the outside to access the long arm.
- Bring the hand up to the hip.
- Step your foot in line with his feet (three feet in a line: your two plus his).
- Sit down to the floor and send him over with sumi gaeshi.
- Scissor the legs up into the strong finishing position.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Every double leg entry creates a long arm (crosses your body) and a short arm (same side as opponent's head) — always attack the long arm.
- Attacking the short-arm side means fighting through his head, which fails — the same head-as-barrier principle from the single leg material applies here.
- Turning toward the long arm also defends the takedown itself: it opens your body so your knee comes across instead of your hands getting driven to the floor or your knee getting pulled into a hip-down position.
- Once the long arm is secured, pull the hand to your hip — this denies the opponent the low, knee-pulling position where his double leg finish is strongest.
- Finish mechanic: knee outside his hip, big step to line up feet with his, sit down, send him over with a hip throw (sumi gaeshi), scissor the legs through to land on his power line for the kimura finish.
