# One-On-One Grip To Bar Arm — Turtle Kimura Setup

## Overview

The mechanical foundation for attacking the kimura against turtle position: how to take a one-on-one grip on the opponent's arm, upgrade it from a weak hand-fight into something you actually control, and convert that control into a bar arm that pins the arm across the opponent's back — the setup position from which the kimura itself is finished. Starting position is behind a training partner in a defensive turtle position.

---

## The one-on-one grip

- A one-on-one refers to any situation where your hands come through behind the opponent's armpit and secure his arm. It can be taken on either side, and you can change sides and go on the attack from either.
- **Common mistake:** the natural tendency is to just grip the wrist. This isn't entirely wrong and can have some success, but it's fairly easy for the opponent to pommel his arm around the corner and free his hand, moving away from you.

## Upgrading the grip: knuckle line, then palm-to-chest

- Instead of the wrist, control the **knuckle line**. You are never allowed to grab fingers (illegal), but you are fully allowed to grab the knuckle line itself.
- Even a knuckle-line grip alone isn't fully secure — if the opponent pommels his hand backward, he can still free it.
- The real fix: take the hand and connect his palm to his own chest. Once his palm is pinned to his chest, it becomes surprisingly difficult for him to pull the arm free — a good lock.
- Caveat: this is still just one of your hands against one of his — not inherently mechanically advantageous. Don't try to hold this grip forever; use it to generate effective motion toward the actual kimura hold.

## Generating effective motion toward the kimura

- The goal of effective motion here is simple: get past your opponent's head.
- From behind the training partner, get hold of his hand, then start moving around him and apply **two hands on one** — a genuine two-on-one control on his arm.
- Off that two-on-one, step your knee over his head and lift/lever the arm out. This creates a classic lever: one end of the lever is your forearm across the back of his shoulder and tricep, high up by the armpit; the other end is down at the wrist. This alone exerts considerable force and already resembles a kimura in shape.
- When the opponent tries to twist the arm free from this lever, it's surprisingly difficult — even more so once both hands team up to lift the hand higher than the level of his back.

## Locking in the bar arm

- The instant the hand goes higher than the back, bring your arm through and lay it as a bar across the training partner's arm.
- **Critical rule:** do not put your hand on the same side as the hand you're gripping — if you do, he can bring his elbow back down underneath himself and break the grip.
- Instead, drive your forearm across to the *opposite* shoulder. Now, when he tries to bring his arm back down, it's locked in place.
- From here it's easy to tie the arms up into a genuinely strong kimura hold.
- **The key detail:** the bar must run across the opponent's back so that when he tries to return his hand underneath himself, it's like trying to force it through your forearm bones — it simply cannot happen.
- With the bar arm secured this way, you now have a very strong ability to move the opponent around at will.

---

## Key Takeaways

- One-on-one grip = hands come through behind the armpit; don't just grip the wrist — control the knuckle line instead (fingers are illegal to grab).
- The real control point is connecting his palm to his own chest — surprisingly hard for him to pull free, though still just hand-vs-hand, not mechanically dominant on its own.
- Use the grip to generate motion, not to hold indefinitely: the goal is to get past the opponent's head via a two-hands-on-one control.
- Stepping the knee over his head and levering the arm (forearm at the shoulder/tricep, other hand at the wrist) already resembles a kimura and is very hard to twist out of.
- When bringing the bar arm across, never put your hand on the same side as the gripped hand — go to the opposite shoulder, or he can drop his elbow and break the grip.
- The bar running across his back is what makes the position unbreakable: his hand can't return underneath him without going "through" your forearm bones.
