# Recap: Bottom Knee Vs. Top Knee Attacks

## Overview

Recaps the T-kimura's role as a restraining position and uses that recap to introduce the next major branch point in the system: once you've controlled the elbow battle, you face a fundamental choice between initiating your attack with the bottom knee or the top knee, depending on how the opponent's escape attempt plays out.

---

## Recap of the restraint

- T-kimura is a position of restraint — it lets you hold your opponent down long enough to bring effective attacking options to bear on him.
- Initially, entering T-kimura is a battle for elbow position: the opponent always wants to come up on top of you, can't turn away, can't stay still, and so must turn into you.
- His ability to get up is determined entirely by elbow position — if he gets his elbow inside, or even just gets it to the mat, he has a good chance of turning up into you.
- You, meanwhile, have an excellent chance to keep him pinned by using his own elbow and forearm as a lever to put him down repeatedly, buying time to set up your own attacks.

## The fundamental choice: bottom knee or top knee

- From T-kimura you have a fundamental choice in how to attack: initiate with the **bottom knee** or the **top knee**.
- If the opponent is naive and just stays where he is, there's no reason not to simply come up on top and pin him — but a live opponent will look to turn up into you, which is what creates the choice.

### Bottom knee route

- As you control the opponent's elbow, he fights to get up — the critical point is when he comes up to a single elbow/hip base. If he goes any further, he's up to his knees, and you need to return him down (you don't want him flat on his back, since that limits your knee attacks).
- As the opponent comes up to his elbow, that creates the space needed to penetrate with the bottom knee.
- Working from the bottom knee opens up a whole host of attacking options, covered in following chapters.

### Top knee route

- The alternative: use lever and fulcrum to put the opponent down flat onto his back and shoulders.
- From there, put your own head on the floor and come across with the top knee — the top knee goes over his body, letting you come up into a good attacking position on top of him.

## The two-choice framework

- If the opponent gets up, go in with the bottom knee and run all your attacks from bottom-knee position.
- If you're able to keep the opponent's two shoulders on the mat, put your top leg over as a cross-face — this blocks his head when he tries to come up, letting you come up with the top knee into a good attacking position.
- This creates the fundamental split in the T-kimura system: bottom-knee attacks versus top-knee attacks.

---

## Key Takeaways

- T-kimura restrains via elbow control: opponent must turn into you, and his elbow position determines whether he gets up.
- Two attacking branches exist from T-kimura, chosen based on what the opponent does: bottom knee if he gets up to his elbow/base, top knee if you put him flat on his back and shoulders.
- Bottom knee penetrates the space created as he rises to a single elbow/hip; top knee comes across after using lever and fulcrum to flatten him.
- This bottom-knee vs. top-knee split is the organizing choice for everything that follows in the T-kimura attacking system.
