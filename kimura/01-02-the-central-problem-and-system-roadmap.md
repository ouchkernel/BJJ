# The Central Problem And System Roadmap

## Overview

Once the figure four grip is locked, everything in the system hinges on a single question: can you get your opponent's hand behind his back or not? This lesson lays out the decision tree that follows from that question — the "central problem" that gives the whole kimura system its direction, plus the escalating series of fallback options (power lines, legs, alternate submissions, alternate positions) used when the direct route is blocked.

---

## The central problem

- The application of the standard figure four kimura creates one central problem: getting your opponent's hand successfully behind his back.
- Whether or not you can do this determines how the rest of the system is used.

## If the hand goes behind the back

- Go directly toward submission holds — this works from both top and bottom position.
- The key element in both cases is establishing a **power line**:
  - **Top position power line** — usually employed from a side pin.
  - **Bottom position power line** — usually employed from closed guard or a triangle variation, commonly the trimura (half kimura, half triangle).

## If the hand cannot be quickly gotten behind the back — bring in the legs

- Traditional kimura is two arms against the opponent's two arms — this is the classic criticism of the move: "it's a strong man's move," ultimately your two hands against his two hands.
- When the opponent locks his two arms together defensively, avoid a pure hands-vs-hands battle. Instead, bring the legs into the kimura, since legs are significantly stronger than arms.
- Favorite tools for this: **cross kimura** and **kimura plata / Tarikoplata** — use leg strength to force the hand behind the back even against a strong-armed opponent.

## If even the legs can't get the hand behind the back

- You'll sometimes be stuck with a kimura lock applied but the opponent's hand still in front of his stomach, hips, or thighs.
- Two recommended alternatives:
  1. **Switch to a different submission** — e.g., armbar, juji gatame, or triangle.
  2. **Switch to a different position entirely** — usually the back, since it's the favored position overall. The two key skill sets for this are:
     - **T-kimura position** — lets you keep the opponent's hand pinned in front of his chest while you move around his body into a variety of positions and submissions (covered in its own section later).
     - **Head and arm kimura** — a strong avenue toward taking the opponent's back.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The system's central question after locking figure four: can the hand go behind the back or not? Everything downstream follows from the answer.
- Hand goes behind the back → attack directly, using the appropriate power line (top: side pin; bottom: closed guard/trimura).
- Hand won't go behind easily → bring in the legs (cross kimura, kimura plata) to exploit the leg-vs-arm strength advantage rather than fighting hands against hands.
- Hand still won't go behind, even with legs → switch submissions (armbar, juji gatame, triangle) or switch position (commonly to the back, via T-kimura position or head-and-arm kimura).
- This roadmap — direct attack, then legs, then switch submission/position — is the backbone the rest of the course builds on.
