# The Opposite-Shoulder Roll And Break Fall Principle

## Overview

Having established the conventional "right-right-right" pattern (right lead, roll over the right shoulder, attack the right arm), this lesson introduces its counterpart: rolling the Kimura over the opposite shoulder. It explains why the opposite-shoulder roll feels awkward the first times you try it by tracing it back to the very first thing every jiu-jitsu student learns — the break fall — and argues the awkwardness is worth it for the surprise value it creates.

---

## Why the opposite-shoulder roll feels strange at first

- Rolling over the opposite shoulder from your lead side can feel "klutzy" the first times you try it, because you're rolling in what feels like an unnatural fashion.
- The reason traces back to the standard beginner break fall, taught on day one of jiu-jitsu:
  - From a crouch with one side forward (say, right side), you put your **opposite hand** (left) on the mat.
  - This forms a triangle: your hand at the apex, your feet as the base.
  - You roll over your **forward-side shoulder** (right, matching your right-side-forward stance) and slap out on the other side (left hand).
  - This is the most basic, most universally taught move in the sport.

## The Kimura roll is built on the same triangle principle

- Standing right side forward, instead of putting your hand at the base of the triangle the way a beginner would in his first break-fall class, you lock the Kimura instead — the Kimura grip takes the place of the hand-post.
- Off that locked Kimura, you roll through into a winning position, using the same right-side-forward-to-right-shoulder-roll mechanics as the basic break fall.

## What an incorrect break fall shows about the opposite roll

- If you took a total beginner and told him to roll over the *left* shoulder while standing right side forward, the triangle theory falls apart — he'd be rolling against his own body, and the inevitable result is a terrible landing. No one would ever teach a beginner to break fall that way; they'd get hurt.
- However, more advanced athletes *can* roll in that "opposite" direction using the Kimura grip to control and support the roll (something a beginner's unsupported break fall doesn't have).

## Why bother: the surprise value

- Everyone expects a right-side lead to attack a Kimura on the opponent's right arm and roll over the right shoulder (the right-right-right rule).
- Rolling over the opposite shoulder instead catches opponents off guard precisely because it breaks that expectation.
- Situations that call for it come up naturally — e.g., when the opponent reacts to your inside position by grabbing your legs (a common defensive response, discussed further in the following lesson), the opposite-shoulder roll becomes a live option to counter it.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The opposite-shoulder Kimura roll is modeled directly on the basic beginner break fall: forward side, opposite hand posts to form a triangle, roll over the forward shoulder.
- Rolling over the "wrong" (opposite) shoulder as a beginner, unsupported, produces a bad landing — that's exactly why it feels awkward the first times you try the Kimura version.
- The Kimura grip is what makes the opposite-shoulder roll viable at an advanced level — it replaces and supports what the hand-post would normally do.
- The payoff for tolerating the awkwardness is surprise value: opponents expect the right-right-right pattern, and the opposite-shoulder roll breaks that expectation.
- This roll becomes a practical tool specifically when opponents react to your inside position by grabbing your legs — the counter to that reaction is developed in the next lesson.
