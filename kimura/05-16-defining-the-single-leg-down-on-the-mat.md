# Defining The Single Leg Down On The Mat

## Overview

A short definitional chapter that sets up one of the most important applications of the kimura in the whole system: using it against a single leg takedown attempt when both athletes are down on their knees. Before teaching the technique, this lesson precisely defines what "single leg down on the mat" means and distinguishes it from a standing single leg, and surveys the common ways the position arises.

---

## What "single leg down on the mat" means

- Defined specifically as a situation where **both** the athlete shooting the single leg and the athlete being taken down are on their knees.
- This is a big, recurring part of Danaher's training with his students.

## Contrast with a standing single leg

- If both athletes are standing and one shoots in for a single leg, that's a different scenario — covered separately, later, in the standing-position kimura material.
- If one athlete is down on his knees but the other is still standing, this is still classified as **standing position**, because one of the two athletes remains standing.
- Only when **both** athletes are on their knees does it count as a single leg down on the mat.

## Why this position matters

- Most competitive jiu-jitsu time is spent down on the floor, not standing — so single legs down on the mat are just as common as, if not more common than, the standing version.

## Common ways it arises

- Grappling on the knees and the opponent shoots straight in for a single leg from there.
- Opponent starts in a seated butterfly guard, pushes you back, comes up quickly, and shoots in for a single leg — both end up on the mat.
- Opponent is on the bottom of half guard, gets an underhook, comes up to his knees, and the position becomes a single leg down on the mat.
- Opponent is in a bottom pin (e.g., side control), gets an underhook, spins up to his knees, and again the position becomes a single leg down on the mat.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Single leg down on the mat = both athletes on their knees; if either athlete is still standing, it's classified as standing position instead.
- This position is extremely common because most jiu-jitsu is played on the floor, not on the feet.
- It arises from many different starting points: grappling on the knees, opponent shooting up from seated butterfly guard, opponent coming up from half guard bottom with an underhook, or opponent spinning up from a bottom pin/side control with an underhook.
- This definitional groundwork sets up the kimura-based response to the position covered in the following lessons.
