# Outside Arm Elbow Control — Standing Vs. Seated Roll-Over

## Overview

A further bottom-turtle kimura entry for when the opponent's outside arm is positioned so you can score his elbow directly with a reach-back grip, rather than needing to roll him first. Once that elbow grip and the kimura are secured, there are two ways to finish the roll-over: standing (high-amplitude, powerful, but genuinely dangerous) and seated (the same finish, much safer, and the version normally taught in class).

---

## Securing the position

- With the opponent behind you and his outside arm accessible, reach back with both hands and catch him **above the elbow**. Once you're behind the elbow it's a very strong grip — hard for him to pull it back out.
- Lock in and also catch his wrist.
- From here, several finishing routes are possible.

## Standing roll-over (high risk — advanced only)

1. Take your inside leg and hit an "inside leg stand up." This is easy once you already have the kimura, because he can no longer lock his hands.
2. Your inside leg stands up; start locking in the initial kimura.
3. Turn in on your training partner — you can either turn in and go underneath him, or stand up and go over the top.
4. You'll often be able to stand up faster than he can follow; put pressure over him and wind him straight down to the mat.
5. Come down to your elbows, switch elbows, come up to the line of his shoulders, bring the foot over, into the power line, strong breaking position.
- **Caution**: this move works, but it's an uncontrolled situation — both people moving at speed with a locked arm lock in play, which produces real injuries when it goes wrong. Reserve it for two very advanced athletes; never teach it in a beginners class. Advanced practitioners can perform it safely, but "moving body weight + falling body weight + an arm lock" is never a safe combination for the inexperienced.

## Seated roll-over (the version normally taught)

1. If the elbow is so far back you can't reach it, abandon the elbow-scoring plan: put your hand in his back pocket and turn him to face you in the usual fashion (his arm has to commit around your waist).
2. Otherwise: rise up, score the elbow, catch it.
3. Step your inside leg up, then **fold your legs down into a seated position** rather than standing.
4. You still have a good kimura here. Curl your foot underneath your body and catch your opponent around the leg.
5. Fall back — you land in a great attacking position.
6. If he doesn't roll out, you finish him right there.
7. Most opponents will roll: come up, tripod your legs across him, and arrive at the bone-breaking finish.

## Shallow tight-waist variation

- Measure the tight waist grip he's applying. If it's very shallow, you won't be able to reach the elbow — but the shallowness means you can simply put your hand in his back pocket instead.
- Face your training partner in the usual fashion; he has to commit his arm around you.
- As the tight waist comes in, score the elbow as you rise, catching a kimura.
- There's still a danger he circles back around to your waist — keep turning in to prevent it.
- Standing from here is very effective but dangerous for most people to perform daily in class, so the usual approach is to have the opponent sit and throw your leg over the top for a strong break.
- If he rolls through instead, come up tripod, elbow on the floor, into the same strong breaking finish.

## Key Takeaways

- When the outside arm is positioned so you can reach it directly, use a reach-back grip above the elbow rather than needing to roll the opponent first.
- Two finishing routes from the same kimura grip: standing (high-amplitude, powerful, but carries real injury risk from falling body weight combined with a locked arm lock — advanced-only, never for beginners) and seated (same finish, much safer, the default classroom version).
- If the elbow is too far back to reach — grip is too shallow — abandon that plan and use the "hand in the pocket" method to turn the opponent to face you instead.
- Seated version: fold into a seated position, curl the foot under, catch the opponent's leg, and fall back into the attack — finish immediately if he doesn't roll, or follow his roll to a tripod/elbow-down finish.
- Whichever route, the destination is the same bone-breaking finishing position.
