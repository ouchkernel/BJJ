# Half Guard To Closed Guard Kimura Conversion

## Overview

This lesson combines the strengths of both positions covered so far: half guard kimuras are easy to enter but dangerous to hold, closed guard kimuras are safe to hold but hard to enter. The solution is to enter from half guard and convert into closed guard before finishing, getting the entry ease of one and the finishing safety of the other.

---

## Starting position and initial control

1. From bottom half guard, the opponent wants chest-to-chest contact — always prevent this by bringing the knee into a shallow knee shield.
2. As the opponent crowds forward, use a bar across the opponent's shoulder line (elbow and hand) to hold them off — this makes it hard for them to get cross face or work effectively.
3. Go outside the training partner's elbow. The ideal is to get the hand behind the back, but a good opponent will often get their hand inside their own thigh first, defeating the direct attempt.

## Conversion sequence 1: one-handed kimura into closed guard

1. When the direct hand-behind-back attempt is denied, come out onto the elbow and curl the wrist around the opponent's forearm — a one-handed kimura frame, up on a base that still controls the opponent's body.
2. Bring the second foot up to the training partner's knee and hit a sliding action — the near knee comes in and blocks.
3. Sit down to the floor and shimmy the hips outward — this reopens the opportunity to work back into the kimura even if the opponent keeps the hand hidden.
4. General approach while working: keep the head up, body tucked, and put weight on the training partner.
5. Invert the leg on the far side to cross the feet — this closes the guard around the opponent, bringing everything up to the shoulder line, ready for a strong finish.

## Conversion sequence 2: arm extraction into closed guard

1. From the same shallow knee shield / inside position, as the opponent drives forward, hold them off and move them down the body.
2. If the opponent does a good job hiding the hand and it cannot be extracted directly, come up into a one-handed frame onto the elbow — now the opponent cannot easily pull the arm free.
3. Take a foot to the opponent's knee and shimmy the hips back to get the knee into the outside position; lock over the training partner's leg.
4. Lift the pelvis off the floor to put weight over the training partner — this produces the arm extraction.
5. As the opponent tries to hold base, shoot the hips out and invert the second leg, bringing everything up to the shoulder line for excellent finishing pressure (again, closing into closed guard).

## The underlying logic

- Closed guard kimuras are notoriously safe: the opponent can't skirt around the legs and counterattack. But they're relatively difficult to enter in the first place.
- Half guard kimuras are, by contrast, easy to enter but present real dangers once locked — the opponent has more room to counter.
- The solution is to treat these as complementary: enter more easily from half guard, then convert to closed guard (crossing the feet/inverting the far leg) to get the safety of that position for the actual finish.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Closed guard kimuras are safe but hard to enter; half guard kimuras are easy to enter but risky to hold — this lesson merges the two by entering in half guard and converting to closed guard before finishing.
- Always prevent chest-to-chest first with a shallow knee shield and a shoulder-line bar (elbow + hand).
- If the opponent hides the hand inside their own thigh, come up into a one-handed elbow frame rather than forcing the direct extraction.
- Two extraction tools: (1) sliding the near knee in as a block, sitting down, and shimmying the hips out; (2) locking over the opponent's leg and lifting the pelvis to extract the trapped arm.
- The finish, in both variants, comes from inverting/crossing the legs to close the guard and bringing everything up to the shoulder line.
