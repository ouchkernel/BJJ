# Kimura Recovery From Single Leg On The Knees

## Overview

A counter for when a talented opponent successfully angles off during a single-leg exchange on the knees. The usual response to a single leg down on the mat is to keep the opponent squared up and sprawl back into a front headlock system, but a good opponent will sometimes bump your controlling hand forward, peek his head to the outside, and threaten your back. This chapter shows how to convert that exact scramble into a kimura, and then how to ride the opponent's own recovery attempt into a strong top position.

---

## The base dynamic

- Both athletes are on their knees, one working a single-leg attack on the other.
- The attacker (bottom man in this exchange) is always looking to form an angle and work around behind the defender.
- The defender's default plan: keep the opponent square, sprawl the legs back, and retreat to a front headlock, then work the front headlock system from there.
- Against a talented opponent, this plan sometimes fails — the opponent succeeds in creating the angle.
- Kimura is presented as one of the best tools for recovering from that angular disadvantage.

## The problem: the head peeking out

- When you commit to the kimura in this scramble, the opponent's head often starts trapped in front of you.
- A skilled opponent will bump your hand/arm forward toward the mat, causing his head to slide backward and "peek out" to the outside.
- Once the head peeks out, the opponent can transfer toward your back — a dangerous back-exposure situation if you don't have an answer.

## The recovery sequence

1. As the opponent bumps and peeks his head out, transferring toward your waist, keep your hand hugging his elbow with your hand staying inside his forearm.
2. Bring your outside leg up (foot planted).
3. Pull your elbow down toward the mat and look underneath your own armpit — this lets you rotate/travel across your training partner's body.
4. Lock up the initial kimura grip from this position.
5. Read the opponent's reaction:
   - If he aggressively turns up into you (trying to come to his own top position), put your foot into his hip.
   - This sets up a simple sumi gaeshi–style roll: elevate with the kimura arm and send him through, ending in a strong T-kimura position (perpendicular to his body, elbow controlled) with him underneath.

## Variations shown

- **Deeper single-leg version**: opponent gets deep on the leg, bumps forward and peeks out, transfers to the waist. Same principle — hand stays over the wrist, outside leg comes up, look underneath the own legs as he goes down to a hip, scissor the legs across his body back into the kimura. As he tries to come up on top, fit the foot into his hip (knee protruding out) and lift/send him over into the T-kimura.
- **Aggressive top-hold response**: after the roll, if the opponent tries an aggressive hold on top, the foot performs the same elevating (sumi gaeshi–style) action — push off the other foot, elevate with the arm, and send him up into a strong pin on top instead of just the T-kimura.

## Follow-up once in the T-kimura position

- From the T-kimura there are many attacking options available once the opponent is trying to stand back up.
- A simple continuation shown: knock the opponent back down to the hip/tip position, then scissor back up into the kimura position again — a repeatable cycle if he keeps trying to recover.

## Key Takeaways

- When a single-leg scramble on the knees goes bad and the opponent successfully angles off, kimura is a high-percentage way to recover the position rather than abandoning to a scramble.
- The danger point is the opponent bumping your hand and peeking his head out — that's the moment back exposure becomes a real threat.
- The fix: keep the hand inside the forearm/hugging the elbow, bring the outside leg up, pull the elbow down and look under your own armpit to travel across the body and lock the kimura.
- Read the opponent's reaction to the kimura lock — an aggressive turn-up into you can be redirected with a foot in the hip into a sumi gaeshi–style roll, landing in the T-kimura position (or a full pin on top if he fights the roll aggressively).
- The T-kimura position that results is a strong, repeatable hub — you can cycle opponents back down and re-enter the kimura as they try to stand.
