# Supinated Wrist Grip Entry And Chest-Trap Control

## Overview

A practical answer to a problem created by the elbow-penetration entry covered just before this: switching to north-south and digging underneath the opponent's elbows works, but the space under the elbow is small and your wrist is thick, so a well-structured opponent can fight the elbows down to the mat and scramble back to guard. The fix is to stop trying to force a thick body part (the wrist) under the elbow, and instead thread a thin body part (the fingers) under the wrist — then anchor the resulting grip with your chest so the opponent can't simply yank the arm free.

---

## The problem with elbow penetration

- From north-south, working underneath the opponent's elbows is a proven idea, but the gap under the elbow is narrow while the wrist trying to go through it is thick.
- A defensively sound opponent turns each elbow down toward the mat as you work side to side, denying entry.
- Given enough time, the opponent develops space, throws his legs over, and recovers guard — the entry stalls out.

## The fix: penetrate with fingers, not wrist

- General rule: it is far easier to slide the thin cross-section of your fingers underneath a target than to force the thick cross-section of your wrist underneath the opponent's elbow.
- With your arm pressed down tight, dig the fingers underneath the opponent's wrist rather than trying to get your own wrist under his elbow.
- This grip is easy to establish but has an obvious weakness on its own: the opponent can simply lift his fist upward and slip free. Something has to pin the wrist down once you have it — that something is your chest.

## Chest-trap entry sequence

Starting position: across the opponent's side, opponent has both arms in tight (two arms over) in a sound defensive structure.

1. Take a palm-up (supinated) grip with your near hand.
2. Break your arm back, then punch it through underneath the opponent's wrist and grip it.
3. Immediately pin that wrist under your own chest.
4. When the opponent tries to yank the arm out, it is wedged in place and effectively stuck.
5. From here it's easy to draw the opponent up and pull him into the winning Kimura setup position covered earlier.

## Finishing options once the grip is trapped

Once the wrist is chest-trapped and you're in the winning position, several finishing routes are all described as highly effective, and the choice is situational:

- Work the trapped hand behind the opponent's back directly.
- Throw a leg over into a cross-Kimura style position.
- Transition into a bicep-slicer position.

## Second demonstration (repeat, different framing)

- Opponent again presents a tight structure with two arms over, making elbow-underneath entries hard.
- Take the palm-up grip, fight it through, and lock onto the opponent's wrist.
- Wedge the captured wrist underneath your chest — any attempt by the opponent to yank the arm free is frustrating and largely ineffective.
- Pull to expose enough space under the elbow to slide your hand into the winning Kimura position.
- From there, bring a leg across the opponent's body and finish with strong breaks, using whichever finishing methodology fits.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Elbow-penetration entries fail against a good structure because the wrist is too thick to force through the narrow gap under the elbow — a tight opponent can turn the elbow down and scramble away.
- Solution: penetrate with the thin fingers underneath the wrist instead, then trap that wrist under your chest so the opponent can't just lift the fist free.
- Sequence: palm-up (supinated) grip, punch the hand through, lock the wrist, pin it under your chest, then pull the opponent up into the winning Kimura position.
- Once trapped this way, the arm can't be yanked free — from there, go behind the back, throw the legs over, or move into a bicep-slicer, whichever finish fits the situation.
