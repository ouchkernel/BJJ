# Standing Kimura Vs. The Single Leg (Head Outside)

## Overview

The counterpart to the head-inside single leg counter: here the opponent's head ends up to the **outside** when he picks up the single leg, either because you intentionally moved it there or because he initiated that way. Head outside gives much easier access to the wrist and elbow for the kimura — there's no head in the way acting as a barrier — but it comes with a tradeoff: it also gives the opponent much easier access to switch to a double leg, so wrist control becomes essential.

---

## The tradeoff of head-outside single legs

- With the head to the inside, the head itself is a barrier to the kimura and has to be stuffed out of the way first.
- With the head to the outside, there's no such barrier — access to the wrist and elbow for the kimura is much easier.
- The cost: it's also much easier for the opponent to reach across to a double leg from this position.
- Because of that risk, it's important to always block the wrist so he can't make that switch.

## Sequence

1. Control the head and block the wrist so switching to a double leg is not an easy option.
2. Punch your hand in and step all the way up to your training partner's feet.
3. This produces a high-amplitude sumi-gaeshi-style throw that puts him over.
4. Scissor your legs. As he tries to come up on top, rely on the extraordinary leverage of the kimura — even if he manages to fully return to his base, it's still relatively simple to send him back down.
5. Come up with your legs on both sides of his body; as your legs spread ninety degrees apart, bring the elbow in for a strong finishing position.

## Repeat run-through

1. From a standing start with the opponent's head outside on the single leg: block the head and block the wrist first.
2. Secure the kimura grip, lower your level, and step up to your training partner's feet.
3. This gives another high-amplitude sumi-gaeshi throw.
4. As he turns into you afterward, focus on lifting the trapped elbow.
5. Scissor your legs and take the elbow down to the floor — you land in a perfect position to attack with the kimura.

## Key Takeaways

- Head outside on a single leg means no head barrier to the kimura, so wrist and elbow access is much easier than the head-inside case.
- The tradeoff: head outside also gives the opponent easier access to switch into a double leg — control the wrist and the head to prevent it.
- Core sequence: block head and wrist, secure the kimura grip, step all the way up to the opponent's feet for a high-amplitude sumi-gaeshi-style throw.
- The kimura's leverage is strong enough that even if the opponent regains his base after the throw, you can still send him back down relatively easily.
- Finish by scissoring the legs and driving the elbow down (or spreading the legs ninety degrees on both sides of his body and bringing the elbow in) into a strong finishing position.
