# Practical Application Roadmap — Top, Bottom, And Standing

## Overview

The pivot from theory to practice: a roadmap for the most common scenarios in which kimura actually gets used in competition and training. The scenarios are grouped into three main categories, and the study begins — as recommended for learning the position from scratch — with the side kimura from top position, before moving into the harder problem of how to actually get to a kimura against a defending opponent.

---

## The three practical categories

- **Top position**
- **Bottom position**
- **Standing position**

Within each of these there are many, many ways to apply kimura. The material starts with top position.

## Where to begin studying

- The recommended starting point for learning kimura mechanics is the **side kimura position** — across the opponent's side, kimura locked up. This is a great way to make first serious inroads into the underlying principles.
- Everything covered up to now assumed you already had the kimura locked, then worked from there. Studying applications means solving an earlier problem: how to actually **get to** a kimura in the first place.
- Once an opponent knows you have a dangerous kimura, they will do everything they can to prevent you from getting into kimura situations at all.

## Neutral position creates openings

- Example: an opponent in a seated open guard, playing a neutral exchange — neither person controls the other, and both have good options.
- Because it's neutral and their legs are still in the equation, the opponent doesn't feel threatened and isn't afraid to reach for your body in various ways.
- That reaching arm is what creates the kimura opportunity — as soon as your head is higher than their elbow and your elbow is higher than theirs, you can easily go into a kimura.
- This works because the opponent doesn't feel threatened by a neutral position — they wouldn't reach out this freely from a compromised position.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Practical kimura scenarios fall into three buckets: top position, bottom position, standing position.
- Start learning kimura mechanics from side kimura on top — best for understanding the principles.
- The real practical problem isn't finishing a kimura you already have, it's getting to one against an opponent who knows what a kimura is and defends accordingly.
- In neutral exchanges (e.g., open guard), an opponent doesn't feel threatened and will reach for your body — creating a natural kimura opportunity the moment your head is above their elbow and your elbow is above theirs.
