# Roll-Through Kimura: Timing The Hip Connection

## Overview

The roll-through is presented as an absolutely crucial skill: no matter how well you try to control an opponent's head while finishing a kimura from side control top, there will always come a day you can't control it, and you must be able to roll anyone over regardless of size — provided you keep the hand behind the back. The drilled version of the roll-through is easy to learn, but under live resistance people often get a disappointing result. This lesson isolates the exact timing detail that makes the difference: keeping your hips connected to the opponent throughout the roll.

---

## The test situation

- Bottom side control (for demonstration purposes). The wrist has been secured well and the kimura grip is in place, but the head has not been controlled — either because the opponent knows what he's doing on bottom, or because of a technical error.
- Pressure is applied and the opponent sits up. At this point, stepping over the head is no longer an option.

## Acknowledging what's good and what's bad

- **Bad**: the head is completely uncontrolled, and there's a good chance you end up going backwards.
- **Good**: the opponent's hand is still behind his back. As long as you have that, you own him — it looks like chaos, but it's chaos guided by you for one simple reason: the hand behind the back.
- When the opponent starts coming up, don't retreat backward in a straight line — go forward into a roll so that he ends up following you. Even though you're now technically in bottom side control, you're still in the driver's seat because you still have his hand behind his back.
- If the opponent froze in place and refused to move, you could technically break the arm right there — but a live opponent won't be that naive; he'll hit a forward roll.

## Where it goes wrong

- The opponent hits the forward roll and you come up too slowly — you lose contact between your hips and his stomach.
- As he rolls, he comes up into a seated position while you're still chasing, too late, too late, too late.
- He turns, faces you, pulls his arm free, and ends up squared up and on top. The golden opportunity is lost.
- Diagnosis: you had the hand behind the back and rolled him through — that part was fine. But when he rolled through, space developed between you and him because you didn't time the movement of your hips to follow his roll.
- This timing detail is what separates merely putting someone under pressure from actually finishing them, and it's exactly what needs to be drilled deliberately.

## The correct method

1. When the situation starts to deteriorate and he begins coming up, pitch across his body so your hips make direct contact with his hips.
2. As you come up and go through the roll, maintain that hip-to-hip contact.
3. Your leg (the leg nearer his body) must connect to him and follow as he rolls through, so that when you land, you land in a perfect braking position, right on the power line.
4. From there, go through for the finish.
5. The critical rule: your quadricep must maintain contact with the opponent's stomach and abdominals throughout the roll. If contact is lost and the opponent moves quickly and with determination, he will roll through and away from you. As long as the quad stays connected to his stomach, he can never get away — you'll always cover the head and go from chaos to a controlling, powerful finish.

## Demonstrated comparison

- **Correct**: hip-to-hip contact from the start; the leg follows the opponent throughout the roll; landing puts you in the finishing position with the head controlled and a strong kimura opportunity available.
- **Failed**: the leg fails to move with the opponent as he rolls; space develops; both people come up together in an uncontrolled scramble; the opponent turns rapidly to his knees, and the position is lost — you're forced back to guard.

## The core rule

- Timing the roll-through means following the opponent's movement and staying hip to hip throughout. That's what takes a chaotic, uncontrolled kimura situation and turns it into one with everything needed for success: hand behind the back, full control of the opponent's head, and the legs engaged in the kimura.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The roll-through is a mandatory skill because you will not always be able to control the head from side control top — but if the hand stays behind the back, you're still in control even in apparent chaos.
- Don't retreat backward when the opponent starts to rise — roll forward so he follows you.
- The single failure point people run into live: losing hip-to-hip / quad-to-stomach contact during the roll, which creates space and lets the opponent turn to face you and free his arm.
- Fix: pitch your hips onto his, keep your leg (and specifically your quadricep) glued to his stomach and abdominals throughout the entire roll.
- Correct timing lands you on the power line with the head controlled, hand behind the back, and legs engaged — the full package needed to finish.
