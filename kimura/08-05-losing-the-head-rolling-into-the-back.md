# Losing The Head: Rolling Into The Back

## Overview

A contingency lesson for when the leg-controlled Kimura roll goes wrong at the critical head-timing moment — the opponent's head beats yours across during the roll, killing the Kimura. Rather than treating this as a failure, it is reframed as a clean entry into back control via the back crucifix.

---

## The problem

- One potential failure point in the roll: the opponent's head gets in front of your head during the transition.
- When that happens, he is able to sit up before you can establish head control, which makes the Kimura impossible to finish.

## The silver lining

- Losing the head this way sets up a very nice entry into the opponent's back.

## What happened in the demonstration

1. From turtle, the arm is hooked as usual and the opponent transfers his weight.
2. The same roll is hit off the wrist grip.
3. Being a little late on the roll allows the training partner's head to pass in front.

## The recovery sequence

1. The instant the opponent's head is winning the race, immediately bring both hands back to your own body.
2. As the opponent turns up toward you, drive your body across his and lock back a head-and-arm control (back-crucifix-style).
3. No matter how the scramble resolves — even if the opponent ends up on top in some fashion — there is always the ability to fall back on the back crucifix at a minimum.

## Converting back crucifix into back position

- Applying pressure on the training partner's trapped arm in the back crucifix forces him to raise his pelvis to relieve the pressure.
- That pelvis-raising reaction is what takes you right back into full back position.

## The mindset

- Don't panic if you feel you've lost the head during the roll — it is not the end of the world.
- In fact, it is often an easy way to enter the opponent's back.

---

## Key Takeaways

- If the opponent's head beats yours across during the leg-controlled Kimura roll, the Kimura is dead — but the position is not.
- Immediate recovery: bring both hands back to your own body, drive across the opponent as he turns up, and lock a back-crucifix-style head-and-arm control.
- The back crucifix hold is a reliable fallback in any scramble outcome from this roll, even if the opponent ends up on top.
- Arm pressure in the back crucifix forces the opponent to raise his pelvis, which naturally transitions you back into full back control.
- Treat "losing the head" as a known, favorable branch of the sequence rather than a failure to recover from.
