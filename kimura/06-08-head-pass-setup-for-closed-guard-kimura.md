# Head Pass Setup For Closed Guard Kimura

## Overview

Kimura from bottom closed guard has been a conspicuous statistical failure over recent jiu-jitsu history — not because the hold itself is flawed, but because the traditional setups (sitting up to a hand, over-wrapping the elbow) don't translate well into live competition. This lesson names the three obstacles that make closed guard kimura so hard to reach and introduces a head-pass entry designed to get around the biggest of the three: head position.

---

## The three obstacles to closed guard kimura

To get into a kimura position from bottom closed guard you have to work against three things simultaneously:

1. **Your opponent's head**
2. **Your opponent's hands**
3. **Your opponent's arms/biceps** — specifically where his arm wraps around your body and flattens you out

The traditional method — coming over the cross and framing around the opponent's arm, or exercising wrist control while propping up on an elbow with the outside hand first — addresses some of this, but the head remains a persistent obstacle that stops the attacker from getting up.

## The head-pass entry

The core trick: physically pass the opponent's head to the side rather than fighting to get up and around it. Once the head is moved out of the way, the kimura grip becomes available.

### Setup

1. From bottom closed guard, take inside position on the training partner — secure the head and shoulders with a fairly conventional collar tie plus a hand over the opponent's arm.
2. Take the collar tie with one hand (e.g., left), and bring the other hand up close to the ear (not the face or eyes — this is a control point, not a strike).
3. From this position, open the closed guard with confidence.

### Passing the head

1. Physically push the opponent's head to one side (e.g., left) while bringing the same-side arm across to the other hand.
2. This clears the head out of the way — one of the three main impediments is now gone.
3. Take the elbow inside the opponent's elbow and go over the top with the arm to lock what looks like a basic ("hackneyed butcher") figure-four kimura grip — at this stage you have elbow control but not yet wrist control.

### Finishing the entry

1. Even without the wrist, the elbow-only figure-four grip is shockingly difficult for the opponent to yank free of. They can stand up, posture, and try to fight — the grip holds.
2. Move the body out to the side, invert the near knee, and lock further.
3. Come down to the wrist in the conventional fashion — work with the opponent's hand inside your thigh, and bring the thigh over the top to help control it.
4. Bring everything up to line with the shoulder — the standard finishing alignment ("power line" / "shoulder line") where even a small pull produces a strong effect.

## Variation: elbows-across entry

An alternate route to the same result: take inside position, pull the opponent forward, secure the head, pass the head, and bring the two elbows across each other (cross position). From there, one elbow goes inside and you come up just enough to lock the same clumsy-looking figure-four — again with only elbow control initially, wrist control added afterward. Turn the body out to the side, cross the legs, bring everything up to the shoulder line, and finish.

## Practical notes

- This method is deliberately simple — it isn't a technically dense sequence, it's a single decisive action (moving the head) that removes one of the three chronic obstacles to closed guard kimura.
- The grip you initially lock does not need wrist control to be effective — elbow control alone is enough to hold the position while you work down to the wrist.
- The intent is explicitly to raise the historically poor success rate of kimura from closed guard.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Three things stand between bottom closed guard and a working kimura: the opponent's head, hands, and the arm that wraps around and flattens you.
- The head-pass entry solves the head problem directly: secure a collar tie and a hand near the ear, open the guard, and physically push the head to one side while bringing your arms together on the other.
- The resulting figure-four grip only has elbow control at first — that's enough to hold the position; wrist control is added afterward using the thigh.
- Everything should finish aligned on the shoulder/power line, where a small pull produces a strong breaking effect.
- This entry was introduced specifically because traditional closed guard kimura setups have historically underperformed in live competition despite the hold itself being sound.
