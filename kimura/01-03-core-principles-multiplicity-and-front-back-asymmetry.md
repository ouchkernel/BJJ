# Core Principles: Multiplicity And Front/Back Asymmetry

## Overview

The first two of the kimura system's core concepts, both aimed at explaining why the system is built the way it is. The multiplicity principle justifies studying a whole family of kimuras instead of one variation. The front/back asymmetry principle explains why the "get the hand behind the back" goal from the previous lesson matters so much — it's not arbitrary, it's exploiting a structural weakness in how the human body works.

---

## The multiplicity principle

- Never limit yourself to one variation of a given submission hold — submission athletes consistently do better with a multiplicity of submissions available to them.
- The kimura system uses seven varieties of kimura (see the previous lesson) for exactly this reason. Other kimura variations exist beyond these seven, but they're deliberately excluded — the system's view is that those situations are better covered by alternative methods of attack.
- Why multiplicity matters in live sparring: opponents constantly present unexpected lines of resistance. Trying to hammer through every line of resistance with a single tool is very difficult. A multiplicity of tools makes the job significantly easier.
- Practical instruction: study a *group* of kimuras, not just any one kimura variation.

## The front/back asymmetry principle

- Fundamental fact about the human body: hands positioned in front of the body are strong and well coordinated. Once a hand is forced behind the back, it becomes dramatically weaker and less coordinated.
- This directly undercuts the "kimura is a strong man's move" criticism — you can make even the strongest man very weak by getting his hand behind his back.
- This is why the first goal on locking a figure four is to quickly and efficiently get the hand behind the back: it cuts down the amount of resistance the opponent can bring to bear, taking advantage of this simple, observable bodily asymmetry.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Multiplicity principle: never rely on one variation of a submission — a group of options (the seven kimuras) covers the many different lines of resistance opponents present in live sparring.
- Front/back asymmetry principle: hands in front of the body are strong; hands forced behind the back become dramatically weaker and less coordinated — this is the actual mechanism that neutralizes even strong opponents.
- These two principles together explain both *why* the system has seven kimuras and *why* getting the hand behind the back is always the first goal once figure four is locked.
