# Recap: The Two Directions Of The Kimura System

## Overview

A short recap chapter that restates the central organizing idea of the whole kimura system before moving on to new material: the instant you lock the figure-of-four, you have a binary choice of direction — break the hold or restrain with it — and a short list of fallback strategies if the breaking route stalls.

---

## The two directions

- The moment you get a hold of a kimura and lock the figure-of-four, there are two directions you can go:
  - **Breaking direction** — work to get your opponent's hand behind his back.
  - **Restraining direction** — keep the hand in front of the body.
- Both faces of kimura — breaking on one hand, restraining on the other — are manifested throughout the system already covered.

## Breaking mechanics recap

- If you're going the breaking direction, the first priority is good breaking mechanics.
- If you can't get your opponent's hand behind his back using your hands alone, the correct adjustment is to apply your legs into the kimura and use the strength of your legs and hips to enact the breaking mechanics — not to keep fighting it with hands and arms alone.

## Fallback strategies if breaking stalls

If the breaking direction isn't working out, or isn't something you're interested in pursuing, there are two other strategies:

1. Switch to another form of submission hold.
2. Switch to a completely different position or attacking system — the back is given as an example.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Every kimura splits into two directions the instant the figure-of-four locks: breaking (hand goes behind the back) or restraining (hand stays in front).
- When hand-only breaking mechanics fail, add legs and hips — don't just keep muscling it with the arms.
- If breaking isn't working or isn't the goal, the fallback is either a different submission hold or a full switch to another position/attacking system (e.g., the back).
