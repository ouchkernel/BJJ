# The Seven Varieties Of Kimura

## Overview

The opening lesson of the system lays out its founding claim: kimura is not one move but a family of seven related attacks, all built around a single dominant grip. The standard figure-four kimura is named as the "king" of the family — the foundation that most of the other six either grow out of or exist to support when the figure four alone runs into resistance.

---

## The seven varieties

1. **Figure four kimura** — the classic, best-known version, learned earliest in most people's jiu-jitsu. This is the one most associated with the word "kimura" and is treated as the king of the system; most kimura attacks are built off this grip.
2. **Figure four bicep kimura** — the grip is switched and shortened, closer to a mata leao (rear naked choke) grip, tightening the hold and changing the basic breaking dynamics compared to the standard figure four.
3. **Cross kimura** — the legs are brought in to greatly increase braking power in situations where it's difficult to get the opponent's hand behind his back.
4. **Kimura plata** (also called Tarikoplata after a well-known Norwegian competitor who popularized it) — a mix between kimura and omoplata, again used when it's tough to get the hands behind the back.
5. **Trimura** — a hybrid between the triangle and the kimura, used to immobilize the opponent's head and cut down his available resistance while working toward the kimura lock. Can be applied from bottom position or from side control (different triangle shape, same principle).
6. **One-handed kimura** — used in specialized situations; a useful addition to the arsenal even though it's used less often than the others.
7. **Legs kimura** — the exception to the rule below: there is no figure four here at all. The legs are used from the very start of the attack, and this variation is commonly used against turtle position.

## How the seven relate to each other

- All of the first six — figure four, figure four bicep, cross kimura, kimura plata, trimura, one-handed kimura — begin from the figure four kimura grip.
- The legs kimura (the seventh) is the sole exception: it never passes through a figure four at all, going straight in with the legs instead.
- Because most of the system's attacks flow from it, a strong understanding of the standard figure four kimura is treated as the essential foundation for everything else in the course.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Kimura is a family of seven distinct attacks, not a single move — studying the family, not just one variation, is what makes the system effective.
- The standard figure four kimura is the king of the group and the foundation most of the others are built from.
- Six of the seven varieties (figure four, figure four bicep, cross kimura, kimura plata, trimura, one-handed kimura) begin with a figure four grip; the legs kimura is the lone exception, going straight to the legs with no figure four stage.
- The legs kimura is specifically associated with attacking turtle position.
