# Controlling The Head: Beating The Shoulder

## Overview

Introduces the "trinity of power" that governs kimura strength — legs, head control, and elbow-to-shoulder-line — then works through the head-control leg of that trinity in detail from a side kimura position. The core mechanism shown is "beating the shoulder": getting your hip up and over your training partner's shoulder so your leg can step over his head, capping how far he can sit up and turning your leg into a drive source for the pull.

---

## The trinity of power

- Kimura power is measured most importantly by three factors:
  1. To what degree the legs are incorporated into the kimura.
  2. To what degree the opponent's elbow is brought up to his shoulder line.
  3. To what degree the opponent's head is controlled.
- Getting at least one of the three strongly represented produces a strong kimura attack. Getting all three produces a devastating one.
- If the opponent's head can come free, he can first weaken the lock and then ultimately escape from it — so locking the head down is essential to a strong attack.

## Beating the shoulder — the problem to solve

- Starting point: basic kimura hold from side position.
- Most defensive opponents will frame a hand into your hip (a good escape habit on their part — it lets them start recovering guard/legs).
- That framing hand and forearm make it hard to get your hip up and over your training partner's shoulder, which is the prerequisite for stepping your leg over his head.

## Sequence: beating the shoulder and stepping over the head

1. Get height over your training partner's shoulder despite the framing arm.
2. Turn your hips in to collapse his arm.
3. Bring your knee into his ribs.
4. Start a back-stepping action — as you switch your weight, it moves directly over your training partner's shoulder line.
5. As you come in on the arm, begin lifting the elbow joint and step directly over your training partner's head.

## Why the leg placement matters

- Stepping the leg over the head puts a definite limit on how far the opponent's head can rise as you apply the pulling force on the elbow.
- Without the leg over the head, the opponent can simply sit up with you when you pull, creating problems.
- Placement detail: it's tempting to drop the knee down for a tighter head trap, but that kills your ability to pull on the arm — keep your foot up close to his shoulder line instead so the leg stays available to drive.
- Your leg (the one over the head) becomes your drive leg — you push off it, generating a tremendous pulling force between your head, spine, and leg, which converts into a strong pull on the elbow.

## Contrast with the common mistake

- The typical error is to just push with the arms — this produces a very weak, anemic-looking kimura.
- The correct version: fully trap the head, push off the drive leg from the floor, and pull with the upper body and back moving away from the opponent. The opponent can't follow the pull because your leg goes directly over his head.
- This satisfies the head-control element of the trinity of power.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Kimura strength comes from three factors — legs incorporated, elbow to shoulder line, head controlled — get at least one strongly represented, ideally all three.
- If the opponent's head is free, he can weaken and then escape the lock — head control is not optional against a competent opponent.
- To beat the shoulder: get hip height over the shoulder, turn hips in to collapse the arm, knee to ribs, back-step your weight over his shoulder line, then step your leg over his head while lifting the elbow.
- Keep your foot near the shoulder line rather than dropping the knee down — you need the leg free to drive, not just to trap.
- The drive leg pushes off the floor while your head/spine/back pull away from the opponent — that combination, not arm strength, produces the strong pull that a leg-trapped head can't escape.
