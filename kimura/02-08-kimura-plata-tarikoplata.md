# Kimura Plata (Tarikoplata)

## Overview

The second of three leg-incorporated kimura variations off the same dorsal starting position as the cross kimura. It superficially resembles an omoplata (though it isn't really similar in mechanism) and takes its nickname — "tarikoplata" — from a young Norwegian competitor named Tarik who used it to great effect after training with Danaher's friend Sean Williams. Where the cross kimura keeps the figure-four hand grip and sits back, the kimura plata releases the hand grip in favor of a turning, hip-driven action that separates even the strongest locked hands.

---

## Setup

1. Start from the same locked-kimura position as the cross kimura, coming up and over the training partner and stepping in.
2. Unlike the cross kimura, there is no need to keep the figure-four hand grip. Take your own hand and hold your own thigh.
3. Use the second hand as a base of support on the floor.
4. Do not sit back for this version — there's no advantage to it here.

## Turning mechanics

- Start the action by turning your hips around the corner. This turning action — not arm strength — is what separates even the strongest man's locked hands and puts the hand behind his back, even against his will.
- Close one leg, extend the other; the extension off that closed/extended leg pairing produces a very strong finish.
- Extension alone finishes most opponents. For an especially flexible ("gumby") opponent who survives the extension, go the extra distance: turn the hips in a circle toward the training partner's head after extending the leg. This produces fantastic additional breaking pressure.

## Push vs. turn — which does the work

- In the second demonstration angle: get a hold of the opponent's arm, turn to a slight angle, and step the foot over the top.
- Withdraw the pushing hand — the leg replaces it as the pushing tool. The former pushing hand becomes a post hand for stability.
- Bring the legs back into a contracted position — overly extended legs here are "a disaster." Contracted legs give the ability to push and separate the hands.
- The push separates the hands, but it's the turn of the hips around the corner that actually does the breaking. Inflexible opponents can sometimes be finished by the push alone, but the turn is what handles resistant opponents.
- As with the rest of the system: always aim for a pull-dominant finish, not a push-dominant one. Bring everything in, let the pull do the work, turn and lift the elbow for the strong finishing position.

## Kimura plata vs. cross kimura

| | Cross Kimura | Kimura Plata |
|---|---|---|
| Hand grip | Figure-four kept throughout | Released — hand holds own thigh instead |
| Body position | Sit to buttocks | Stay more upright |
| Base | — | Extra post hand on the floor for stability |
| Breaking action | Leg strength separates hands directly | Hip-turning/rolling action separates and breaks |
| Dominance | — | More dominant to one side of opponent's body; easier to maintain head position above opponent's |

- Danaher personally favors the cross kimura more often, but rates both as highly effective — each has pros and cons.

## Why this matters

- Kimura plata is the second of three methods (alongside cross kimura and the upcoming "trimura") for putting the legs into the kimura battle instead of relying on arm-versus-arm strength.
- Sets up the third variation, which incorporates the triangle (senkaku) to trap the opponent's head as well.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Kimura plata starts from the same locked position as cross kimura but releases the figure-four grip — your hand holds your own thigh, and the second hand posts on the floor.
- No sitting back; instead, turn the hips around the corner to separate even the strongest locked hands.
- Close one leg, extend the other for the base finish; add a hip turn toward the opponent's head for extra-flexible opponents.
- The push separates the hands, but the hip turn is what actually breaks the arm — always aim for a pull-dominant finish, not push-dominant.
- Keep legs contracted, not overly extended — overextension in this position is a disaster.
- Kimura plata trades cross kimura's leg-strength separation for an upright, more stable, hip-turning approach; both are effective, with cross kimura as Danaher's personal preference.
