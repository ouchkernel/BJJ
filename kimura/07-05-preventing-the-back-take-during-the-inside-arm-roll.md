# Preventing The Back Take During The Inside Arm Roll

## Overview

The inside-arm roll from turtle position is powerful, but it carries a real danger against talented grapplers who are good at taking the back: mishandled, the roll can leave you underneath the opponent's legs with him shuffling up your body and slotting in two hooks — the exact reversal you were trying to avoid. This lesson explains why controlling the opponent's elbow throughout the roll is what prevents that back take from being exploitable, and details the hip-positioning and leg-clearing mechanics that keep you safe through the scramble.

---

## What can go wrong

- As you attempt to roll the opponent (a highly skilled grappler, good at getting the back) over the exposed elbow, the roll can go badly: you end up between his legs, he shuffles up your body, you slide down his — and he lands with two hooks in.
- At that point you are very much the defensive player. This is exactly what the roll is supposed to prevent, not cause.

## The good news / bad news framing

- **Bad news**: your back is exposed to the opponent, since you started the exchange in the position where he was already behind you and ready to go.
- **Good news**: you're controlling him at the elbow, which makes it difficult for him to do the one thing he needs to exploit back position — get his hips even a fraction above your hips.
- Practical result: he may still technically get "your back," but as long as you stay tight to the elbow, it becomes very difficult for him to actually exploit it.

## Staying above the hips during the roll

- As you hit the roll, one leg often gets entangled inside the opponent's legs — that's fine, as long as you keep your **knee high above him**.
- Keep your hips above his belly button, up toward his chest. With hips this high:
  - He can get one hook in, but the second one becomes much harder.
  - He has little real control to work with.
  - It's relatively easy to start pulling his feet apart and bringing your knees up to your chest.
  - (If the opponent were naive here, it would even be possible to thread into leg attacks — not the focus of this lesson, but worth noting as an option.)
- The goal is a **perpendicular angle**: staying above his hips so he doesn't get any real, meaningful contact with your legs.

## Clearing the legs

- As he starts hooking onto your legs, actively work to free them and bring them somewhere he can't operate against.
- When he tries to shimmy up your body, it should be difficult for him.
- If he gets two crab-ride-style hooks behind your legs and tries to lift/move you, it's relatively simple to extract and move your legs to the side.
- Bring the legs over the top and scissor over, landing back in the good attacking position — head forward over his hips, ready to go into a tight kimura.
- The general point: as long as you control the opponent's elbow, it's difficult for him to get two hooks in and score, or to pull you down into a stranglehold.

## The hip-height rule

- Stay riding with your hips up close to his chest. The more your hips drift down toward his hips, the more successful he becomes at attacking you.
- Why: when the opponent has two hooks in from behind, for him to actually control you his hips must be **higher** than yours (his hips end up in front of your lower back — good for him, terrible for you).
- You want the reverse: your hips higher than his. That's the variable that decides who wins or loses these scramble situations.
- Whoever's hips end up on top of the other's determines the outcome — that single relationship is the whole battle.

## Full recap sequence

1. Opponent locked behind you; expose his elbow, fight to get the hand inside.
2. As you roll him over, stay over his chest with your hips.
3. Expect a foot fight — he wants to thread his feet inside; keep your knees up toward your chest, using your hands to clear his legs if needed.
4. (Note: this is also where opportunities to extend into leg-lock attacks exist, though not covered here.)
5. Take your foot across and hook it onto his foot to clear his legs away — this works regardless of how strong his legs are.
6. Use the legs like a pendulum: throw one leg to carry the legs across, scissor.
7. End up in a good position across his side, ready to apply the kimura.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The danger in the inside-arm roll isn't losing the roll outright — it's landing it in a way that gives the opponent a clean back take with two hooks.
- Controlling the opponent's elbow throughout the roll is what denies him the one thing he needs to exploit back control: getting his hips above yours.
- Keep hips above his belly button/toward his chest, forming a perpendicular angle that limits him to at most one functional hook.
- Actively clear tangled legs rather than fighting through them — hook his foot to displace his legs, then scissor back to a good side position.
- The core rule of the whole scramble: whoever's hips end up higher wins. Ride high, don't let your hips drift down toward his.
- Done correctly, the inside-arm roll is a safe way to escape turtle bottom while avoiding both his hooks and his back control.
