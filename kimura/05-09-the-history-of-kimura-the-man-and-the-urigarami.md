# The History Of Kimura The Man And The Urigarami

## Overview

A historical aside on Kimura the man, as distinct from "the kimura" the move. It covers the Japanese naming (Urigarami), why the Brazilians renamed the hold after him personally, his encounter with Elio Gracie, and — most relevant to the rest of this volume — the historical evidence that his favorite personal setup for the hold was as an attack against turtle position.

---

## Kimura was a man, not just a move

- We typically refer to "kimura" simply as a move, or the lock — but it's worth remembering Kimura was a historical figure: one of the greatest judo players of all time, arguably the greatest, depending on who you ask.

## The naming

- The Japanese don't call the hold "kimura" — they call it Urigarami, and they actually have longer, more specific terms, because they also refer to the American lock as a form of Urigarami. So they have separate longer terms distinguishing an American lock Urigarami from a kimura Urigarami.
- Kimura himself was a man, and the reason Brazilians call the hold "kimura" rather than Urigarami is because he was so proficient in its use — most famously against Elio Gracie when he visited Brazil in the 1950s.
- Beyond that famous match, he spent time in Brazil training with many different people, who quickly recognized he was a formidable grappler on the floor and particularly adept at the use of Urigarami — what jiu-jitsu now calls the kimura.

## His favorite setup

- Digging into the history of Kimura the man shows he was very versatile — he used the kimura lock in many different scenarios.
- But it does appear he had a favorite setup, and where most of his personal kimuras were attacked from: predominantly as an attack upon turtle position.

---

## Key Takeaways

- "Kimura" names a man before it names a move — a historically dominant judo competitor.
- The Japanese term is Urigarami; a longer form distinguishes it from the Urigarami term also used for the American lock.
- Brazilians renamed the hold after him due to his dominant use of it against Elio Gracie and others in Brazil in the 1950s.
- Historical evidence suggests his favorite, most-used personal setup for the kimura was as an attack against turtle position — the subject taken up next.
