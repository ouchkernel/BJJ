# Bridging From Kesa Gatame Into Kimura

## Overview

Continuing directly from the kesa gatame chapter: how to actually convert the kizu (kimura) kesa gatame position into a strong, pull-dominant kimura when the opponent starts to fight back toward his underhook. The mechanism is a hip-elevation bridge that turns the position into a scissor across the opponent's body, followed by the same shoulder-beating, diagonal-line-of-force footwork used elsewhere in the system.

---

## Starting point

- You're controlling the opponent in kizu kesa gatame (underhook on the far shoulder, focus on shoulder/elbow, not head).
- The opponent starts to swim through and threaten your back with an underhook.
- Response: surrender the head (it was never the real point of control — the real focus is the elbow and shoulder).

## The bridge-and-scissor entry

1. As the opponent comes in behind you, take a step forward and commit down toward the mat.
2. The goal is to create hip elevation — your hips end up higher than the opponent's hips.
3. From this elevated position, start locking the kimura grip.
4. Because your hips are higher, your legs become "weightless" over the opponent's body — you're essentially balanced on the point of his hip.
5. Scissor your legs across his body in one motion, ending up on the opposite side of his body from where you started.

## From the initial lock to the breaking position

- Getting the initial kimura lock only gives you *position* on the opponent — it does not yet give you a *breaking* position.
- The breaking position requires reaching the diagonal line of force (the shoulder line), same principle used throughout this system.
- Sequence once the opponent puts a hand on your hip to stop you moving:
  1. Sit down and beat the opponent's shoulder.
  2. Come back in the opposite direction, focusing on getting your elbow to the floor.
  3. Beat the opponent's shoulder, step over the opponent's head.
  4. Move all the way up to the idealized line of force.
  5. By the time your elbow reaches the opponent's elbow and your legs separate roughly 90 degrees, even the smallest pull on this pull-dominant kimura produces a strong result.

## Repeating from a standard side-control entry

- The same movement also works starting from a basic side-control position (not just off a kesa gatame catch): opponent punches a hand through, you switch into kizu kesa gatame focusing on shoulder/elbow.
- Controlled this way, the position is genuinely hard to escape (done poorly, it's easy to escape — done well, focusing on shoulder/elbow, the opponent's attempts to move out are slow and labored).
- Once he's slowed down, go across the shoulder, elevate your hips above his, split your legs and balance on his ribcage, scissor across his body, come up, beat the shoulder, step over his head, and move to the diagonal line of force for a strong kimura attack.

## Another entry variation (from kazoo/underhook fighting)

- Starting in a kesa-gatame-adjacent position where the opponent frames and pushes on your face, you can pick up an underhook on his arm as he frames — losing the more specialized hold but returning to a regular kesa gatame with strong attacking position.
- If the opponent shocks/pushes your head forward and you lose head control, that's acceptable — the focus of control remains the elbow/shoulder, so his attempt to come up and take your back is slowed rather than immediate.
- As he works hard to bring your body weight forward, that effort creates the opening: go forward, expose the arm, apply the grip over his forearm, walk around the corner, get the elbow to the floor, bring your body up to his head and shoulders, step over his head, and finish on the line of force.

## Role of kesa gatame in the overall system

- Kesa gatame (in its kizu/underhook form) is used here not as a standalone finishing hold but as a **transitory position** — something to control and slow the opponent with when your other holds are failing and he's threatening your back with an underhook.
- The specific value: it buys just enough time to transfer into kimura and then vault from one side of the opponent's body to the other into the strong breaking position (diagonal line of force) that the system favors.

## Key Takeaways

- Kizu kesa gatame is a bridge, not a destination: when the opponent underhooks and threatens your back, surrender the head and use hip elevation to scissor into a kimura on the far side.
- Hip elevation is the mechanical key — get your hips above the opponent's hips so your legs become weightless and can scissor freely across his body.
- The initial kimura lock is only position, not a break — you still need to travel to the diagonal shoulder line: beat the shoulder, get the elbow to the floor, step over the head, separate the legs ~90 degrees.
- This entry works from multiple starting points — a direct kesa gatame catch, a standard side-control switch into kizu kesa gatame, or a reactive underhook pickup off the opponent's framing — the finishing mechanics are the same each time.
- The whole point of using kesa gatame here is as a stalling/transitional tool: control shoulder and elbow just long enough to convert into a strong, pull-dominant kimura on the diagonal line of force.
