# The Diagonal Power Line

## Overview

One of the most crucial concepts for side kimura: positioning your body along a diagonal "power line" across the opponent rather than perpendicular to him. Getting this alignment right is what allows you to lift the opponent's back off the floor, control his head, and generate maximum pulling pressure — and it's demonstrated with an empirical self-test (the no-hands kimura) that proves whether you're actually lined up correctly.

---

## The mistake: perpendicular alignment

- The common instinct is to apply kimura from a perpendicular angle — going straight across the opponent's body.
- Being perpendicular to the opponent limits how much you can lift his back off the floor and get your hand behind him into a breaking position.

## The fix: the diagonal power line

- Instead of going perpendicular, line your body up along a straight diagonal that runs from the opponent's one hip to his opposite shoulder (e.g., left hip to right shoulder).
- This diagonal line — opposite hip to opposite shoulder — is where your body should be lined up when applying side kimura.
- In this alignment: your legs sit at roughly 90 degrees, and you'll ultimately step over the opponent's head to control it.
- The result: your hip dominates his (far) shoulder, and your shoulder dominates his (opposite) hip — this diagonal is the mechanically optimal breaking position, not the perpendicular one.

## Applying the power line

1. Start with the basic kimura lock, lifting the opponent's elbow off the floor and rolling his wrist inward.
2. Walk your hips up toward his shoulder.
3. Step over his head, moving your own head down toward his hips as you do.
4. Sweep your elbow to his elbow; let your legs separate to 90 degrees.
5. This is the optimal breaking position — from here it's easy to take your head back and away to apply a strong pulling force.

## Head position and the pull

- If your head crowds in close to the opponent's head, it limits how much you can pull.
- Only when your spine straightens and your head moves back along the power line do you get optimal pulling pressure.
- This reinforces the pull-dominant kimura principle: you want the head moving back and away, not staying crowded in, to generate maximum breaking pressure.

## "Beating the shoulder" and head control

- The goal, once you have your grip, is to "beat" the opponent's shoulder — bring your hip up to and applying force on his shoulder line.
- This positions you to step over his head and gain real head control, which is part of the broader "trinity of power" idea (controlling and mobilizing the opponent's head is a major factor in succeeding with kimura).

## The no-hands kimura test

- A good empirical test of whether you're correctly lined up on the power line: attempt a **no-hands kimura**.
- Sit up to the training partner's hip, step across his head, and apply the position with your control hand not even touching the opponent and your other hand only lightly resting the blade of the wrist against his wrist (not gripping).
- Sweep your elbow to his, let your head move back — if you're correctly positioned on the power line, you still get a strong pull and real breaking pressure with essentially no hand contact.
- This demonstrates you're maximizing the pulling effect through positioning alone, not pushing with the hands — the mechanical perfection of the power-line position does the work.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Don't apply side kimura perpendicular to the opponent — that limits your ability to lift his back and get a hand behind him.
- Line your body along the diagonal power line: opposite hip to opposite shoulder. Your hip dominates his shoulder; your shoulder dominates his hip.
- Sequence: lift elbow off floor, roll wrist inward, walk hips to his shoulder, step over his head, sweep elbow to elbow, legs at 90 degrees.
- Head position controls pulling power: crowding your head in limits the pull; moving your head back along the power line (spine straightened) maximizes it.
- "Beating the shoulder" (hip pressure on his shoulder line) sets up stepping over the head for real head control — head control is central to kimura success.
- The no-hands kimura test — applying the hold with minimal/no hand grip — proves whether your power-line positioning is correct; good positioning alone produces breaking pressure.
