# Elbow To Shoulder Line In Practice

## Overview

A practical, hands-on look at the second element of the trinity of power: bringing the opponent's elbow up to his shoulder line before attempting the break. The core claim is that the further the elbow sits from the shoulder line, the less pressure the opponent feels and the more work the attacker has to do — so investing a moment to physically drag the elbow up first pays off in a far more efficient break, even on a straightened arm.

---

## Recap: the trinity of power

- Kimura power is measured by three factors: legs incorporated, elbow brought to the shoulder line, and head control.
- One factor strongly represented gives a strong kimura; all three gives a devastating one.

## The shoulder line concept

- The shoulder line is the line joining the opponent's two shoulders.
- The further the opponent's elbow is from that line, the less pressure he feels from the kimura.
- The closer the elbow comes to the shoulder line, the less work is required to get an effective break.

## Bringing the elbow to the shoulder line

- Demonstrated here on a relatively straight arm deliberately (a sharply bent arm is generally preferred, but a straight arm shows the technique still works if the mechanics are correct and the opponent won't let you bend it).
- Once at the opponent's head and shoulders, physically take his elbow up to the shoulder line with a pull — not a push. You cannot push an elbow to the shoulder line, only pull it.
- Sequence:
  1. Bring your head over the elbow first — the exact opposite of the head position used to finish the kimura.
  2. Find his elbow with your elbow.
  3. Move your head away, pulling his elbow up to the shoulder line.
- If the opponent tries to keep his hand down by his hip, don't try to yank it up in a haphazard fashion — make a strong armed test first: lift and take the elbow up in a controlled way.
- Once the elbow is on the shoulder line, bring your elbow to his, trap the head, and engage the pulling action described earlier — this is where severe breaks occur.

## Why the extra step is worth it

- If the arm is left down by the hip, it's very hard to get a break — substantially more mechanical work is required.
- Taking the extra time to bring the elbow up to the shoulder line first, then breaking from there, requires substantially less mechanical work for the same or even more devastating result.
- Once the elbow is up on the shoulder line, everything from that point on is about the pull backwards.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The shoulder line (the line joining the two shoulders) is the target destination for the opponent's elbow before you commit to the break.
- Elbow far from the shoulder line = opponent feels little pressure and you do more work; elbow on the shoulder line = the break comes cheap.
- You pull the elbow to the shoulder line, you never push it — head goes over the elbow first, then away as you pull.
- This works even on a straight arm, though a bent arm is generally preferred — mechanics matter more than arm bend.
- Investing a moment to bring the elbow up first is a net efficiency gain: less total mechanical work for an equal or more devastating break than attacking the arm down at the hip.
