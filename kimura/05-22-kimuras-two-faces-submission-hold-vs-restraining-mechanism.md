# Kimura's Two Faces — Submission Hold Vs Restraining Mechanism

## Overview

One of the deepest messages of the overall Kimura system: the Kimura grip has two distinct faces, or aspects. One face points toward submission — driving the hand behind the back to attack the shoulder. The other face is used purely as a restraining mechanism — a method of control applied when the trapped hand stays in the anterior (front) position, used not to finish but to dominate and dictate where the opponent's body goes. This lesson runs the single-leg entry drill again specifically to illustrate the restraining-mechanism side of the Kimura, and then explains how repeated drilling of it builds a reliable counter to strong underhook players attacking from bottom position.

---

## The two faces of Kimura

- **Submission-hold face**: the hand is worked behind the back, targeting the shoulder joint for a finish.
- **Restraining-mechanism face**: applied when the opponent's hand stays in the anterior position, in front of his body. Here the Kimura isn't used to submit — it's used to control the opponent into desirable positions where you can dominate the action.
- This distinction is framed as one of the deepest messages in the whole video — the same grip serves two entirely different tactical purposes depending on where the trapped hand ends up.

## Demonstrating the restraining-mechanism face

- Training partner has good inside position and shoots quickly to the leg, working for an initial angle.
- Response: place a hand on his head for control, then shoot the other hand through, making sure to go wrist-deep.
- If the opponent tries to snap the head forward from here, the trapping hand is used as a base of support — this keeps the position stable.
- Key confidence point: he isn't going to take the back from this position. Trust the Kimura frame — he can't move you around, can't lift you off the mat, and can't switch to a double leg because the second leg is already posted out.
- From here, the wrists are locked by simply crossing them, and it becomes an easy matter to put him down to the floor.
- Two options once locked: commit backward to T-Kimura, or snake the hand through and hit a hip-heist as he tries to get back up — ending in good position on top instead.

## Why this gets drilled the same way

- Repeated drilling of this entry builds the ability to go into the Kimura rapidly, smoothly, and efficiently as a way of defending single legs on the mat.
- As confidence in the frame grows, this drill makes it noticeably easier to counter strong underhook players attacking from bottom position.
- Scenario: as the partner comes up from bottom, the defender already has the position being described — even though he came up successfully from the bottom, he isn't going to be taken down easily from there.
- As the attacker tries to press over the top and use his single-leg position, he finds it's quite difficult to progress — the defender can easily sit him through, bring him over, and return him right back to where he started, repeating the rep.
- Used this way, the drill develops the ability to rapidly, easily, and efficiently reach the Kimura positions that dominate a single-leg attempt — even when the opponent gets deep on the leg and forms an effective angle.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Kimura has two faces: submission (hand driven behind the back) and restraining mechanism (hand kept anterior, used to control and dominate position rather than finish).
- The restraining-mechanism version relies on trusting the frame — it stops the opponent from taking the back, lifting you, or switching to a double leg (because the second leg is already posted), even if he manages to stand back up.
- Once wrists are crossed and locked, the same two branches apply: commit to T-Kimura, or hip-heist to the top as he tries to stand.
- Constant repetition of this entry builds a reliable, efficient counter specifically against strong underhook players coming up from bottom position — even against opponents who get deep on the leg and establish a good angle.
