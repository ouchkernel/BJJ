# Bending The Arm And Exposing The Back

## Overview

Addresses the next major mechanical question: should the opponent's arm be bent more than 90 degrees or less than 90 degrees, and — more importantly — how do you actually get a straight-armed opponent into a sharply bent position with the hand pinned behind the back? The answer combines a simple structural trick (exposing the back off the floor) with a wrist-control idea borrowed from arm wrestling. The result is presented as pure leverage, requiring no strength advantage at all.

---

## More than 90 degrees vs. less than 90 degrees

- Both angles can produce a full break — arms have been broken from both a sharply bent position and a more open one.
- Stated preference: keep the elbow sharply bent to **less than 90 degrees** whenever possible.
- Reasoning: breaks from the sharply bent position tend to be more catastrophic, and it's generally easier to control the opponent's arm and lower his ability to resist from there.

## The problem: a flat back

- Starting point: training partner in a relatively straight-arm position, hands already placed correctly (no daylight between your wrist and his).
- If the opponent's back is flat on the ground, you have no ability to pull his arm in behind his back — there's no room to work the bend.
- The first job is therefore to **expose the back** — get it up off the ground.

## Step 1 — expose the back

- Put up a support leg (posting one foot on the ground). This is very difficult to do with both knees down.
- From the support-leg position, bring your elbow to the training partner's elbow and simply point your elbow at the ceiling.
- Pointing the elbow at the ceiling always brings the opponent's back up off the ground.
- A side effect of this motion: it puts the opponent's wrist behind his own elbow, setting up an easy "throttle" of the wrist — like twisting the throttle grip on a motorcycle.

## Step 2 — throttle the wrist (the arm-wrestling trick)

- Once the back is exposed, throttle your wrist over the top of the opponent's, bringing his hand all the way in behind his back into a position of extreme mechanical weakness.
- Once the hand is locked in behind the back this way, it's almost impossible for the opponent to recover it and pull it back out.
- **Arm-wrestling parallel**: in arm wrestling, a big part of winning is getting your hand over the top of the opponent's hand — whoever gets on top can dominate the other's arm down to the table/floor. The same principle of "get your hand on top" is used here to bend the training partner's arm and draw it behind his back.

## Full sequence

1. Point your elbow at the ceiling (with a support leg up) — this lifts the opponent's back off the ground and positions his wrist behind his own elbow.
2. Throttle your hand over the top of his wrist, arm-wrestling style, and draw the hand all the way in behind his back into a position of weakness.
3. From here you're set up to apply the breaking mechanics covered elsewhere in the series.

## Why this matters

- Demonstrated repeatedly against a "strong resistant arm" — the sequence works regardless of the opponent's strength because it's pure mechanical leverage, not a contest of force.
- Framed explicitly as dispelling the myth that kimura requires a strong man to apply it: precise placement of lever and fulcrum, combined with exposing the back and using the arm-wrestling wrist trick, lets a much weaker person control a much stronger opponent.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Both >90° and <90° elbow bends can produce a full break, but the stated preference is <90° — more catastrophic breaks and better control of the opponent's resistance.
- You can't bend the arm behind the back if the opponent's back is flat on the floor — exposing the back is the required first step.
- To expose the back: post a support leg and point your elbow at the ceiling; this lifts his back and puts his wrist behind his own elbow.
- To finish the bend: throttle your hand over the top of his wrist (arm-wrestling style) and draw it behind his back into a mechanically weak position he can't recover from.
- The whole sequence is presented as strength-independent — pure leverage lets a weaker person control a much stronger opponent.
