# The Dorsal Kimura Position

## Overview

Introduces the dorsal kimura — the situation where the opponent lies on his side with his arm up in a kimura position, a shape that resembles a shark's dorsal fin. This lesson lays out why the position is tempting (huge submission potential) but structurally weak (huge potential for the opponent to reinforce and escape), and states the central guiding principle of the entire kimura system: the anterior/posterior dichotomy of the hand.

---

## What is the dorsal kimura

- Occurs when the opponent lies on his side with his arm up in a kimura position — the shape resembles the dorsal fin of a shark, hence the name.
- The name is one the instructor made up himself rather than a traditional term — he generally prefers widely-accepted traditional names when one exists, and only invents his own when there isn't one.

## Why it's tempting but risky

- There's huge potential for a kimura finish from this position.
- But there's equally huge potential for the opponent to resist: he can lock his hands together, tuck his hand inside his own thigh, grab his own belt or pants, and a thousand other small defensive actions.
- The thing all of these defenses are trying to prevent is the same: getting the hand behind the back.

## The primary goal and the anterior/posterior dichotomy

- The primary initial goal in any kimura situation is to get the opponent's hand behind his back — one of the single biggest mechanical advantages one person can impose on another.
- Put a strong man's hand behind his back and he becomes weak. Put his hand back in front and he's strong again.
- This anterior/posterior dichotomy is a guiding insight behind the whole kimura system: you always want the opponent's hand posterior (behind), and he always wants it anterior (in front).

## The central problem of the kimura system

- The central problem: how to keep the opponent's hands separated and prevent him from reinforcing the threatened hand — with his other hand, another part of his body, or his clothing.

## Why the dorsal position itself isn't the finish

- The dorsal position needs to be converted into a stronger breaking position. It's a decent breaking position on its own, but not as powerful as the side position, because it lacks access to the diagonal line of power (the "power line").
- Even if the opponent's hands are successfully separated from this position, there's a limit to how much breaking force can be imparted, because the body can't rotate toward the power line.
- It's relatively easy for the opponent to post his hands on your knee, push his whole body out, and start turning into you — even threatening your own arms with arm locks.
- Head control is weaker here too — even squeezing the knees together doesn't give the same head control as the stronger position.
- The biggest problem: it's hard to use pull-dominant kimuras from the dorsal position. Working from here, you almost always end up pushing the hand around the corner instead — a push-dominant kimura, which is mechanically less sound than a pull-dominant one.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Dorsal kimura = opponent on his side, arm up in kimura position, resembling a shark's dorsal fin.
- Primary goal of any kimura situation: get the opponent's hand behind his back — hand-behind-back makes even a strong opponent mechanically weak.
- The anterior/posterior dichotomy (you want the hand behind, he wants it in front) is the guiding insight of the whole kimura system.
- Central problem of the kimura system: keeping the opponent's hands separated and preventing him from reinforcing the threatened arm.
- The dorsal position alone is a weak finishing position — no access to the power line, vulnerable to the opponent posting on your knee and turning out, and it forces you into mechanically inferior push-dominant kimuras instead of pull-dominant ones.
