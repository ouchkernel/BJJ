# Converting Dorsal Kimura To Side Kimura

## Overview

The direct follow-up to the dorsal kimura problem: if you find yourself in a dorsal kimura and successfully get the opponent's hand behind his back, don't stay there and finish with a push — convert into the mechanically stronger side kimura so you can access the power line. This is called the single most desirable transition from a dorsal kimura into a strong finishing position.

---

## Why convert

- Finishing directly from the dorsal position reduces you to a pushing mode.
- From a push-dominant dorsal finish, it's easy for the opponent to post his head on your knees, push his head out, start spinning his body, and even threaten you with arm locks — an uncontrolled situation.
- The fix: whenever you get the hand behind the back from a dorsal position, go through into the mechanically stronger side position instead of finishing on the spot.

## The conversion sequence

1. **Elbow to armpit.** Take the training partner's elbow and connect it to your own armpit.
2. **Hand to scapula, not lower back.** As you bring the hand behind the back, drive it all the way up to the scapular muscles — never bring the hand to the lower back, that's never a good target.
3. **Windshield-wiper the feet and turn.** Take both feet, windshield-wiper them, and turn with your chin in front of the opponent's shoulder so you can take your elbow over his body.
4. **Elbow over, not down in front.** Never go down with your elbow leading in front — that produces a clumsy face-plant and a poor finishing position. Instead take the elbow over and post on the elbow (the elbow takes the fall, not your face).
5. **Big step across the body.** Once posted on the elbow, take a big step across the training partner's body. This places you precisely on the power line.
6. **Finish.** From here you have a very controlling position with devastating finishing mechanics — a fast, strong break.

## Repeating the key details

- After the turn, shrug the elbow over the opponent's hip.
- Push the pelvis forward, step with the legs spread roughly 90 degrees, and go elbow to elbow.
- If the opponent isn't flat enough, the position can start to degenerate back into a push — correct by pushing the pelvis forward and taking the big step to reset onto the power line.
- Once truly on the power line, even a movement of millimeters is enough to get an effective break.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Never finish a kimura directly from the dorsal position if you can help it — it forces a weak push-dominant finish that's easy for the opponent to escape or counter.
- The conversion: elbow to armpit → hand to scapula (never lower back) → windshield-wiper feet and turn, chin in front of the shoulder → elbow goes over the body and posts (never lead down with the elbow in front, that's a face-plant) → big step across the body onto the power line.
- Landing on the power line lets even a tiny amount of extra pressure produce a strong, fast break.
- This dorsal-to-side conversion is described as the single most desirable transition from a dorsal kimura into a strong finishing position.
