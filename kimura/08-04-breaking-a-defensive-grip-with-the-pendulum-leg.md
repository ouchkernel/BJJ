# Breaking A Defensive Grip With The Pendulum Leg

## Overview

A troubleshooting lesson for the leg-control turtle entry: once an opponent has seen the roll a few times, he will anticipate it and defensively lock his hand against his own thigh to prevent it going behind his back. This lesson shows how to break that defensive grip using the pendulum action of the outside leg, and how to finish the pin and lock even after the grip is broken.

---

## The problem: the anticipating opponent

- After the move has been hit a few times, it becomes obvious to the opponent what's coming once the rolling action initiates.
- Many opponents respond by putting their hand in a defensive position — commonly tucked inside their own thigh — to prevent the hand from being taken behind the back.

## The governing principle

- As with any Kimura: if you cannot get the hand behind the back directly, trust in the strength of your legs.
- The advantage is structural: the opponent is now fighting the strength of your legs with only his hands — an exchange that favors you. You also have the entire rolling body weight of your body available to separate whatever defensive grip he has.

## Breaking the grip with the pendulum leg

1. Hook in on the training partner in the usual fashion (wedge, hook the arm).
2. As you come around the corner to make your grips, the opponent plants his hand defensively inside his own thigh — you feel stymied.
3. Use the outside leg as a pendulum. The pendulum effect stresses the opponent's grip — even just a light wave of the leg puts pressure on his hand.
4. The opponent grips as tight as he can in response; tension builds, then a good strong movement of the pendulum leg quickly brings the hand down into a position where the pass-off (from left hand to right hand, i.e., his leg control passing hands) can be made easily.
5. As he tries to get up, the grip you now hold makes that very difficult.
6. Bring your hand across, heist (lift the hips off the floor), and post the hand across his thighs on the other side — a winning position.

## Finishing details

- When you come up for the break, the opponent will typically try to get his head up first — this is blocked by your lat muscle and tricep.
- As you heist and come up to your knees, your hand catches across his two hips, making it very difficult for him to move around.
- From here, turn back toward the head for a very strong and powerful lock.

## Full sequence, repeated for clarity

1. Wedging knee, initial hook with the foot — good position to start turning the training partner.
2. Reach all the way through; he gets a defensive grip on his own leg.
3. Come up into what should be a winning position.
4. Outside leg as pendulum — quickly breaks his grip, puts his hand behind the back.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Expect opponents to anticipate this roll and defend by tucking the hand against their own thigh — this is a normal, high-percentage counter to prepare for.
- The pendulum action of the outside leg is the tool for breaking that defensive grip: even a light wave stresses it, and a strong pendulum motion breaks it down.
- The exchange favors you structurally — it's your legs and rolling bodyweight against only his hands.
- Head control still matters at the finish: the lat muscle and tricep block his head from rising, and the hand across his hips prevents him from moving as you turn back toward his head to lock the Kimura.
- Trust in leg strength over direct hand-vs-hand grip fighting whenever the defensive grip appears.
