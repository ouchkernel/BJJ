# The Family Of Six Kimuras

## Overview

Closes out the kimura family by summarizing why having multiple variations matters, then introduces the sixth and final member of the family — one that doesn't come from the classic figure-four grip at all, but starts entirely with the legs, typically from turtle position. This lesson also states, plainly, that this six-move family is a deliberate curation: other kimura variations exist, but the instructor's own system sticks to these six because the rest were judged unnecessary or better covered elsewhere.

---

## Why a family of kimuras

- The kimuras covered so far tend to cover each other very well — whenever one fails, another steps up to take over.
- Employing this multiplicity lets you break through very strong defenses that a single method of attack could never get through.
- General principle: always have more than one way of expressing a given move — that's the only way to cover contingencies.

## The sixth kimura: the leg-entry variation

- Unlike the other five, this one doesn't stem from an initial figure-four grip — it starts entirely with the legs, and is typically done from turtle position.
- Often referred to as a "reverse omoplata," but it actually has more in common with a kimura.
- Sequence:
  1. From turtle, the knee comes in and wedges on the training partner.
  2. Lock and get the opponent's arm locked in place.
  3. Pass the arm across to the other side. From here there are several options — grab the training partner's wrist, or grab a hold of his leg.
  4. Put his hand behind his back.
  5. Rotate through on the training partner and send him across.
  6. Control the training partner, come up to the knees, catch the hips, and rotate back.
- Result: the same kind of kimura breaking pressure as the hand-based versions, just generated with the legs instead.

## The complete six-move system

- Using all six kimuras as a team lets you break through resistance at every level of the kimura — whether the resistance shows up in the finishing position or in the entry to the finishing position. One of the six will be appropriate for the situation.

## Is this the limit of kimura?

- No. Other versions of kimura exist and are fascinating — bahata plata (a leg-based kimura) is mentioned as one the instructor has always enjoyed and can be used quite successfully.
- Beyond that variation, many others exist too.
- But for the instructor's own teaching and the system taught to his students, these six are the ones actually used. As a general rule, other varieties were found to be either unnecessary, or better covered by other moves or other kimuras in the six-move family — so the system sticks to these six.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Five kimuras built on the figure-four grip cover each other; the sixth is fundamentally different — it starts from the legs alone, typically from turtle position, and doesn't use a figure four at all.
- The leg-entry sixth kimura is sometimes called a "reverse omoplata" but functions more like a kimura in its mechanics and pressure.
- Using all six as a team means resistance at any stage — entry or finish — has an answer.
- Other kimura variations exist beyond these six, but the instructor's system deliberately limits itself to these because the rest are redundant or better handled by other moves already in the system.
