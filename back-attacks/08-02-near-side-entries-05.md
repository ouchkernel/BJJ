# Near Side Entries 05

## Overview

Builds on the near side entry system with the "two legs establish one hook" principle — using one leg to physically restrain an opponent's leg so the other leg can enter as a hook without a fight. Applied to both foot entries and knee entries, and shown across breakdown and leg-drag scramble scenarios.

---

## The Two-On-One Leg Control Principle

- Previously shown with knee entries via a sagging entry: stepping over the opponent's foot so two of your legs pinch around one of the opponent's legs, creating a strong restraining pinch before the hook is even placed.
- Applied to foot entries with a different feel: one leg pins the opponent's leg at the hamstring, radically slowing that leg down and greatly facilitating the foot entry of the other leg.
- The hand is never strong enough to hold or move an opponent's leg against resistance — fight leg strength with leg strength, not hand strength.

## Hamstring Pin From a Breakdown

- From a tight waist breakdown, as the opponent tries to recover to turtle, plant the near knee/shin low, right at the opponent's tailbone.
- As the opponent fights to get up, weight settles over the hamstring — acting like a handbrake on that leg.
- Bring the head forward as the opponent drives up; the hook leg slides in effortlessly because the opponent's leg is already pinned and slowed.
- Without the pin, a fast opponent can bring the knee to the elbow and close the space before the hook can enter — the pin is what buys the time.

## Leg Drag Scramble Application

- Common scenario: after a leg drag pass, the opponent refuses to turn face-in (which would give up the pin) and instead turns away, trying to climb to turtle.
- Sit the shin down on the hamstring for maximal body contact as the opponent turns away and drives up.
- The pinned hamstring slows the opponent's leg, letting the other foot slide straight in as a hook.
- Seize the opposite arm for diagonal control so that when the pair falls to the mat, both hooks and the finishing grip are already in place.

## Applying the Principle to Knee Entries

- The same two-on-one idea works even more simply with knee entries: pinch both knees around one of the opponent's legs (wedging it between the two knees) before transitioning to the hook.
- Beginners typically just throw a hook in with no restraint on the opponent's leg; skilled competitors consistently use two-on-one control first — the hallmark distinction between the two levels.
- This method requires no flexibility to execute — usable regardless of physical limitations (demonstrated as effective even for an athlete with a hip replacement and pending knee replacement).

---

## Key Takeaways

- Fight an opponent's leg strength with your own leg strength — never try to hold or move a leg with your hand alone.
- Pin the opponent's leg at the hamstring with one leg to slow it down and open a clean lane for the other leg to hook.
- The tailbone-knee placement during a breakdown is what lets you catch a recovering opponent's leg before they can get up to speed.
- Two-on-one leg control (whether via knee or foot entry) is what separates championship-level back takes from beginner ones, who just throw hooks in.
