# Straitjacket System — Introduction

## Overview

A short introduction to the Straitjacket System (SJS) itself: what it is, why it's the most well-known and most statistically successful part of the whole back-attack system, and the competition track record that put it on the map.

---

## What the Straitjacket System is

- SJS is the most well-known and well-publicized part of the overall back attack system.
- It involves using the legs to pin the training partner's arms in a position where they become relatively helpless, opening up attacks to the neck via the rear naked strangle.

## Competition track record

- In competition, SJS has been the number one method of success from the back by a wide margin — statistically it isn't close between the SJS and the auxiliary parts of the system. The overwhelming majority of finishes have come from the SJS.
- Some of the most notable successes came in submission-grappling tournaments using an EBI-style overtime format, where competitors choose a starting position for overtime rounds (back position vs. armbar position). Athletes using this system would exclusively choose back position, knowing they had a system giving them a very high percentage chance of success.
- Their extraordinarily high success rate in EBI overtime relative to other athletes generated considerable interest in the SJS specifically.

---

## Key Takeaways

- SJS = legs pinning the opponent's arms to open a relatively defenseless path to the rear naked strangle.
- It is, by a wide statistical margin, the single most successful method of finishing from the back in this system's competition history.
- Its EBI-overtime track record (athletes choosing back position exclusively, with a very high success rate) is what drove broader interest in learning it.
