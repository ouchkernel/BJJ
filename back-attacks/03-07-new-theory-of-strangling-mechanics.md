# New Theory Of Strangling Mechanics

## Overview

The closing chapter of the volume explains the mechanical secret behind the students' unusually powerful and fast rear naked strangle finishes — including George St-Pierre's rapid finish of Michael Bisping, and finishes by Gordon and Nicky Ryan, Garry Tonon, and Eddie Cummings against elite opposition. Onlookers tend to credit strength or conditioning; the real explanation is a mechanical shift away from classical constricting strangle mechanics toward a rotational method that mirrors the mechanics of a gi collar strangle.

---

## The classical method and its limits

- Classical rear naked strangle mechanics are built around a **constricting** action: the elbow lines up directly with the opponent's chin, then the athlete drives the two elbows together in a forward contraction.
- This works — it's not being dismissed as ineffective, and even a poorly applied strangle can render an inexperienced opponent unconscious.
- But it's arm/pectoral/shoulder dependent, and that creates a real limitation: if the opponent gets inside position on your forearm and pulls vertically down his chest with all his strength, you can't close your elbows enough to generate sufficient pressure.
- The only "scissoring" effect in the classical method is your two elbows moving toward each other — purely a function of your pectorals, arms, and shoulders.
- Consequence seen often in competition: an athlete gets stymied on a rear naked strangle attempt, their arms fatigue toward exhaustion, the strangle fades out, and their performance drops for a period afterward while they recover.

## The rotational method

- Modeled on the mechanics of a gi collar strangle, where everything rotates — the choking elbow slides over the opponent's shoulder as the head moves away, regardless of exactly how the collar is gripped. Even the Japanese terminology for the classic gi strangle refers to a sliding action over the shoulder.
- Without a gi, the same rotational principle is applied: don't worry about lining the elbow up with the opponent's chin. Once hands are closed, the entire emphasis shifts to rotating the strangling elbow (right elbow, for a right-handed strangle) over the opponent's shoulder.
- This still works even when the opponent locks both arms in and pulls down as hard as he can — the rotation itself generates the pressure, independent of his counter-pull.
- Head position: rather than tucking the head down (which limits how far the elbow can rotate), look in the direction of the strangle. The instructor even rotates his own chin onto the back of the opponent's skull to help drive the elbow's rotation further.

## The mechanical reason it's stronger: coupled forces

- Classical method power ceiling = arm/pec/shoulder contraction only.
- Rotational method adds a second force: the chest drives forward into the opponent's back while the elbow simultaneously pulls back in the opposite direction.
- This creates two coupled forces acting in opposite directions on the opponent's neck — a genuine scissoring/turning force, not just a contraction — and the resulting pressure is described as "immense," holding up even against full two-handed resistance where the classical contraction stalls out.
- It also shifts the muscular load from arms/pectorals to the back, lats, and scapula — the same muscle groups used in a gi choke.

## Endurance advantage

- Lats can hold a squeeze for two to three minutes without much problem; biceps cannot — they exhaust quickly.
- Because the rotational method is driven by the back rather than the arms, the arms themselves never get tired applying it.

## Live demonstration described

- Two training partners pull down on the strangling arm as hard as they can, from a low, strong pulling position.
- The rotational, coupled-force method goes through regardless — described as too much force for arm resistance alone to stop.

## Why it matters at the highest level

- Strangles generally don't need much pressure to work against an unresisting or inexperienced opponent — even a poorly applied one succeeds.
- The rotational method matters most against elite, skillfully escaping opponents where you may not have long to finish and classical arm-based pressure may not get through (or may exhaust your arms trying).
- Result: faster finishes, and finishes that go through even elite, high-level resistance — cited as the explanation for the striking speed and power of finishes from students such as the Ryan brothers, Garry Tonon, and Eddie Cummings, and for GSP's rapid finish over Michael Bisping (a weight class above him).

---

## Key Takeaways

- Classical rear naked strangle mechanics = forward contraction, elbow toward the chin, powered by arms/pecs/shoulders — works, but has a real ceiling and fatigues the arms.
- Rotational method = rotate the strangling elbow over the opponent's shoulder (like a gi collar strangle), not into the chin.
- Power comes from coupled forces: chest driving forward + elbow pulling back, creating a genuine scissoring/turning force on the neck.
- This shifts the workload to the back/lats/scapula, which have far more strangle-holding endurance than arms/biceps.
- Look in the direction of the strangle (don't tuck your head down) to allow full elbow rotation — even use your own chin against the back of the opponent's skull to help.
- The payoff is most visible against elite, resisting opponents: faster finishes, no arm fatigue, and pressure that holds up under maximum two-handed resistance.
