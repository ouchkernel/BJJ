# Closed Wedges Principle

## Overview

The tenth and final critical principle. As with the leg lock system, Danaher has a general preference — not universal, but broad — for closed wedges over open wedges, because closed wedges add a security of grip that open wedges lack. Applied to the back, this principle governs how the knees and feet should be used for lower-body control, with a ranked series of fallback options depending on body proportions.

---

## The knees, not just the feet, are the hooks

- In the back position, the knees function as wedges around the opponent's body and are the real source of lower-body control — the "hooks" people usually think of as the lower legs (knee to foot) are, more precisely, based on the knees themselves.
- Simple proof: place the feet in what would be called a crab-ride position (feet not inserted as hooks at all) — you still retain excellent control of the opponent's hips purely from knee position.
- With open feet, the opponent's path to escape is to beat the foot first, then critically beat the knee, and get out of the back position.

## Ranked options for closing the wedge

Anything that prevents the opponent from beating the foot and then the knee is valuable. In order of preference, based on your leg length and your opponent's build:

1. **Body triangle (figure-four) locked on the outside** — the single strongest lock available; this is the general preference when leg length and the opponent's waist size allow it. The two knees lock fully around the opponent's body, making it significantly harder for him to misalign himself from you.
2. **Body triangle locked on the inside** — second preference, when the outside lock isn't available.
3. **Crossed feet locked over one hip** — nearly as good as the body triangle; especially useful when your legs are short or your opponent is generously built through the waist. (Avoid crossing feet directly between the opponent's legs — that leads to different problems, though there are simple solutions for it.)
4. **Feet held close together without locking** — post one foot on the opponent's hip, bring the other foot up close to it. Technically still an open wedge, but the closeness gets most of the benefit of a closed wedge without requiring the leg length or opponent slimness a true lock demands. Far better than symmetric, wide-open feet, which let even small movements beat the hooks.
5. **Upper-body closure as a last resort** — when body proportions make any lower-body closure impossible (very short legs against a very thick-waisted opponent), close the wedge with the upper body instead: lock the hands/arms around the head and shoulders in a tight seatbelt-style grip. Even if the opponent beats the bottom hook, this upper-body lock still lets you maneuver, come up, and turn him back into a controlling position.

## The general rule

- Preference order: full body triangle (outside, then inside) > crossed feet over the hip > feet held close without locking > upper-body closure only when lower-body closure truly isn't achievable.
- The underlying goal never changes: prevent the opponent from misaligning his body from yours by closing whatever wedge — lower body or upper body — is actually available given the two athletes' proportions.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The knees, not the feet, are the true basis of hook control from the back — feet alone with open wedges let the opponent beat the foot, then the knee, then escape.
- Preference order for closing the wedge: body triangle outside > body triangle inside > crossed feet over the hip > feet held close without locking > upper-body seatbelt closure as the last resort.
- Choose the option based on your leg length relative to your opponent's waist size — don't force a body triangle that your proportions don't support.
- Even when a true lock isn't possible, closeness alone (feet held tight together, or an upper-body lock) still meaningfully restricts the opponent's ability to misalign and escape.
