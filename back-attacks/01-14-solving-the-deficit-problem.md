# Solving The Deficit Problem

## Overview

Having covered ways to stay aligned, the course returns to the second central problem: the deficit problem, where the attacker is always numerically outnumbered from back position. Before introducing the actual hand-fighting solution, this chapter first dismantles a widely held piece of jiu-jitsu orthodoxy — the idea of a "strong side" and a "weak side" — because clearing up that misconception is what frees the attacker to choose whichever side actually helps solve the deficit.

---

## Restating the deficit problem

- From the back, the attacker has at most one or two offensive instruments: usually a single strangle arm (since the other arm is typically a control arm underneath the opponent's arm), or at most two strangle arms if a body triangle is locked in and frees up the control arm.
- The opponent has two or three defensive instruments: two defensive arms and a jaw, or one defensive arm and a jaw if the attacker is already controlling one of his arms.
- No matter how the arms are configured, the attacker is always outnumbered — a strange conclusion for what's supposed to be the single best attacking position in the sport. The straitjacket system's hand-fighting/leg-trapping core exists specifically to redress this imbalance, by using a leg as an additional hook to trap one of the opponent's arms and create an uncontested strangle hand.

## Dispelling the strong-side / weak-side myth

- Conventional wisdom: once hooks are in, there's a gap between the control hand and the attacker's own upper body on one side. Through that gap, the opponent's head can move and break the head trap — so that side gets called the "weak side." Falling the other way, straight into the arm, is called the "strong side."
- The author explicitly rejects this framing and doesn't even use the terms strong side/weak side — he speaks instead of **underside** and **overside**, and treats his system as neutral as to which way he falls. In some cases he actually prefers falling to what most people would call the weak side, because it makes it easier to use the legs to trap the opponent's arms — exactly the tool needed to solve the deficit problem.

## What actually determines ease of escape: the closed circle

- The real variable isn't which side you fall to — it's whether there's an open gap for the opponent's head to travel through. If the hand and head are poorly placed, yes, there's a wide-open hole and the escape is easy regardless of side.
- **Upper-body closed circle:** the strangle hand goes over the opponent's shoulder and the attacker's own chin comes down to touch that same hand (ear to ear, head forward on the training partner). This seals the gap completely — there's no hole left for the head to travel through, on either side.
- **Lower-body closed circle:** this is exactly the protected bottom hook from earlier in the course (post rear mount or body lock) — sealing the gap at the hips the same way the chin-to-hand grip seals the gap at the shoulder.
- With both circles closed — upper and lower body — escaping to one side becomes exactly as difficult as escaping to the other. Side no longer determines difficulty; posture and gap-closing technique do.

## Why this matters for solving the deficit

- Once you accept that side is neutral for safety purposes, you're free to choose whichever side best sets up the leg-trapping hand-fighting system — rather than being locked into the traditionally "safe" side purely out of habit, at the cost of never being able to free a leg to trap an arm.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The deficit problem: attacker has 1–2 offensive instruments (strangle arm[s]), opponent has 2–3 defensive instruments (arms + jaw) — always outnumbered.
- The traditional "strong side / weak side" distinction is a myth built on sloppy hand and head positioning, not on which direction you actually fall.
- Close the circle in both halves of the body: chin-to-hand over the shoulder seals the upper-body gap; a protected bottom hook (post rear mount or body lock) seals the lower-body gap.
- With both circles closed, side becomes irrelevant to safety — freeing you to fall toward whichever side best supports using a leg to trap the opponent's arm, which is the actual mechanism for solving the deficit.
