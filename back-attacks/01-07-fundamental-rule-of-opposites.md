# Fundamental Rule Of Opposites

## Overview

The single most important structural idea in the entire Straitjacket System: the **head trap**. Your head and your strangle arm must always be on opposite sides of the opponent's head, trapping it between them. This "rule of opposites" is described as running throughout the whole instructional series — everything the offensive player does is designed to preserve it, and everything the defensive player does is designed to break it.

---

## What the head trap is

- Whenever you're working a rear naked strangle from the back, you want your head and your strangle arm on **opposite sides** of the opponent's head, working in unison to trap it between them. (Other, non-strangle back attacks don't rely on this — but anything built around the rear naked strangle does.)
- Typical shape: a control hand on one side, a strangle hand on the other (e.g., strangle arm = right arm), with the head on the left — opposite the strangle arm.
- The moment your head and your strangle hand end up on the **same side**, there is no viable threat of a strangle. It becomes trivially easy for the opponent to turn inside you and face you, destroying the strangle entirely.
- It doesn't matter *how* this happens — whether it's your own error (head drifted to the wrong side) or the opponent's action (e.g., grabbing a two-on-one grip on your strangle arm and physically transferring it across to his other side). Either way, the result is the same: strangle arm and head now on the same side of his head, and the threat is gone.

## Offense vs. defense, defined by this one relationship

- As the offensive player, your entire job when behind the opponent is to preserve the relationship: his head trapped between your head and your strangle arm, on opposite sides.
- As the defensive player, the opponent's entire job is to break that relationship — either by shifting his own body/head alignment, or by physically moving your strangle arm across to the same side as your head.
- Every escape attempt the opponent makes, no matter how it's dressed up, boils down to trying to get your strangle hand and your head onto the same side of his head. Once you see it this clearly, both your job (preserve it) and his job (break it) become much easier to understand and anticipate.

## Why this is called the "fundamental rule of opposites"

- This is presented as a foundational insight that will run throughout the entire straitjacket system: your ability to create and maintain a head trap is the key to succeeding from the back; the opponent's ability to break it is the key to his escape.
- Once this relationship is explicitly understood (rather than obeyed only subconsciously, which most grapplers already do to some degree), everything else about attacking and defending the back position becomes clearer.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Head trap = strangle arm and head on opposite sides of the opponent's head, at all times. Same side = no strangle threat, full stop.
- This relationship can be broken by your own positional error or by the opponent's active grip-fighting (e.g., moving your strangle arm across) — both destroy the threat identically.
- The offensive player's whole job is preserving this opposite-sides relationship; the defensive player's whole job is breaking it. This single idea underlies essentially every escape and every counter to that escape throughout the rest of the system.
