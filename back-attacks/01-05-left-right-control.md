# Left/Right Control

## Overview

The first of the core preliminaries of back control: you must be able to control the opponent's turning movement in both directions, left and right, at all times. A common failure pattern — controlling one side of the body but not the other — is identified, along with the tools (elbow positioning, diagonal control, double hooks) that close the gap.

---

## Why left/right control matters

- Whenever you're behind an opponent, it's absolutely critical to be able to control their movement both left and right. A very common mistake is controlling one side of the body (right or left) while leaving the other open, which makes it relatively simple for the opponent to start escaping.
- If your opponent is ever able to fully turn — inside your arms or inside your hooks — they will eventually turn all the way into you, and you will no longer be on their back. Preventing left/right turning is therefore critical to keeping the position at all.

## Upper body: one arm under, one arm over

- The base back-control shape is one arm underneath the opponent's arm (control hand) and one arm over the shoulder (toward the strangle).
- With only a left control hand under one arm, the elbow behind the opponent's body restrains him from turning to the right — but it does nothing to stop him turning left, which becomes his line of escape.
- With two arms in front of the opponent (one control-hand style, elbow behind one shoulder; one elbow in front of the other shoulder), both directions are covered: the elbow behind stops one direction, the elbow in front of the other shoulder stops the other.

## Elbows, not locked hands, are the real control

- People commonly misunderstand back control as being about locking the hands together. Locking the hands really just holds the elbows in place.
- At some point you have to unlock your hands to work toward a strangle — with locked hands you present no serious submission threat. You have to be able to work with open hands and still control the opponent.
- The actual focus of upper-body control is the elbows: one behind the opponent on one side, one in front at the shoulder on the other side. As long as elbow position is maintained, opponent movement is controlled even with the hands open.

## Diagonal control (one hook + one hand)

- Sometimes you'll only have one hand on the opponent. In these cases, control of both sides has to come through the legs.
- If you only have a hook on the same side as your controlling hand, the opponent can still easily turn to the opposite side and eventually escape — hook and hand on the same side leaves the other side wide open.
- The fix is **diagonal control**: one hook and one hand on opposite sides of the body. If the right hook is in and the opponent turns left, the right hook stops him; if he turns right, the left hand stops him. Control runs in a diagonal line — through the right leg, up the opponent's right hip, to his left shoulder held by the hand.
- Diagonal control means that even with only one hook and one hand, movement is constrained on both sides simultaneously.

## No upper body grip = two hooks required

- If you have no effective upper-body control at all — not underneath either arm — the onus shifts entirely to the legs: you must have two hooks in, in some form (conventional hooks, or better, a body triangle).
- Without a control hand under the opponent's arms, only two hooks controlling both sides of the lower body will stop the turning motions that would otherwise take you off the back.

---

## Key Takeaways

- You must always be able to shut down turning in both directions — one-sided control is the single most common reason opponents escape the back.
- Elbow position (not hand-locking) is the real mechanism of upper-body control — this is what lets you go to open hands and still hold position.
- Diagonal control (hook and hand on opposite sides) covers both directions with minimal grips; losing your only upper-body grip means you need two hooks (ideally a body triangle) to compensate.
