# Overarm Side — Wedging Method

## Overview

The best time to trap an opponent's arm before falling to the overarm side is *before* committing to the floor at all — usually while the opponent is still in turtle position. This chapter covers the wedging method: using a knee as a wedge to break the opponent's base and trap an arm pre-emptively, with two distinct versions depending on whether the opponent is in a closed or open turtle position. Trapping the arm first and rolling into rear mount second — reversing the usual order — produces an especially strong trap, because the opponent's own body weight ends up reinforcing it.

---

## Turtle position: open vs. closed

- There are many forms of turtle position, but the simplest useful distinction is open vs. closed.
- **Open turtle**: no contact between the opponent's elbows and knees; elbows sit outside the line of the knees. Advantage: a wide, stable base of support. Disadvantage: relatively easy to penetrate and attack.
- **Closed turtle**: opponent's elbows come inside the knees, with contact between elbows and knees. Advantage: very defensively sound — hard to armbar, hard to strangle, everything locked in tight. Disadvantage: that tightness costs stability — the base narrows and becomes easy to pull over and off-balance.

## The law of extension and contraction

- A fundamental rule of jiu-jitsu: an opponent can have extension or contraction, never both at once.
- Contract everything in → difficult to submit, but easy to off-balance.
- Extend everything out → stable and hard to off-balance, but comparatively easy to finish with submission holds.
- Learning to play between extension and contraction is central to both versions of this method.

## Method 1 — Closed turtle: knee wedge to break the base

1. Start behind a training partner in closed turtle position. Apply a tight waist grip controlling the hips, with a hand on the floor, and block his knee.
2. Move your body across and use your knee as a wedge to pull him over — as his base breaks, break him down to a hip.
3. Because he was contracted, his only realistic response to avoid being flattened is to extend an elbow out to the mat and base out. (If he doesn't, you simply pull him straight into a pin.)
4. The instant he bases out with the elbow: go around the corner and lock a seatbelt grip. Rise up, taking weight off your knees, and place a wedge in behind the training partner's tricep.
5. This makes it easy to swing your foot around the corner and hook; rake the leg back and drive the knee to the floor as he returns to his base — the arm is now well trapped.
6. Put your head down on the floor next to his head, open your hips, and roll through. Both bodies' weight now lands on top of the trapped arm; insert your hook. The opponent has no defensive arms left, and you're in a perfect position to finish the strangle on the other side.
7. Reinforcement bonus: because both body weights are on the trapped arm, this trap is stronger than a same-side trap applied after the roll — the arm is pinned by both your leg *and* his own body weight.

## Method 2 — Open turtle: knee wedge behind the elbow

- The standard method for attacking turtle position covers the opponent's hips. A better alternative against an *open* turtle position uses a wedge behind the elbow instead — changing body position, placing the knee behind the elbow with the foot out wide.
- This variation gives better mobility around the head and shoulders and sets up attacks on the opponent's near-side arm; the classical hip-covering method is better suited to controlling the hips.

1. Post on the training partner's two hands. Rise, turning your head across him, and bring your knee off the floor.
2. Penetrate with the knee so it becomes a wedge behind the opponent's arm/tricep.
3. Foot-placement trick: point the knee *outward*, away from the opponent's centerline, and bring the heel in first — this makes contact easy with no flexibility required. (Pointing the knee across the opponent's centerline while his arm is tight makes the hook very difficult without a lot of flexibility.)
4. Only after the heel connects, turn the knee inward and start a raking action backward with the leg, catching the opponent's arm and driving your knee to the floor.
5. Put an arm around the neck, head to the floor, open your hips, and roll the training partner through. His secondary arm ends up trapped under his own body weight; insert the hook. Both arms are now constrained, giving an uncontested strangle hand.
6. If the opponent's chin happens to be down as the roll completes, this is an easy opportunity for a mandible strangle. Otherwise, finish with either a one-arm or two-arm strangle.

## Notes on grip and trap strength

- When crossing feet to lock the trap tight, the leg actually controlling the opponent's arm is the bottom foot in the crossed position.
- A strong grip on the opponent's knuckle line (rather than higher up the wrist/forearm) makes it very difficult for him to extract the trapped arm once you're rolling through.
- The usual order — take rear mount first, then look to trap an arm — is deliberately reversed in both versions of this method: trap the arm first, then take the rear mount / fall to the strangle side.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Trap the arm *before* falling to the overarm side — typically while the opponent is in turtle position — rather than trying to trap it after landing on a pinned leg.
- Distinguish open turtle (stable, easy to penetrate) from closed turtle (defensively tight, easy to off-balance) — the law of extension and contraction means the opponent must choose one weakness or the other.
- Closed turtle: knee wedge breaks the base → opponent bases out with an elbow → seatbelt grip → wedge behind the tricep → foot hooks around → roll through, arm trapped under combined body weight.
- Open turtle: knee wedges behind the elbow (point the knee out, heel in first, then turn in and rake) → roll through with the same trapping effect.
- Trapping the arm before the roll (rather than after) makes the trap stronger, because the opponent's own body weight reinforces it once you roll through.
- End state either way: an uncontested strangle hand, ready for a one-arm, two-arm, or mandible finish depending on how the roll settles.
