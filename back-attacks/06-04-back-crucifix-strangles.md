# Back Crucifix — Strangles

## Overview

The last of the auxiliary systems covered in this series. The Back Crucifix is presented as excellent, mechanically and tactically sound, and surprisingly underused in jiu-jitsu — Marcelo Garcia is named as the most notable practitioner. Unlike conventional back control (where your spine aligns with the opponent's), the Back Crucifix works with your spine perpendicular to theirs, while keeping the one feature that matters most: chest-to-back contact and an uncontested strangle hand. This chapter covers the position's theory, two ways to enter it, and the one-handed rotational strangle that finishes from it.

---

## How Back Crucifix differs from — and complements — conventional back control

- In conventional back control (hooks in), your spine lines up with the opponent's spine, and strangle attacks are built from that alignment.
- The Back Crucifix changes the geometry entirely: your spine runs **perpendicular** to the opponent's. The one thing that stays constant is **chest-to-back contact** — the feature that matters most for controlling an opponent and setting up an arm strangle.
- This makes Back Crucifix a natural complement to conventional back attacks, not just a standalone position. An opponent's primary defense against normal back control is to break spinal alignment. When that happens, instead of fighting to restore alignment, you can accept the misalignment and transition directly into a Back Crucifix — sustaining the attack instead of losing the position. Example: opponent beats the bottom hook and rolls over the top of your trapped arm — go straight into Back Crucifix rather than resetting.

## The head-trap principle (shared with the main system)

- For any arm strangle from behind, the strangle arm must be on the opposite side from your head. Head and strangle arm on the same side = no strangle, opponent escapes easily.
- This is usually established through a seatbelt grip or a garrote-style grip. It creates a wedge — your head on one side, your shoulder and bicep on the other — that physically traps the opponent's head between the two wedges. The moment your head moves to the wrong side, that wedge disappears and the opponent's head is free to move and escape.

## The uncontested strangle hand

- Back Crucifix constrains both of the opponent's arms — one by your legs, one by your hand — leaving you a completely uncontested hand to strangle with. This is the same underlying idea used in the main straitjacket system, just built off a perpendicular angle instead of spinal alignment.

---

## Entry 1: turtle breakdown

The biggest problem in getting to Back Crucifix is trapping the opponent's arm effectively — arms are often held in tight against the body.

1. Create off-balancing (kuzushi): hand in wrist-deep or elbow-deep on the opponent, pulling to force them to base out on an elbow. (If they just collapse onto a shoulder instead, that's a free pin — take it.)
2. As the opponent bases on an elbow and tries to recover back to turtle, break them down to the mat with a tight waist grip.
3. Post a fist on the floor — this noticeably slows the opponent's attempt to spring back up.
4. Catch and post the arm, take your knees off the floor, and penetrate with a knee.
5. Sweep your foot around the corner to hook the opponent's arm.
6. Hunt for the second arm as the opponent tries to return to base — catch it.
7. Result: Back Crucifix established, one arm trapped by your legs, one by your 1-on-1 grip.

## Entry 2: sit-through / seatbelt (for an "open" turtle)

Use this when there's space between the opponent's elbows and knees (an "open" turtle), or when the opponent uses a wide base you can't off-balance with entry 1.

1. Establish a seatbelt grip on the opponent's back.
2. Push your knee inside the opponent's tricep.
3. Level-change and drive the knee in, sitting on top of the opponent's forearm/your own buttocks.
4. Use the seatbelt grip to pull the opponent over and capture the arm.
5. Result: a supine position, one arm trapped by your legs, the opponent's hand trapped by your hand.

**Rule of thumb for choosing:** the more the opponent tucks their elbows in, the easier they are to off-balance to the side (entry 1). The more they open up / widen their base, the easier it is to simply penetrate with the knee (entry 2).

## The one-handed rotational strangle

With both of the opponent's arms pinned, this is the ideal moment to strangle — you have the uncontested hand.

### Flattened fist method (getting through the chin/jaw)

- Never lead with the thick parts of the hand and forearm — you'll always be shut out under the jawline.
- Lead with the **thinnest part first**, like the edge of a knife: the thumb knuckle.
1. Thumb knuckle enters under the jawline first, cutting a path.
2. Open into a flattened fist and finger-walk across the opponent's chest.
3. The wrist enters, the elbow follows.
4. Hand goes around the corner; chin comes forward over your own wrist.

### Closing the circle

- Picture the strangle arm as a circle around the opponent's neck. An **open circle** (a gap between your hand and your body) gives a poor strangle and a built-in escape route.
- A **closed circle**: hand goes all the way behind, chin comes forward over the wrist — the circle runs from your shoulder, around through your chin, up through your head, fully enclosing the opponent's head.
- Once the circle is closed, the finish is simply rotating your elbow directly over the opponent's shoulder (the **rotational method**).

### The #1 mechanical mistake: leading with the wrist

- Many people go across with the wrist first and always come up short — they never close the circle.
- Correct sequence: touch your **elbow** to the opponent's breastbone/chest first — that's what actually penetrates. Once the elbow makes contact, the hand effortlessly reaches full extension over the top and the circle closes easily.
- Your head has to move (go forward) for the arm to penetrate correctly. A stationary head means the hand fails to seat properly — the head moves forward at the initial penetration and again to seat the finish.

### Supine version

1. Sit down to your buttocks, pulling the opponent backward while catching the far arm.
2. Hand shoots elbow-deep behind the opponent; chin forward to close the circle.
3. Knee comes up as a wedge in the opponent's armpit, locking their active (far) arm in place.
4. Control the near arm at the **knuckle line**, not the wrist — controlling only the wrist lets the opponent free their wrist and start defending.
5. Rotate elbow over the shoulder for the finish.

### Prone version

1. Control the knuckle line of the opponent's hand at roughly neck height.
2. Post your free hand on the mat for stability while the opponent tries to twist or move.
3. When the opportunity appears, snake your hand through and over the top; head goes forward.
4. Raise the elbow to strangle — mechanically every bit as effective as a collar strangle.

### Drilling progression

1. Start without the Back Crucifix at all — just turtle position. Single hand through, square up to the opponent.
2. Instead of shooting the arm straight across to the shoulder (the common mistake that always comes up short), drive the **elbow** to the opponent's chest first.
3. Once that penetration feels natural, add the trapped-arm/live-resistance element and practice keeping your head moving as the opponent works to escape.
4. Self-diagnostic: can you get your wrist all the way over the opponent's trapezius? If not, the elbow isn't touching correctly and the one-handed strangle won't finish.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Back Crucifix trades spinal alignment for a perpendicular angle but keeps the essential ingredient of chest-to-back contact and an uncontested strangle hand.
- It's a natural complement to conventional back attacks: when an opponent breaks spinal alignment to escape, accept the misalignment and transition into Back Crucifix instead of resetting.
- The single biggest mechanical fix for the one-handed strangle: lead with the elbow into the opponent's chest, not the wrist across the front — the elbow is what actually penetrates, and the hand follows to close the circle.
- An open circle (gap between hand and body) is a failed strangle waiting to happen; the finish only works once the circle is fully closed and the elbow rotates over the shoulder.
- Choose your entry by how the opponent's elbows sit: tucked in → off-balance to the side; opened up/wide base → penetrate with the knee.
