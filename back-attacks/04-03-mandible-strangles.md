# Mandible Strangles

## Overview

One of the great consequences of the enhanced rotational mechanics covered so far is that reliable strangles become possible even without penetrating cleanly under the opponent's jaw. This is the mandible strangle — going directly over the jawbone rather than underneath it. Spectators are often confused watching these finishes: they assume the opponent tapped from simple pain. He didn't. Understanding why a mandible strangle actually causes unconsciousness — and how to tell a real one from a "butchered" one — is the subject of this chapter.

---

## Why athletes don't tap to jaw pain

- It is physically painful to have someone close a tight hold over the jaw and teeth — but at world championship level, athletes are not intimidated by pain. They've dealt with it their whole careers, and there's money, pride, and prestige on the line. They will not tap to simple discomfort.
- They tap because they're about to pass out.

## The physiology behind it

- No strangle is 100% efficient — none closes off all of the body's blood supply to the brain. Strangulation works by *reducing* blood supply, not eliminating it completely.
- There are four arteries leading to the brain, not two: two carotid arteries in front, and two (vertebral/subspinal) arteries in the back. The only way to fully cut off blood supply to the head is decapitation — obviously not a legal option.
- Because a strangle only needs to reduce flow, not eliminate it entirely, a mandible (over-the-jaw) strangle can still be effective with the right mechanics, even without clean carotid contact.

## Setting up the mandible strangle

- Preference is always to penetrate underneath the training partner's jawline first. The mandible strangle is the answer when that's genuinely not possible.
- Match your elbow with the training partner's chin — physically pulling the elbow around if necessary to get that alignment. This requires deeper penetration than a standard jaw strangle; the elbow has to go further around, and the hand can be used as backup to help secure alignment.
- Once alignment is set, use the same rotational finishing method covered earlier, going through the cross-wrist sequence to lock up hand position.
- This works even when the opponent grips your forearm as hard as possible with a strong defensive grip, jaw jammed down, and you have zero penetration of the jawline — the act of rotation alone creates a strong enough strangle.

## The empirical test: bloodshot eyes

- There's a simple, unfakeable proof that a strangle is actually working: bloodshot eyes.
- Demonstrated close to the camera: as the mandible strangle is applied — opponent's jaw down, no under-the-jaw penetration, both hands dug in defensively, shoulders raised — clear bloodshot effect appears in the eyes, confirming a genuine strangle rather than mere discomfort.
- Also demonstrated with hooks actually in, falling to a side, opponent successfully setting his jaw so under-jaw penetration is impossible: pull the elbow as far around as possible, cover the wrists, and the rotational finish still results in unconsciousness if there's no tap.

## What a failed (fake) mandible strangle looks like

- Common failure patterns: pressing a forearm over the teeth or nose, trying to lift the opponent up, generally "butchering" at a strangle. These are not real strangleholds — just unclean technique that annoys a training partner, could draw referee penalties in competition, and will never cause anyone to pass out.

## The defining test: elbow to sternum

- The key mechanical requirement: your elbow must touch the training partner's sternum.
- If the elbow does not touch the sternum, you are not performing a mandible strangle — you are simply causing annoying discomfort.
- Once the elbow touches down on the sternum, rotating over the shoulder produces an effective strangulation even against a fully set defensive jaw and grip.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Mandible strangle = strangling over the jawbone when penetrating under it isn't possible — not a pain-compliance technique, a real strangle.
- Physiology: strangles work by reducing (not eliminating) blood flow through four arteries to the brain (two front, two back) — full clean carotid contact isn't required.
- Setup: match elbow to chin (deeper penetration than a standard strangle), then apply the same rotational finish and cross-wrist hand mechanics.
- Unfakeable proof of a working strangle: bloodshot eyes.
- The test for a real mandible strangle vs. an annoying fake: **elbow must touch the sternum.** No sternum contact = discomfort, not a strangle.
- World-class opponents tap to the threat of unconsciousness, not to pain — that must always be the actual goal.
