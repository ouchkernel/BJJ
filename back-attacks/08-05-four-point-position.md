# Four Point Position

## Overview

Addresses a position increasingly important in both submission grappling (especially under ADCC-style point structures) and jiu-jitsu generally: the four point position, where the opponent comes up onto hands and feet (no elbows or knees on the mat) rather than sitting in a traditional turtle. Explains why the standard tools for controlling the back fail here, then introduces the asymmetrical rear mount as the answer, along with two ways to break the position down to the floor — an elbow-to-wrist method and the Power Half Nelson.

---

## What the Four Point Position Is

- Instead of a traditional turtle (elbows and knees down), the opponent comes up onto four points of contact with the floor: hands and feet, with no elbows and no knees on the mat.
- This radically changes how back control has to be maintained — the opponent's back slopes down toward the floor, creating problems for every traditional method.

## Why Traditional Methods Fail

- Seat belt: locking a traditional seat belt is a disaster here. The opponent only has to walk backward, and because of the angle of the back, a simple head shrug slips the seat belt right off — you inevitably slide off the opponent's body. Skilled bottom players deliberately tripod up to four point specifically to shrug off a seat belt.
- Symmetrical hooks: traditional square hook placement lines you up directly with the opponent's spine. If the opponent walks backward, your whole body is dragged along with them and hooks get lost — a common way back control is scrambled away from.
- Crucifix: even the crucifix, using the rear leg to hook, is awkward and doesn't work well against this angle.
- Net effect: the four point position is highly resistant to the traditional methods used to control the back and break an opponent down into the finishing system.

## The Answer: Asymmetrical Rear Mount

- From a standing rear body lock, bump the opponent down into the four point position, driving the leg in.
- Immediately pinch two legs around one of the opponent's legs (two-on-one) so a leg can't be left free for the opponent to roll into and attack.
- Golden rule against four point: never go over-under (the standard seat belt setup). Always stay with both arms behind the opponent's two arms — this is what prevents the slippage problem that dooms a traditional seat belt here. If you go over-under and the opponent walks backward, you slip straight off; staying behind both arms keeps you glued to the body no matter which way the opponent moves.
- Throw the far hook in first — normally cautioned against on the floor, but safe here because the opponent's weight is already on their hands (they're already "stood up," so there's no risk of them standing up out of the position).
- Set the hooks asymmetrically rather than square: one hook shallow (ankle-deep) and one hook deep (knee-deep). This asymmetry is what allows hanging off the opponent's body using the legs alone, with no hands.
- With the hooks set, thread an arm in behind the opponent's arm and bring the head down next to the opponent's head, loading heavy weight onto the opponent's hands.

## Breaking the Opponent Down

**Elbow-to-wrist method**

- Take the elbow to the opponent's wrist and draw both wrists together, breaking the opponent down to the floor into the winning position.

**Power Half Nelson (for a strong opponent)**

- When the opponent is too strong to move by the wrist directly, come up and place a bar across the back of the neck.
- Bring the hands through and lock palm-to-palm.
- Push the opponent's head down — driving it underneath the armpit rather than straight forward — to complete the breakdown all the way to the mat.

## The Acid Test

- The measure of whether the asymmetrical hooks (long hook / short hook) are correctly placed is the ability to hang your full body weight off the opponent using the legs alone, hands completely free — to the point where you can bring your face in front of the opponent's and look at them with no hands involved.
- If that's achievable, the legs are working efficiently enough to hold the position in a competitive context against a standing opponent fighting the four point position.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Four point position (hands and feet, no elbows/knees down) breaks the traditional seat belt and symmetrical-hook approach to back control — expect slippage and lost hooks if you try the standard tools.
- Golden rule: never go over-under against four point — always stay behind both of the opponent's arms.
- Set hooks asymmetrically (one deep/long, one shallow/short) rather than square — this is what lets you hang off the body hands-free.
- Two ways to finish the breakdown to the floor: elbow-to-wrist (draw the wrists together) for a normal opponent, or the Power Half Nelson (neck bar, palm-to-palm hands, push the head under the armpit) for a strong one.
- Acid test for correct hook placement: hang off the opponent's body using legs only, no hands.
