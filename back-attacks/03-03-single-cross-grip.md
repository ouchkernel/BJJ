# Single Cross Grip

## Overview

Cross grips are preferred over straight grips throughout the system, and this chapter introduces the minimum cross grip you should be hunting for the instant you get behind your opponent: the single cross grip. It's built out of double straight grips via a move called the "pass-off," which exposes and captures the opponent's wrist through what's nicknamed the "suicide zone."

---

## Why cross grips beat straight grips

- A cross grip does a better job of controlling your training partner by using his own rib cage and torso.
- It controls his elbow better: when he yanks away, his elbow stays pinned close to his own body, and your elbow is reinforced by his rib cage.
- Contrast with a straight grip: your elbow is unsupported and his elbow is unrestrained, so a violent yank from him often results in a breakout.
- Cross gripping also creates a "slipping effect" — like going to the end of a rope — using the added width of the hand to your advantage, so when he pulls away it's much harder for him to slide free.
- Because of all this, cross grips are rated higher than straight grips in the overall hierarchy.

## The single cross grip: minimum requirement

- The single cross grip is the floor — the minimum grip you need to be dangerous from the back. There are better grips (covered in later chapters), but if you can get at least a single cross, you can hook onto most people's arms, pin them behind the back, and strangle.

## Getting there: the pass-off

1. Start from double straight grips, with one arm under and one arm over as usual, primary defensive hand already tied up.
2. As you fall to your side, get away from straight gripping as early as possible.
3. Take your hand and insert it between your two existing hands.
4. Turn your opponent's hand to expose the **suicide zone** of his wrist.
5. Slide the blade of your hand between your two hands and cover the suicide zone with your thumb.
6. When your training partner yanks to escape, he only tightens the knot at the end of the rope — you now have a **single cross grip**.

## Why the cross grip controls the chest-line demarcation so well

- The single cross grip does an excellent job of keeping the opponent's wrist below the pectoral demarcation line by itself.
- Because of the thumb's inside position, if he tries to bring the wrist north toward his chin, your thumb blocks it directly — a strong mechanical stop, not just positional control.

## Finishing from single cross

1. Once you have single cross, there's no need to wait for anything else — his other arm doesn't matter yet.
2. Throw your foot over and touch your own hand with it (prior perception).
3. Use the heel to thread through and put the secondary hand behind his back.
4. Go hunting for the primary defensive hand, catch it, pass it, and lock it up on the knuckle line.
5. Result: two trapped arms and an **uncontested strangle hand** — the constant goal of the whole system.

## The contested-strangle problem this solves

- Without this system you end up "outnumbered three to one": two defensive hands and a chin against your one strangle hand — described as a disaster, and a familiar frustrating experience even after passing guard and taking the back with hooks/points scored.
- The entire point of every hand-fighting sequence in the system is to eliminate that contested situation and arrive at an uncontested strangle hand.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Cross grips > straight grips: better elbow control (both his and yours), plus a rope-end slipping effect that makes escapes harder.
- Single cross is the minimum viable grip from the back — get at least this every time.
- Build it via the pass-off: insert your hand between your existing grips, turn to expose the suicide zone, cover it with your thumb.
- Single cross alone strongly resists the wrist rising above the chest line, thanks to the thumb's inside position.
- Once you have single cross, go immediately — foot over via prior perception, shoelaces behind the back, then hunt down the primary hand at the knuckle line for an uncontested strangle hand.
