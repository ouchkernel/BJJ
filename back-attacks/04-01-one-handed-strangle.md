# One-Handed Strangle

## Overview

A new approach to rear naked strangle mechanics — the rotational finishing method — makes it possible to reliably strangle people with a single arm. One-arm strangles have always existed, but historically they worked mainly for athletes with unusual physical attributes (grip strength, forearm size, etc.). This method needs none of that: just correct mechanics. Combined with the right head and hand positioning, a one-handed strangle built this way carries most of the finishing power of the full two-arm version — and it solves a very specific, common problem: what to do when your control arm can't be extracted to set up the conventional two-handed finish.

---

## The problem it solves

- Classic finishing position: behind the training partner, forearm penetrated to the underside, hooked into a strangle-type situation.
- Often it's simply not possible to extract the control arm cleanly to apply the conventional two-handed strangle — the opponent's primary defensive hand is actively fighting to keep that arm pinned or monitored.
- Whether the hand you're holding is the opponent's secondary arm or something else doesn't matter — once the neck is penetrated, it's time to strangle, and a very strong strangle is available even with just one arm in place.

## The core mechanical idea: closing the circle

- Simply placing the hand on the opponent's shoulder and squeezing the wrist toward your own shoulder will not strangle many people.
- The hand needs to penetrate all the way through, behind the training partner's back, while the jaw comes forward — this closes a circle around the opponent's neck.
- When the opponent tries the ordinary defensive head movement to slip out, the closed circle makes escape very difficult.
- Contrast: if the hand is forward but the head is back, there's an open hole in the circle — exactly where the opponent escapes and gets away.
- Practical execution: turn the wrist to meet the chin, drop the elbow, and rotate over the shoulder using the rotational method.
- Done correctly, there is not much practical difference in finishing power between a one-handed strangle and the conventional figure-four two-arm strangle — it's an intimidating tool.

## Step-by-step sequence

1. From behind the training partner, penetrate with the chin down, using the flat fist method.
2. Walk the arm across — don't settle for merely gripping the shoulder, and don't settle for a gap between chin and wrist. A gap will deteriorate into a pull back into the throat, which is little more than an annoyance to a high-level opponent.
3. Bring the arm all the way through; the hand tucks around the corner.
4. Critically, the chin comes forward and touches your own wrist — this is what closes the circle.
5. Even if the opponent manages to extract an arm, grab your forearm, pull down on the wrist, crunch the neck, round the shoulders, and do everything textbook defense calls for, the rotational finish from this position is still too much.

## Why it works despite the defense

- The one-handed strangle built this way delivers around 90% of the finishing power of a full two-arm strangle.
- It's a fast, high-percentage way to apply extreme pressure with just one arm — useful exactly in the moment the second (control) arm can't be freed to complete a conventional finish.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Don't wait for a clean two-handed setup — a properly built one-handed strangle carries ~90% of a two-arm strangle's finishing power.
- The mechanical key is closing the circle: hand penetrates all the way through behind the back while the jaw comes forward to meet the wrist. Hand forward with head back leaves an escape gap.
- Use the flat fist method to walk the arm through, then chin-to-own-wrist, then rotate over the shoulder for the finish.
- This method requires no special physical attributes — just the mechanics of closing the circle and rotating to finish.
- Works even against a fully engaged defense (forearm grabbed, wrist pulled, neck crunched, shoulders raised).
