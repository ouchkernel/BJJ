# Protecting The Bottom Hook

## Overview

Since the entire escape sequence starts with beating the bottom hook, the first line of defense is to make that step as hard as possible before it ever happens. This chapter covers three ways to reinforce the bottom hook through leg configuration, plus how to handle the common fear that locking the legs together (crossed feet / body triangle) exposes you to a footlock.

---

## Why conventional hooks fail

- With plain symmetric hooks, falling to one side drops one foot to a low position (the bottom hook) with essentially no elevation and no weight behind it.
- Because the hook sits so low and carries no weight, even a minimal movement from the opponent is enough to slide over it — it offers almost no real resistance.

## Three ways to reinforce the bottom hook

- **Post rear mount.** Post the top foot on the opponent's hip and drive weight through it like a bridging motion, then bring the other foot up to meet the posted one. This does two things: it loads real body weight into the opponent's hips (making hip movement difficult) and it raises the bottom hook's elevation so the opponent has much further to travel to beat it.
- **Figure-4 body triangle.** From the post rear mount (or directly), lock a body triangle around the opponent's abdomen — preferably with the leg on the outside, though a compromise version (one conventional hook plus a body triangle) also works well. This creates a closed set of wedges: the bottom hook is now reinforced by the other leg, making it very difficult to untie.
- **Crossed feet under the hamstring.** Go underneath the opponent's leg and cross your feet under the hamstring, feet pointing in opposite directions. This closes a wedge around the hips almost as effectively as a body triangle. Important detail: cross over the opponent's hip, not just behind their back — going in behind the back alone doesn't give adequate hip control.

## Choosing which reinforcement to use

- **Opponent big through the waist, your legs relatively short** → post rear mount (adds weight and elevation without needing to close a triangle around a wide midsection).
- **Opponent slim through the waist, or you have long legs** → body triangle (outside preferred; inside-leg body triangle as an alternative).
- **Opponent big through the waist and a body triangle isn't achievable** → crossed-ankle rear mount under the hamstring as the fallback.

## Dispelling the footlock fear

- The standard jiu-jitsu warning against crossing feet in rear mount assumes the feet are positioned poorly — inside the opponent's hips. Crossed feet positioned *outside* the opponent's hips carry minimal footlock risk.
- Even in a worst-case scenario (feet poorly placed and an opponent attacking with a footlock counter, or walking the legs to try to finish a triangle-style break over the crossed feet/body triangle), the counter is simple: every one of these attacks requires the opponent to **arch** — head back, pelvis forward — to generate pressure. Post both hands on the opponent's head and stiff-arm; the arch produces no pressure at all. While he's stuck arching with no leverage, simply turn and extract the foot and return to the finishing position.
- This is an advanced-level tactic. Beginners should still be taught the conventional rule of not crossing feet — but at high levels, the security gained from a body lock or crossed feet is worth it, and the counters to it are trivially easy to shut down. Don't be intimidated by the threat of footlock counters to a locked-in back position.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The escape sequence always starts with beating the bottom hook — reinforcing it stops the whole chain before it starts.
- Three reinforcement options: post rear mount (weight + elevation), figure-4 body triangle (closed wedge), crossed feet under the hamstring (closed wedge, hip-crossing required).
- Choose by body type: big waist/short legs → post rear mount; slim waist/long legs → body triangle; big waist with no body triangle available → crossed-ankle rear mount.
- Footlock danger from crossed feet/body triangle is overstated when the feet are positioned outside the hips — any counter attempt requires an arch, and a simple stiff-arm to the head kills that arch completely.
