# The Back As Ultimate Position

## Overview

The opening argument for why the back is favored above every other position in submission grappling — mount, knee-on-belly, side control, north-south, all of it. Two structural reasons are given: a body-mechanics asymmetry and the primacy of strangulation over joint attacks. The chapter also names the problem this whole series exists to fix: jiu-jitsu athletes are excellent at getting to and holding the back, but far weaker at finishing from it.

---

## Why the back is favored

- **Asymmetry of threat.** The human body is well adapted to deal with threats coming from the front, and poorly adapted to deal with threats from the back. Nearly every combat sport chases the idea of a dominant angle — attacking from the flank — and the ultimate flanking position is being directly behind the opponent.
- Working the back maximizes your ability to attack while simultaneously minimizing the opponent's ability to counterattack.
- **Primacy of strangles.** Submission holds fall into two categories: strangulation and joint breaking. Joint breaking is powerful, but there will always be opponents willing to absorb damage and surrender a joint to keep fighting. Strangulation is different — no amount of courage or mental toughness lets someone resist a fully locked strangle. They simply pass out.
- Because of this combination — best angle plus access to the highest-certainty finishing mechanism — the back is rated above mount, knee-on-belly, side control, and north-south in a pure submission-grappling context. In other rulesets (e.g., MMA) other positions can arguably be more dominant, but for submission grappling, back is king.

## The contemporary problem this series addresses

- A large number of modern jiu-jitsu athletes are extremely skilled at reaching and maintaining the back, but a much smaller number specialize in actually finishing from it. Notable exceptions who are as strong at finishing as they are positionally include Roger Gracie, Marcelo Garcia, and Felipe Pena — but they are the exception, not the rule.
- Part of the reason: the back scores heavily in points-based competition, and by the time an athlete gets there they're often so far ahead on points (takedown, guard pass, pin, etc.) that there's little competitive incentive to finish.
- This imbalance was exposed sharply by the rise of submission-only formats (e.g., EBI overtime), where athletes suddenly had to convert back position into an actual finish rather than just holding it — and many struggled.
- The intention of this instructional is to close that gap: give a systematic approach to finishing from the back using the same techniques, concepts, and tactics that have proven successful in championship competition.

## The shape of the system

- The rear naked strangle is established as the single most high-percentage finish from the back and is treated as the centerpiece of any serious back-submission system.
- No single move is unbeatable — the rear naked strangle will sometimes run into resistance it can't break through. A complete system needs a way to distribute attacks across the opponent's whole body rather than relying on one attack to one target.
- The system therefore has two parts: a **main system** (hand-fighting and leg-trapping technique built around setting up the rear naked strangle) and a set of **auxiliary subsystems** that open up submissions distributed across the rest of the body if the main system stalls.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The back beats every other position in submission grappling for two structural reasons: it exploits the body's front/back defensive asymmetry, and it gives access to strangulation, which cannot be willed through the way a joint attack can.
- Most athletes are strong at holding the back and weak at finishing from it — that imbalance is the specific problem this course is built to solve.
- The rear naked strangle is the centerpiece finish, backed by a main system (hand-fighting + leg-trapping) and auxiliary subsystems that spread attacks across the opponent's whole body when the main system alone doesn't get there.
