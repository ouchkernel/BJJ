# Solving The Pinned Arm Problem

## Overview

A third problem, distinct from alignment and the raw numbers deficit: which side you fall to determines whether your own leg stays free enough to actually trap the opponent's arm. Falling toward the strangle-hand side — the traditional "safe" choice — comes with a hidden cost: it pins your own leg and removes your ability to solve the deficit problem at all.

---

## The orthodox advice and its logic

- Standard teaching: when working a right strangle hand, fall to the right (the strangle-hand side) to maximize the chance of success.
- The reasoning isn't foolish — falling toward the underarm/strangle side leaves less space on the far side for the opponent to get his head and shoulders to the floor and break alignment, so it does offer some genuine alignment security.

## The hidden cost: the pinned leg

- Falling to the strangle-hand side pins that same-side leg to the mat under the opponent's own body weight.
- Once that leg is pinned, it can't be used to hook and trap the opponent's arm — which is exactly the tool the deficit-solving hand-fighting system depends on.
- Net result: more alignment security, but the deficit problem becomes nearly impossible to solve, since the opponent keeps his full complement of defensive arms and a jaw against your one strangle hand.

## The preferred fix from rear mount: fall away from the strangle hand

- When already in rear mount, fall **away** from the strangle hand instead — the exact opposite of the traditional advice.
- This leaves the leg on that side unweighted and mobile. As soon as the opponent's hand drops below the line of his chest, come over the top with that free leg and hook/trap the arm.
- Result: an uncontested strangle hand for the first time — both of the opponent's hands are now occupied (one by your control hand, one by your trapping leg), leaving a free hand to work the jaw and finish.

## The alternative fix from turtle: hook before you fall

- If the opponent is in turtle position (i.e., you haven't yet committed to the floor), you can hook his arm *before* falling — meaning the leg is never pinned in the first place.
- This makes it safe — even preferable — to fall to the strangle-hand side afterward: the arm is now trapped underneath both athletes' combined body weight, which is arguably a stronger trap than the unweighted-leg version, since escape now requires fighting through the weight of both bodies rather than just one.

## What not to do

- Don't commit to the floor first and only then try to fall toward the strangle-hand side hoping to hook afterward — by that point the opponent is already sitting on top of that leg, and hooking his arm becomes impossible. The only fix at that point is to switch sides.

## Rule of thumb

- **Already in rear mount:** fall away from the strangle hand, so that leg stays free to hook and trap the opponent's arm once his hand drops below chest height.
- **Entering from turtle, with the arm hookable beforehand:** fine — even better — to fall toward the strangle-hand side, since the pre-existing hook gets reinforced by combined body weight once you commit to the floor.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Falling toward the strangle-hand side (the traditional "safe" choice) pins your own leg, which kills your ability to trap the opponent's arm and solve the deficit problem.
- From rear mount, fall away from the strangle hand instead — this keeps that leg free to hook the opponent's arm the moment his hand drops below chest line, producing an uncontested strangle hand.
- From turtle position, hook the arm before committing to the floor — then falling toward the strangle-hand side is fine, and the trap is reinforced by both bodies' combined weight.
- Never sit down first and try to hook the arm afterward on the strangle-hand side — by then the leg is already pinned and un-hookable; the only recovery is switching sides.
