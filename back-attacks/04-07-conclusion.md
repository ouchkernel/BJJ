# Conclusion

## Overview

A wrap-up of the Straitjacket System (SJS) — described as the centerpiece of the entire back attack system, the source of the most competition success, and the position that disproportionately occupies training time. This chapter ties the underarm-side and overarm-side halves of the system back together around one single unifying goal.

---

## The core distinction, restated

- Most of jiu-jitsu describes falling to one side or the other as "strong side" vs. "weak side." This system rejects that framing entirely — the approach to the back is fundamentally neutral between sides.
- The real terms: underarm side and overarm side. Which one you fall toward doesn't matter.

## The single goal, regardless of side

- Whichever side you fall to, there is one clear goal: create an **uncontested strangle hand** — a state where there is nothing at all between your strangle arm and the opponent's neck.
- Achieving that always requires the legs, because of a structural problem that runs through the entire back position: the opponent will always have more defensive instruments (two arms) than you have offensive instruments (one strangle arm). This is the **deficit problem**.

## Underarm side — recap

1. Protect the bottom hook first — reinforce it, take it up high, and lock it in place if possible; at minimum, put weight on the hips. This makes it much harder for the opponent to begin misaligning his body from yours.
2. With that secured, restrain the opponent's two arms. The legs are typically used to restrain the secondary defensive arm — a hierarchy of grips culminating in the double cross grip lets you hook a leg over the secondary arm and pin it behind the opponent's back.
3. Then constrain the primary defensive arm, so nothing remains between the strangle arm and the neck but the chin.
4. Penetrate with the flat fist method, then finish with the rotational method — closer in spirit to gi strangles than no-gi strangles, with the elbow rotating over the shoulder. This produces very strong strangles one-handed, two-handed, or even directly over the mandible when under-the-jaw penetration isn't available.

## Overarm side — recap

- Typically used when the opponent is in a turtle position, or a similar posture with elbows off the mat (e.g., a restless referee's position).
- Needs a wedge behind the training partner's tricep, then a leg comes around to hook.
- Roll through first, trap the arm second, then use body weight to help pin the trapped arm before finishing.
- When the arm can't be trapped before the roll (opponent already sitting on the bottom leg), side switching provides the fallback: trap the primary arm with the free leg, recover a control hand, pommel the head to the correct side, then finish.

## The unifying takeaway

- Which side you fall to is not the important variable — it never was.
- What matters is reaching the uncontested strangle hand, by whichever route the position offers.
- Doing so resolves the two central problems that make finishing from the back difficult: the **alignment problem** (keeping strangle arm and head on opposite sides of the opponent's head) and the **deficit problem** (having fewer offensive tools than the opponent has defensive ones).

---

## Key Takeaways

- The Straitjacket System is neutral between underarm side and overarm side — "strong side / weak side" thinking is discarded entirely.
- The single goal on either side is the uncontested strangle hand: nothing between your strangle arm and the opponent's neck.
- The legs are what make this possible, because they compensate for the deficit problem — the opponent's two defensive arms vs. your one strangle arm.
- Underarm side: protect the bottom hook, trap the secondary arm with the legs (double cross grip), trap the primary arm, penetrate, rotational finish.
- Overarm side: wedge behind the tricep from turtle position, roll and trap the arm (or side-switch if already fallen), pin with body weight, finish.
- Success from the back ultimately comes down to solving two problems: the alignment problem and the deficit problem — the entire system is built to solve both, regardless of which side you end up on.
