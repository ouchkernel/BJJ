# Near Side Entries 02

## Overview

Builds on the previous chapter's low-amplitude knee entry to handle a resisting opponent who refuses to stay broken down, and introduces the diagonal control principle for stabilizing a single hook until the second one is available.

---

## Leg-assist entry against strong resistance

- Some opponents know that if they stay broken down, you'll finish establishing hooks and get rear mount — so they fight hard to return to turtle position after every breakdown. This is common in ADCC-style competition (turtle scores nothing against you, but giving up hooks is dangerous) and in submission grappling generally (hooks strongly correlate with a following strangle).
- When you put weight over the opponent's head to sag him down and he resists hard, watch his hips: **his buttocks rising** is the tell that he's fighting the breakdown.
- The moment you see that: step your foot over and inside the opponent's ankle.
- As he resists the turtle breakdown, put weight over his head and pull on his leg — this physically drags your hook into place as you break him down, even if he then scrambles back up to his base and you follow.
- Result: a hook is established even against an opponent actively fighting to prevent it.

## Diagonal control

- One hook alone is not enough — it only controls one side of the opponent's body, and the "infallible rule of the back" is that **both sides must be controlled** at all times.
- The moment you have one hook in, immediately snatch a **one-on-one grip on the far side** with your free hand.
  - Near-side hook controls that side of the body.
  - Far-side one-on-one controls the opposite side.
  - Together, if the opponent tries to move in either direction, both sides are accounted for.
- Without the far-side arm control, an opponent can easily sit through to guard on the uncontrolled side. With diagonal control in place, if he tries to sit to guard, it becomes easy to roll across his body into a good attacking position instead.
- General principle for maintaining control with partial tools:
  - Only hooks available → need **two** hooks.
  - Only arms available → need an **over-under** (or similar) grip controlling both sides.
  - Only **one** hook available → pair it with an **opposite-side arm** grip (diagonal control).
- Demonstrated again facing the camera: seat belt → knee penetrates → break opponent down → step over the ankle to secure the hook. When the second hook isn't immediately available, throw the far-side hand in for diagonal control instead — this keeps you attached to the opponent no matter which way he moves (rolling through, falling to a side, etc.), ready to progress into the finishing systems covered earlier in the series.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Tell for a resisting opponent: buttocks rising while you apply head pressure = he's fighting the breakdown — that's your cue to step your foot over and inside his ankle and pull the leg to drag the hook into place.
- One hook only ever controls one side of the body — the infallible rule of the back demands control of both sides at all times.
- Diagonal control = near-side hook + far-side one-on-one grip, used to stabilize a single-hook position until the second hook is available.
- General formula: 1 hook alone is insufficient → pair with an opposite-side arm grip; 2 hooks, or an over-under grip with the arms, are the other ways to satisfy "both sides controlled."
