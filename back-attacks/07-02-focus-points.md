# Focus Points

## Overview

Before getting into concrete hook entries, this chapter lays out the general theory for working against a turtled opponent: the stances/postures you can take around his body, and the three "focus points" — specific areas of his body — that you actually control, since you can never control his entire body at once. Turtle position is described as probably the single most common position worked against when establishing hooks, and a strong defensive (sometimes even offensive) position in its own right.

---

## Three stances/postures around turtle

There are three good starting configurations for positioning yourself around a turtled opponent. All three are usable; which one you take shapes what grips and attacks are available.

### 1. Knee-pinch squat (diagonal line)

- Take a low squat with both knees down, pinching your knees around the opponent's hips — knee low on the far hip, even lower on the near hip.
- This lines your body up diagonally across the opponent's body (e.g., his right hip to his left shoulder, or vice versa).
- From here, secure either a wrist or a tight waist grip; work a hand underneath the opponent's forearm to set up a seat belt, or a wrist on the far side, near side, or both.
- Main advantage: mobility. You're not really on the floor — you're in a low squat, so you can move with your opponent rapidly, including if he stands up.

### 2. Grounded knee with chest-to-back contact

- Ground one knee next to the opponent's knee; the other leg stays low with the knee on his far hip.
- Critical detail: your knee must be at the same height as his lower back, pressuring forward into him — **not** hamstring draped over his lower back. Hamstring-on-lower-back is not a controlling position; if the opponent stands up suddenly from there, you have no weight on him and fall off.
- Done correctly, this gives strong chest-to-back contact — you can go underneath both his arms, take a seat belt, a single wrist, or a tight waist. All are good; the key element is keeping real weight/control (not hamstring across the back).

### 3. Knee-outside-hip with an unweighted leg

- Bring your rear knee forward, positioned outside the opponent's hip.
- Your other leg goes up in line with his hands — unweighted.
- Typically paired with a seat belt grip.
- Advantage: the unweighted leg is available for other purposes, notably setting up crucifix-style attacks.

**Summary:** knee-pinch squat = mobility; grounded knee = strong chest-to-back control; knee-outside-hip = control plus a free leg for crucifix setups. At any point you can pop between these three postures as you move around the opponent.

## Three focus points of control

You cannot control an opponent's entire body with your entire body — you have to use a large part of your body to control one specific area of his. Three focus points are recommended as the starting point for controlling a turtled opponent: the **hips**, the **head and shoulders**, and the **wrist**.

### 1. Hips — the tight waist

- Grip mechanism: the tight waist. Hand placement can vary by body type — long-limbed athletes may hold at the near waist; for most people the belly button area works; a shallower option goes wrist-deep around the far hip.
- The real target of control is **not** the belly/stomach where the hand sits — it's the **far hip**, controlled through pressure from your elbow. Don't focus on holding the stomach; focus on driving your elbow into the far hip.
- As the opponent tries to move away or return to turtle, the elbow-on-hip connection lets you pull him back and off-balance him, breaking him down to a hip and an elbow.
- You can switch the tight waist from one side to the other if you can't move the opponent from the first side.
- Purpose: breakdowns. Once broken down to a hip/elbow, the opponent has to transition back to turtle (or referee's position) to be athletic again — and that transition is exactly where many back attacks originate.

### 2. Head and shoulders — the seat belt

- Grip mechanism: the seat belt. Head goes across to the far side; far arm goes underneath the opponent's arm, near arm goes over the other arm. Lock hands with a five-finger grip (most versatile all-around starting option), palm-to-palm, or wrist-to-wrist — each has trade-offs.
- Two ways to use the seat belt:
  - **Hands-locked, head/shoulder retracted:** control runs through the opponent's shoulder girdle; gives your head mobility to move around and adjust chest-to-back distance as needed.
  - **Shoulder rolled forward onto the opponent's head:** creates very direct, heavy downward pressure directly on the head, making it extremely difficult for the opponent to rise or stand. This is directly analogous to rolling the shoulder over the hip/thigh during a single-leg takedown to create downward chest pressure that sits an opponent down — same mechanical idea, applied to the head from turtle.
  - Neither is strictly better — they're different tools; head-back gives mobility, shoulder-roll gives maximal downward pressure.

### 3. Wrist — the one-on-one grip

- Used when an opponent is so tight and skilled at hand-fighting that a seat belt or tight waist can't be established.
- The one-on-one is very hard to deny: it only requires the width of your hand to penetrate, unlike a seat belt which needs your hand to travel all the way across the front of the opponent's neck.
- Entry point is behind the opponent's arm (near the tricep), where grip fighting is much harder than in front of his chest.
- Pull on the opponent's shoulder, punch your hand through like a knife, and secure the grip at the **knuckle line**, not the wrist itself — gripping the wrist lets the opponent extend the arm away and escape the grip.
- Once secured, this sets up similar breakdowns to the hip and head/shoulder controls, and opens non-back-related options (maneuvering the opponent over, etc.) as well.

---

## Choosing between them

- Which focus point you use is determined by what kind of turtle the opponent has adopted (open vs. closed/tight).
- A good general starting sequence: begin with a tight waist to off-balance the opponent; as he recovers, take a wrist; as he clears the wrist, come up and control the head/shoulders.
- Any one of the three (hips, head, wrist) gives an excellent means of control and a path to breakdowns that set up hooks. Competence in all three, and the ability to transition effortlessly between them, is the goal.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Turtle is the most common position you'll work against when establishing hooks — start your study here before scramble/other entries.
- Three starting postures around turtle: knee-pinch squat (mobility), grounded knee (chest-to-back control — never hamstring-on-lower-back), knee-outside-hip (control + free leg for crucifix setups).
- You can't control the whole body — pick one of three focus points: hips (tight waist, real control is the elbow on the far hip), head/shoulders (seat belt, hands-locked for mobility vs. shoulder-roll for heavy downward pressure), or wrist (one-on-one behind the arm at the knuckle line, hardest grip to deny).
- Tight waist breakdowns put the opponent in a non-athletic hip/elbow position; his need to return to turtle or referee's position is exactly where back attacks come from.
- Become competent in all three focus points so you can transition between them based on how the opponent turtles up and defends.
