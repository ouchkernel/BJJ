# General Reflections On The Back

## Overview

A short conceptual bridge before the technical material begins: a plain statement of just how tightly the back position and the rear naked strangle are linked, and a preview of what "core preliminaries of back control" are about to cover.

---

## The back / rear naked strangle relationship

- In grappling without the gi, and in combat sports like mixed martial arts, once someone gets to the back, the number one method of finishing — by a landslide — is the rear naked strangle (mata leão).
- The rear naked strangle can barely be separated from the rear-mounted position itself — the two go hand in hand. There are relatively few competition examples of rear naked strangles being applied from anywhere other than rear mount.
- The SJS is built with this tight relationship in mind, but goes further: it uses the legs to remove the opponent's main defensive apparatus — his arms — making the already-close relationship between back position and the rear naked strangle even more functionally effective.

## Setting up the technical core

- Before diving into the SJS itself, a set of "core preliminaries" of back control needs to be established. These aren't SJS-specific — they're general considerations that matter any time you have rear mount and are working toward a rear naked strangle, straitjacket or not.
- These preliminaries form the conceptual core of working successfully from the back toward the finish, and the first of them is the idea of left/right control.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The rear naked strangle and rear-mount position are functionally inseparable in no-gi grappling and MMA — almost all successful strangles come from back position.
- The SJS strengthens this natural relationship further by using the legs to strip away the opponent's defensive arms.
- A handful of general back-control preliminaries (starting with left/right control) apply regardless of whether you're running the SJS specifically — they're foundational to working the back at all.
