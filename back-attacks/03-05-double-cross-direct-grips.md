# Double Cross / Direct Grips

## Overview

The top of the hand-fighting hierarchy from the underarm side: double cross with both grips direct. This is described as the position most associated with student success in competition — not always attainable, but the standing goal whenever a choice is available. It's reached by exploiting the same natural, intelligent defensive instinct that gave up the double cross indirect grip in the previous chapter, but this time via a different opponent reaction: peeling.

---

## Getting there

1. Start from double straight grips, pass off to secure a single cross grip on the wrist.
2. If your opponent stays inert, you can simply attack from single cross directly — but no one competent will just sit there.
3. Opponents typically do one of two things:
   - **Block at the neck.** A losing move — fake the strangle, ignore the indirect grip he's created, and go through anyway to the uncontested strangle hand shown in earlier chapters.
   - **Try to peel the cross grip off** with his free hand, reasoning (correctly, in isolation) that the cross grip is the actual source of his problems — it's what's stopping his wrist from rising above the chest line and it's restricting his ability to pull the arm away.

## Why peeling backfires: the principle of top hands

- You started with two straight grips, both top-hand-dominant.
- You upgraded one to a cross grip (also top-hand-dominant).
- When he reaches over to peel that cross grip off, his hand becomes top-hand-dominant on top of yours.
- But you still have a spare hand. That spare hand simply comes in and pulls his peeling hand off in turn.
- Net result: **double cross, both grips direct** — the best position in the hierarchy.

## Finishing sequence

1. From double cross direct, throw the foot over the top (prior perception, as always).
2. Thread it behind the back.
3. Pass the primary hand back to the knuckle line.
4. Result: uncontested strangle hand, with his chin the only remaining obstacle.
- When your opponent tries to yank away from a full double cross, it's described as very, very difficult to escape.

## Why not just stay on double straight?

- Double straight alone is a workable, finishing grip much of the time.
- At higher levels, though, straight grips don't control the elbows — a strong opponent can violently yank and eventually break free, taking top-hand control of your hand in the process.
- Passing off into cross grips generally performs better because of the elbow-control advantages covered in the single-cross chapter.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Double cross with two direct grips is the best hand-fighting position in the system and the one most correlated with competition success — pursue it when a choice exists, but it isn't mandatory for success.
- Reach it from single cross by baiting the opponent's natural instinct to peel the cross grip off with his free hand.
- The "principle of top hands": every time he upgrades his hand position on top of yours, you still have a spare hand available to counter it — use it.
- Blocking at the neck is a losing defensive choice; peeling the cross grip is the natural, intelligent choice but it's the one that hands you double cross direct.
- Double straight is fine at lower resistance levels, but elbow control from cross grips is what holds up against strong, high-level escape attempts.
