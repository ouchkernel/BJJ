# Introduction To The Auxiliary Systems

## Overview

Sets up the second major pillar of the back attack system. The Straitjacket System (SJS) is built entirely around forcing one weapon — the strangling arm — through strong resistance on one small area of the opponent's body: the neck. No matter how good you get at removing the impediments (defensive arms, jaw, etc.) that stand in the way of that strangle, there will always come a day you can't punch that one weapon through that one small zone. The auxiliary systems exist for that day.

---

## The Straitjacket System's Blind Spot

- SJS's entire intention is concentrated force: one part of your body (the arms) attacking one very small, heavily-defended part of the opponent's body (the neck).
- A large amount of SJS technique is spent removing the impediments the opponent puts in the way — trapping the jaw, the arms, penetrating the neck — precisely because that one target is so narrow and so well defended.
- This concentration is also SJS's inherent weakness: when an opponent successfully pools all of his defense onto one small area, a breakthrough can become genuinely difficult, however good your technique.

## What The Auxiliary Systems Do Instead

- The auxiliary systems have a completely different focus from SJS. Their function is not to force one part of you through one part of them — it's to take your **whole body** and attack the opponent's **whole body**.
- By distributing attacks across the entire body instead of concentrating them on the neck, you become far less predictable, and the opponent is forced to defend a much broader area — a genuinely difficult thing to do well.
- The back attack system as a whole is asymmetrical by design: SJS accounts for the large majority of finishes and training time, but the auxiliary systems are what make the system complete rather than one-dimensional.

## Why Legs Come First

- One of the fundamental asymmetries of the human body is the gap between upper-body and lower-body strength — the legs are roughly two to three times stronger than the arms.
- In situations where the arms can't force an effective strangle, the legs will very often get the breakthrough that the arms couldn't. Many things that stop an arm strangle (an opponent's hands interfering, for instance) simply won't stop a strangle applied with two legs around the neck and shoulders.
- This is why the first and most important of the auxiliary systems is a switch from strangling with the arms to strangling with the legs — the Rear Triangle (Ushiro Sankaku), covered next.

---

## Key Takeaways

- SJS's defect isn't a flaw in execution — it's structural: one attacking tool against one small, heavily-defended target.
- The auxiliary systems fix this by distributing attacks with the whole body over the opponent's whole body, making you unpredictable and forcing a much wider defensive burden onto the opponent.
- Legs are two to three times stronger than arms — when the arms stall, switching to the legs is often the fastest way to get a breakthrough the arms couldn't find.
- This isn't a replacement for SJS — SJS remains the primary tool. The auxiliary systems are the backup plan for the day SJS alone doesn't get there.
