# Back Crucifix — Arm Locks

## Overview

Where the main straitjacket system concentrates fantastically strong attacks on one target — the neck — using one tool — the arms — the auxiliary systems do the opposite: distribute attacks across the opponent's whole body. This chapter covers the arm-lock side of Back Crucifix, which uses the strongest part of your own body (the legs) against a comparatively weak part of the opponent's (the arm) — a genuine equalizer for a smaller or weaker athlete against a bigger, stronger one. It also covers two dedicated entries, the mechanics of the leg-based arm lock, a way to pass the attack from one arm to the other, an alternative scissor-leg finish, and the tactical dilemma this creates between arm attacks and strangles.

---

## Why the legs beat the arms

- The human body is built so the legs are dramatically stronger than the arms. Even if an opponent's upper body is significantly stronger than yours, their upper body won't be stronger than your legs.
- Using your legs against their arm is a quintessential example of putting the strongest part of your body against the weakest relevant part of theirs — one of the easiest ways for a smaller, weaker athlete to control and submit a bigger, more athletic one.

## Entry 1: wrestling referee's position (opponent trying to stand)

- In a normal turtle with elbows down, the opponent can't stand up on you at all. The moment their elbows come **off** the mat, that's a clear signal they're trying to stand — and that's exactly when this entry works, because standing up gives you an extended arm to attack.

1. As the opponent starts to rise, turn the corner.
2. Throw your foot in immediately to hook the opponent's arm — this takes one arm out of their stand-up attempt.
3. Bring your hand underneath and use the **passing method** to draw the opponent's hand to you — don't reach for it directly, pass it hand to hand.
4. Lock up and bring your legs into a figure-4 pattern.

## Entry 2: front headlock / opponent on all fours ("four points")

1. Move your head across the opponent's body.
2. Enter with what looks like the "wrong" leg.
3. Use the same hooking method to bring the opponent's body/foot down to the floor, breaking them down.

## Figure-4 leg-lock mechanics

- If your right leg controls the opponent's right wrist, the right leg forms the actual triangle lock and the left leg forms the supporting half.
- **The initial figure-4 lock is not enough on its own to break the arm** — there's always an adjustment period:
  1. Open the triangle, flare your knees out wide.
  2. "Knee-shuffle" backward, using a free hand posted on the floor for stability (don't try to lock a seatbelt grip here — it's not stable enough).
  3. Wiggle your hips until you feel the **bone of your own hip** settle directly onto the opponent's elbow joint — not your inner thigh or groin, specifically the hip bone.
  4. Once the hip bone is on the elbow: flare the knees and arch your body forward — head rises, pelvis drives forward. That's the break.
- Common mistake: keeping your knees together. There's nothing to sprawl against when you arch forward if your base isn't flared and stable.

## Passing the arm

- If the opponent doesn't react to the initial arm-lock pressure, that's already a finish. Most opponents instead twist the arm out to escape the pressure — and that twisting motion is exactly what you want.

1. Put in a cross-face.
2. Extend the opponent's arm to create direct arm-lock pressure.
3. As the opponent twists the arm out to escape, switch your legs.
4. You end up set up on the opponent's **second** arm instead — repeat the figure-4 lock, knee-shuffle, and hip-to-elbow finishing sequence on the new arm.

## Alternative finish: scissor legs + jaw/head control

- From the supine, broken-down position (reached via the sit-through/seatbelt entry from the previous chapter), a figure-4/triangle leg lock isn't wrong — it can produce breaking pressure — but it leaves play in the opponent's elbow that lets them wiggle and slip, becoming a hard-to-finish "spaghetti" opponent.
- Instead, take the slack out of the opponent's upper body by controlling the head:
  - Cross your forearms and pin the opponent's ear to your chest — the more the head can move, the more the elbow can wiggle free — **or**
  - Control the opponent's jawline specifically (not the eyes or nose), turning the back of your hand into the opponent to restrict head movement.
- With the head locked down, **don't triangle your legs — scissor them instead**: one foot goes ankle-deep to the wrist, the other leg comes up as a wedge in the armpit.
- Bridge off the floor to bring the opponent's elbow directly over your hip for the break.
- Scissored legs are preferred over a figure-4/triangle here specifically because the opponent's elbow can't slip out the way it can from a triangle.

## The arm/strangle dilemma

- The more the opponent defends the arm, the more vulnerable they become to the strangle — and vice versa. This interplay is what actually produces finishes against tough opponents.
- Example: arm lock pressure is there but not breaking (opponent turning/twisting the elbow) → release, flare the hand through, and switch to the stranglehold.
- Example: opponent tightens their strangle defense (chin tucked) → in one motion, walk down the body and switch to arm-breaking pressure instead.
- Keep alternating threats between the neck and the arm — this dilemma is what eventually breaks through against a determined opponent.

## Back to the main system

- Back Crucifix connects fluidly back to rear mount at any time. If the opponent bridges to resist the strangle, shift them over using your hip, lock the figure-4 over the trapped arm, and transition directly back into the primary rear-mount system (arms trapped, etc.).
- This means you can move freely among Back Crucifix, rear mount, and their various finishes without ever losing control of the position.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The leg-based arm lock from Back Crucifix uses your strongest limb (legs) against the opponent's weakest relevant limb (arm) — a genuine equalizer for smaller athletes.
- The figure-4 lock is never enough by itself: flare the knees, knee-shuffle for adjustment, and find the hip bone directly on the elbow joint before arching to finish.
- If the opponent twists out of the arm lock, that twist sets up the *other* arm — pass the attack across via a cross-face and leg switch rather than fighting the escape.
- Scissored legs plus head/jaw control beats a triangled leg lock when the opponent is slippery — the triangle leaves play in the elbow, the scissor with the head pinned doesn't.
- Arm attacks and strangle attacks work as a permanent dilemma: pressure the arm until the opponent defends it, then switch to the neck, and back again — that oscillation is what finishes tough opponents.
- Back Crucifix, rear mount, and their finishes are all one connected system — you can move between them without losing the position.
