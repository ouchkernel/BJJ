# Near Side Entries 01

## Overview

This chapter defines near side vs. far side hooks, states the general preference for entering near side first, and walks through the first concrete technique: a low-amplitude knee entry off a seat belt grip that sags the opponent down and drops a hook in with minimal risk.

---

## Near side vs. far side

- In the overwhelming majority of cases, you'll be positioned on one side of the opponent's turtled body — sometimes fully perpendicular to him, sometimes only partially off to one hip — but almost always with at least a slight bias to one side.
- **Near side hook:** the hook on the same side you're already positioned on.
- **Far side hook:** the hook on the opposite side, requiring you to travel further to establish it.
- Starting off lined up dead-center behind an opponent is relatively rare.

## General preference: near side first

- As a general rule (not absolute — exceptions are covered in later chapters), enter the near-side hook first.
- Most beginning students find knee-based entries easier than foot-based entries, so near-side entries are typically demonstrated with the knee first, though foot-based near-side entries are also essential and will be used regularly.

## Technique: low-amplitude knee entry off a seat belt (tight/closed turtle)

Starting position: opponent in a tight, defensive, closed turtle (elbows in tight).

1. Establish a wrist grip, then transition into a seat belt.
2. As the seat belt locks in, punch your knee into the gap behind the opponent's tricep, over the thigh — point the knee back toward the opponent's hip/crotch.
3. Use your other leg as a drive/push leg.
4. Use a **sagging** method (not a forward roll): sag your weight over your shoulder, pull the opponent's body with the seat belt, and pinch your knees around his hip.
5. The seat belt locks you in place; extend your near-side leg to convert the knee's upper-leg control into a finished hook.
6. Open your second leg and shift your hips into the attacking position directly behind the opponent, ready to attack.

### Same technique, repeated from a facing angle

- Establish upper-body control (wrist → seat belt).
- Move around the corner, punch the knee in behind the opponent, pointing it toward the crotch.
- Sag down to the mat — this is a conservative sagging entry, not a forward roll (forward rolls are covered later in the course as a higher-amplitude method).
- As you drag the opponent down, your grounding knee shovels underneath his body, lifting his hips into the air.
- Maintain the seat belt throughout; your foot passes over the opponent's leg and you land in a strong attacking position.

## Low-amplitude vs. high-amplitude entries

- This sagging knee entry is a classic example of a **low-amplitude** method — the coach's general preference for establishing hooks.
- Contrast with **high-amplitude** methods (covered later in the course): spectacular rolling entries that cover a lot of distance with an athletic-looking roll.
  - High-amplitude pros: fast, and when executed well, very effective and visually impressive.
  - High-amplitude cons: real danger of slippage — a big athletic roll can slip off the opponent's back and land you in bottom north-south.
- Low-amplitude methods **drag** the opponent to the ground rather than throwing him — there's a lot less that can go wrong. They may not look as athletic, but they get hooks in with minimal risk, and they are the entries most used by the world's best competitors at the top level.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Near side = same side you're already positioned on; far side = opposite side, farther to travel. General rule: enter near side first (exceptions exist, covered later).
- Core low-amplitude knee-entry sequence: wrist grip → seat belt → knee punched in behind the tricep pointing toward the crotch → sagging drag to the mat while pinching knees around the hip → extend the leg into a hook → shift hips into position.
- This is a "low amplitude" entry — deliberately undramatic, drags rather than throws the opponent, minimizing risk of slippage.
- High-amplitude rolling entries (later in the course) look more athletic but carry real risk of slipping off and ending up in bottom north-south — low-amplitude entries are the more reliable, competition-proven default.
