# Neutrality Principle

## Overview

The first of the ten critical principles underlying the Straitjacket System (SJS). Traditional jiu-jitsu teaches that there's a "strong side" and a "weak side" to fall toward when taking the back. Danaher rejects that framing: his back system is neutral left or right, and he's equally happy to see an athlete fall to either side — provided one non-negotiable structural demand is respected.

---

## The traditional approach

- Classic back control: underarm, overarm, and head position form the primary upper-body control, hooks in for lower-body control.
- Tradition says: always fall toward the side of your strangling arm. The logic — when you fall that way there's no gap for the opponent's head to escape through.
- The fundamental demand behind this advice is real and non-negotiable: **your strangle arm and your head must end up on opposite sides of your opponent's head.** Fail this and failure of the whole strangle system is inevitable.
- The defensive man's entire game is to force your head and your strangle hand onto the *same* side of his head. That's his one guiding gambit. Yours is the opposite — keep them on opposite sides at all times.

## Why "weak side" isn't actually weak

- Falling away from the strangle-hand side (the so-called "weak side") is only dangerous because it opens a gap between your head and your hand — a gap the opponent's head can slide through, dragging your hand down as it goes and putting your head and hand on the same side as his head.
- The fix isn't which side you fall to — it's closing the distance between your strangle hand and your jaw. If you keep your hand and head connected through your jaw, you've closed the circle around the opponent's neck, and there's no gap left to exploit regardless of which side you fell to.
- The moment your head and hand come apart, the circle opens and the escape route appears — on *either* side.

## The neutral system

- Danaher's back system does not use "strong side" / "weak side" language at all. Instead: **over side** (falling toward the strangle arm) and **underside** (falling away from it).
- Both sides can be used with extraordinary success. Neither is intrinsically better — quality depends entirely on how it's used (i.e., whether head/hand connection is maintained), not which side it is.
- There's a real advantage to falling to the underside: it creates an unweighted leg. Under the right conditions, that free leg can be used to trap an opponent's arm, pinning it underneath your leg.
- Rule of thumb: if you ever feel you're losing head position relative to your hand, stop everything else, re-control your opponent's head, and reset before continuing to attack. The single most fundamental demand of back attacks — strangle hand and head on opposite sides of the opponent's head — always takes priority over whatever else you're doing.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The real rule isn't "fall to the strong side" — it's "keep your strangle hand and head on opposite sides of your opponent's head," which can be satisfied from either side.
- Close the distance between strangle hand and jaw (through head position) and the circle around the opponent's neck stays closed — no escape gap on either side.
- Danaher's terminology: over side / underside, not strong side / weak side — both are legitimate, viable attacking positions.
- Falling to the underside has its own advantage: an unweighted leg that can trap and pin an opponent's arm.
- Whenever head/hand connection is lost, stop and fix it before continuing the attack — this discipline is what actually makes either side safe.
