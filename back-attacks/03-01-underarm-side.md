# Underarm Side

## Overview

The Straitjacket System (SJS) splits into two halves depending on which direction you fall when you're on your training partner's back: the underarm side and the overarm side. This chapter covers the underarm side — falling toward the side of your control arm (the arm that goes underneath your opponent's arm), as opposed to the overarm side, where you fall toward your strangle arm (the arm that goes over the shoulder). Orthodox jiu-jitsu calls the underarm side the "weak side" and the overarm side the "strong side." That labeling is rejected here: the approach to the back is neutral with regard to side, and the underarm side in particular has real structural advantages that the "weak side" label obscures.

---

## Falling to a side, not straight back

- Never fall straight back in a straight line. When you do, your opponent can bridge, and as he bridges his head rises higher than yours.
- A high opponent head is disastrous for strangling: it becomes very hard to finish, and any pull he makes on your strangle arm becomes extremely effective against you.
- Rule: your opponent's head must stay lower than your head. The moment you see his head climbing above yours, stop whatever you're doing.
- Fix: pommel your legs underneath your training partner's legs, get a forward shift, put a single hook in, and break him down to a single hip. Once he's on one hip, his ability to bridge is strongly diminished and his head can no longer beat yours.
- This head-height rule is a standing proviso that applies throughout the whole system, not just this chapter.

## Underarm side vs. overarm side

- Behind your opponent with hooks in: the arm that threads under his arm is the **control arm**; the arm that goes over his shoulder is the **strangle arm**.
- Orthodoxy says falling toward the control-arm side (underarm side) is weak, and falling toward the strangle-arm side is strong.
- The system treats side as neutral — there is nothing intrinsically weak about the underarm side. It's only weak if you let conditions make it weak.
- This is also historically the most recognizable part of the back-attack system, precisely because it goes against orthodoxy: athletes deliberately falling to the "weak side" and getting an unusually high rate of finishes there.

## Why the underarm side works: the unweighted leg

- Falling toward the underarm side leaves your top leg unweighted — your opponent's body weight is not sitting on it.
- An unweighted leg is mobile. It can:
  - Lock down the body with a body triangle (inside or outside).
  - Post on your opponent's hip to add weight onto him.
  - Hook into your training partner's arms — the critical use.
- Falling toward the "strong"/overarm side does the opposite: your opponent's weight lands on that leg, pinning it uselessly under his body. You can't use it to trap his secondary arm, since he's sitting on top of it. Ironically, this actually strengthens his defense — "in that sense it is a strong side," but strong for him, not you.
- There are some arm traps available on the overarm/control side, but they're inferior to attacking the secondary arm with a free leg — covered later.

## The deficit problem

- Your opponent has up to three items of defense: two defensive hands plus his chin.
- You typically have only one or two items of offense (your strangle hand, maybe a control hand).
- You cannot accept being outnumbered in what's supposed to be the most dominant position in the sport.
- The fix is to bring your legs into the equation as offensive instruments, not just hooks for position. Legs immobilize arms, which restores numerical parity between your offensive tools and his defensive tools.
- This is only easy when the leg is unweighted — which is exactly what falling to the underarm side provides.

## Setting up the hierarchy of hand fighting

- Not all hand-fighting grips are equal — some are considerably better than others, though all are usable.
- The system works up a hierarchy from the bottom (weakest workable grip) to the top (best possible grip), covered chapter by chapter from here.

## Key Takeaways

- The Straitjacket System splits into an underarm side and an overarm side; this volume covers the underarm side.
- Never fall straight back — fall to a side, and never let your opponent's head get higher than yours.
- The underarm side is mislabeled "weak" — it actually frees your top leg to trap arms, which the "strong" side does not allow.
- The deficit problem (his 2-3 defensive tools vs. your 1-2 offensive tools) is solved by using unweighted legs as offensive arm-trapping instruments.
- Everything from here builds a hierarchy of hand-fighting grips, working from weakest to strongest.
