# Foot And Knee Entries

## Overview

Once you've broken an opponent down using one of the focus points, the next decision is how to actually insert a hook: with your foot or with your knee. This chapter covers the general theory of that choice — the trade-offs of each, the mistakes to avoid, and the underlying mechanics of how each entry converts into a working hook.

---

## The two penetration points

- Whenever establishing a hook, the two basic ways to penetrate into the opponent's body are the **knee** and the **foot**.
- General rule (not absolute): most athletes find it easier to penetrate with the knee first, foot second.
  - Knee entry requires less flexibility and makes it easier to maintain tight chest-to-back contact — but it's a two-stage process: knee goes in first, hook is established second.
  - Foot entry gives you the completed hook immediately, in one motion — but it demands more flexibility and can be riskier for chest-to-back contact if not done well.
- Both are essential skills long-term. Beginners are generally encouraged to start with knee entries — a little safer, easier, and requires less dexterity while you build a feel for maintaining body connection.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Losing chest-to-back contact.** The most common error: reaching for a hook by lifting the chest off the opponent's back. Almost always bad (rare exceptions exist).
- **Throwing a leg in blind**, especially a far-side hook first, with no weight on the opponent. At the moment your body weight comes off him, there's no control — if the hook doesn't fully land, or he stands up, you fall off in a clumsy, disaster-prone fashion.
- **Trusting a technique because it "worked."** Success against a weak or unaware opponent doesn't validate a method — limited success can actually reinforce bad habits. The real test: would this work reliably against a resisting world champion who knows exactly what you're trying to do? If yes, it's sound. If it only worked because the opponent didn't know what he was doing, it isn't.

## Two diagnostic signals of a flawed entry

1. **Loss of body contact.** How much upper-body contact does this entry cost you? If you have to fully separate from the opponent's upper body to get a hook in, that should set off warning lights.
2. **Loss of weight distribution/control.** If you have to take all your weight off the opponent in a way that leaves his movement completely unconstrained, that's also a disaster — there's nothing holding him down, and if he moves explosively you simply fall off.
   - If both signals are present at once, it's a terrible sign.
- Good knee entries almost never require sacrificing body contact — there are ways to manipulate the opponent's body so the foot (or knee) goes in without losing that contact.

## Knee entry mechanics

- The knee penetrates through the gap between the opponent's elbow, tricep/thigh area — a relatively small opening compared to a full leg.
- Because the entry point (the circumference of the knee) is small, knee entries work even against a tight, closed turtle — there is almost always a hole roughly knee-sized behind the arm.
- The leg is functionally divided into two parts for this purpose: the **upper leg** (hip to knee) and the **lower leg** (knee to ankle).
  - Phase 1: the knee (upper leg) penetrates and functions like a hook on its own — as you sag down toward the floor, the upper leg pinches around the opponent's thigh, constraining his ability to move that leg (he can still move his lower leg, but that's not the concern).
  - Phase 2: extending the leg converts the lower leg into the actual hook, completing the job. The upper leg's initial "hook" becomes the finished lower-leg hook through simple extension.
- Result: from a two-knee pinch around the opponent's leg, extension creates the hook, and it becomes a simple matter to shift and solidify into rear mount.

## Foot entry mechanics

- The foot enters directly as the hook, in a single motion — the moment it lands, the job is done.
- Requires more space and flexibility than a knee entry, and generally requires the opponent to be in a relatively open turtle position (a very tight/clammed-up opponent makes this difficult or impossible).
- Done well: fast, clean, and highly effective. Done poorly (without body contact/weight control): an absolute disaster with no control of the opponent.

## Foot vs. knee summary

| | Foot entry | Knee entry |
|---|---|---|
| Stages | One (foot = hook immediately) | Two (knee first, then leg extension) |
| Speed | Faster | Slower |
| Flexibility required | More | Less |
| Space needed | More (favors open turtle) | Less (small circumference works even vs. tight/closed turtle) |
| Chest-to-back contact | Harder to maintain | Easier to maintain |
| Risk if mishandled | High (loss of contact/control) | Lower |
| Recommended for | Experienced athletes, once comfortable | Beginners, as the default starting point |

---

## Key Takeaways

- Two ways to insert a hook: knee (two-stage, safer, less flexibility needed, small entry point works even against tight turtle) or foot (one-stage, immediate hook, needs more flexibility/space, higher risk if done poorly).
- Beginners should default to knee entries; both must eventually be mastered.
- Never sacrifice chest-to-back contact or weight control to force an entry — these are the two clearest signals of a flawed method, and having both at once is a disaster.
- Judge a technique by whether it holds up against a resisting elite opponent, not by whether it worked once against someone who didn't know what they were doing.
- Knee entry mechanics: upper leg (knee) penetrates and acts as an initial hook; extending the leg converts the lower leg into the finished hook.
