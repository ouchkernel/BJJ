# Rolling Entries 02

## Overview

Covers the second, completely different family of rolling entries: using the legs — rather than the upper body — to hook an opponent's legs and roll directly to the back. Built on the reverse underhook, this method is especially valuable out of scramble situations and is shown from side control, from mount (against the elbow escape), and from half guard top.

---

## The Reverse Underhook

- A normal underhook is one arm under the opponent's opposite-side arm (left under their right, or vice versa).
- A reverse underhook is same-side: your right arm goes under the opponent's right arm (or left under left) — not a conventional underhook at all.
- Its purpose isn't upper-body control — it's to expose the opponent's hips and lower back, which is what makes the leg-based rolling entry possible.
- The reverse underhook is the non-negotiable prerequisite for this whole method — without it, nothing else in the sequence works.

## Creating a Top Leg and Bottom Leg

- From side control (or top side control) with the reverse underhook established, turn to face the opponent's legs.
- The goal is to convert the opponent's symmetrical, square leg position into a top leg and a bottom leg.
- Never try to move an opponent's leg by fighting it with your hand — the hand is never as strong as the leg. Instead, use the hand only to smack/surprise the leg over into the top position in one quick motion, then immediately control it with your own leg.
- Hook the top leg at the Achilles tendon with your foot — controlling it at the far end of the lever makes it very difficult for the opponent to retract, and shifts the fight from hand-strength to leg-strength.

## Rolling Through

- Come up to the knees and lock an outside scorpion on the opponent's ankle, pinning both legs.
- Place the elbow inside the opponent's knee and roll the opponent through — this lands in a strong attacking position (e.g., a cross-body ride).
- From there, one hand can control the hip and one hand the neck; from this position it's a simple matter to hook a foot underneath the opponent's leg and move directly into a favorite back-attack position.
- Two-on-one leg control reappears here: once the top/bottom leg split and hook are in place, catch the opponent's opposite foot too so both legs are controlled before rolling — pass the elbow through and roll for a disorienting effect, landing with a hand under the neck and strong hip/head control.

## Application From Mount (vs. the Elbow Escape)

- The classic elbow escape from mount: opponent turns to a side, traps the mounted athlete's foot, pushes out to trap the knee, then brings the second knee through into guard.
- Counter by deliberately surrendering the foot as bait — offer it willingly (physically lifting it off the mat to invite the escape).
- As the opponent takes the foot, withdraw the knee and center the body back over the opponent's hips.
- Punch the arm through as a reverse underhook, then physically lift the trapped foot out to break the figure-four.
- Catch the second foot, elbow inside, and roll — capturing hip and head control and landing in a strong attacking position.
- Optional detail: uncrossing the opponent's feet first (not essential, but preferred) before catching the second foot.

## Application From Half Guard Top

- From half guard top with the opponent locked high on the legs, tripod and walk across the opponent's body: hand on the hip, elbow inside the opponent's knee, freeing the trapped knee to drive across into a quasi-mounted position (two knees down, but the foot is still held — not a true mount).
- From this quasi-mount, hit the same back-step: bring the leg back through, punch to a reverse underhook.
- Preference is to unhook the opponent's feet directly; if that's difficult, simply hook the opponent's opposite foot instead and roll through the same way.
- Ends in strong head and hip control, ready for the standard back attacks.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The reverse underhook (same-side arm under same-side arm) is the prerequisite for every leg-based rolling entry — it exposes the hips and lower back.
- Never fight an opponent's leg with your hand; use the hand only to initiate the top-leg/bottom-leg split, then control with your own leg at the Achilles tendon.
- Two-on-one leg control (both legs restraining one of the opponent's) applies here just as it does with knee/foot entries.
- This entry works from side control, from countering the mount elbow escape by baiting the foot, and from half guard top via a quasi-mounted back step — making it broadly useful across scramble situations.
