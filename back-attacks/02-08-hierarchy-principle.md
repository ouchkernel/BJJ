# Hierarchy Principle

## Overview

The eighth critical principle. There's a graded hierarchy of hand-fighting positions when working behind an opponent, running from an outright bad position up to the strongest possible grip. Understanding the hierarchy tells you exactly what to look for at every stage of the hand fight.

---

## The two axes of the hierarchy

- **Top vs. bottom hand position.** In general, top hand position is always preferred. If a hand has to end up on bottom, it's tolerable on the strangle side (because the legs can back it up), but top hand position on the control side is always the priority — always fight hardest to keep that one on top.
- **Straight grip vs. cross grip.** Straight grips work; cross grips work better, for the reasons covered under the Specific Grips Principle (the straitjacket effect and the rope-and-knot effect).

## The grading criterion

- The hierarchy is graded by one simple test: how easily can this grip bring the opponent's secondary (defensive) hand — specifically the thumb — below the line of his chest (the pectoral line)?
- Above the pec line, it takes considerable flexibility to hook the opponent's arm.
- Below the pec line, it takes very little flexibility to hook the arm and lock it behind the back.

## The hierarchy, worst to best

1. **Bottom of the hierarchy — two straight grips, both on bottom.** Opponent has top hand position on the strangle side; this is the position you want to escape from as fast as possible, pulling your hand back to reclaim top position.
2. **Adequate — two straight grips, one top (control side) one bottom.** Not great, but workable: from here you can pull the opponent's hand below the chest line and hook over the top into a winning position.
3. **Better — single cross grip (the minimum useful cross grip).** Hold one hand with a cross grip; the opponent's other hand can be anywhere. That alone is enough to trap and eventually convert into a control grip and lock up.
4. **Top of the hierarchy — double cross grip.** The strongest of all the grips. Interestingly, this often arises when the opponent tries to peel off a single cross grip — a reasonable defensive move on his part, but one that risks exposing himself to a double cross grip if it fails, which is dramatically harder to escape than a single.

## Working through it

- From single cross grip: if the opponent doesn't monitor the strangle hand, go straight in and strangle. If he locks onto your wrist to prevent that, bring your foot over, put his hand behind his back, punch through and strip his grip, leaving an uncontested strangle hand.
- From double cross grip: pass the foot over the top, lock in, and again arrive at an uncontested strangle hand ready for the finish.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Grade every hand-fighting position by one test: can you easily get the opponent's secondary hand and thumb below his pec line? Below it is easy to hook; above it requires real flexibility.
- Hierarchy from worst to best: two straight grips on bottom → one straight grip top/one bottom → single cross grip → double cross grip.
- Top hand position on the control side is the priority to protect at all times; bottom hand position is tolerable only on the strangle side.
- The top of the hierarchy — double cross grip — often results directly from the opponent's own attempt to escape a single cross grip.
- Working from a strong position in the hierarchy (single or double cross grip) leads directly to bringing a foot over the trapped arm and arriving at an uncontested strangle hand.
