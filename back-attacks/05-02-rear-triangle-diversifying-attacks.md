# Rear Triangle — Diversifying Attacks

## Overview

The Rear Triangle (Ushiro Sankaku, "triangle from the back") is the first and favorite of the auxiliary systems: a switch from strangling with the arms to strangling with the legs when the Straitjacket System stalls. The transition from a stalled straitjacket position is small and easy, because the same conditions that make the straitjacket possible also make the rear triangle possible. Once the strangle itself is in place, the same control opens into a whole family of joint-lock finishes, so a stalled attack never has to mean a lost position.

---

## The Problem Of Extension

- The central problem of Ushiro Sankaku, like every system in this course, comes down to one thing: extension. You need the opponent's trapped/defensive arm **extended over his head**, not contracted toward his belly button.
- An extended elbow exposes the throat to the strangle and gives the legs their maximum strangulation effect. A contracted elbow protects the opponent — he can push against your own elbow with his, weaken the strangle, and force you into convoluted finishing attempts (crunching forward, reaching and pulling for a leg).
- Those convoluted finishes are the real danger: the more you try them under live conditions, the more movement you generate, and the more chances the opponent gets to turn and slip out of the triangle entirely. Avoid the temptation — always work back toward extension instead.

## Setting Up The 2-On-1

- Once arm-strangling isn't getting a breakthrough, it's fine to abandon that grip entirely. Bring your strangle hand across to the *other* side of the opponent's neck.
- Lift the opponent's wrist and expose the suicide zone of his wrist, matching it to your own suicide zone so the vein lines of both wrists cross.
- As the opponent tries to keep his hand pinned toward his own chest, his arm is bent into a mechanically weak position — identical to an Americana. You're effectively in an arm-wrestling position with the odds in your favor.
- Hug with a 2-on-1 grip: two of your arms against his one. His free arm can't help him because it's trapped underneath your leg.
- Intent: connect his knuckles to your chest. Extend your leg and sit down into a strong position — when he tries to pull his hand back to himself it's like trying to escape an Americana; it just doesn't happen.

## Leg Positioning And Locking The Triangle

- Take your leg over the opponent's shoulder, tilt across to the other side, and kink both legs so your lower legs and feet point in the same direction. Even before the triangle is locked, this leg configuration alone makes it very difficult for him to pull his head free.
- Lock an initial triangle — it won't be tight yet. Reach down, grab your own heel, and hand-assist to a genuinely tight triangle.
- Take your shoelace and hook it behind the opponent's back. Don't let his arm come back down toward his belly button — that's the position that ruins the strangle.
- If he turns out, your leg configuration makes it impossible and actually facilitates the triangle further. If he turns in, your knee blocks him — this is exactly why the legs are locked into this configuration even before the triangle is fully closed.
- Hands trying to grip or pry at your legs have essentially no effect — hands are massively weaker than legs.

## Diversifying Into Joint Locks

There's always going to be a day the strangle itself doesn't get there. From the exact same control (opponent's hands are often already locked together over his head, which is easy to separate), a whole family of finishes opens up without releasing the position:

- **Juji Gatame (basic):** plant your foot flat on the mat and take the opponent's elbow over your hip.
- **Juji Gatame (reinforced):** take the opponent's hand to your own shoulder, block it there, wrap around and put your hands on your own buttocks, then arch — a considerably stronger version of the same lock.
- **Kimura:** lock a Kimura in the same position. Because the opponent's head and arm are already constrained by the triangle, touching his elbow to your abdomen creates immense pressure — his head can't move to relieve it.
- **Americana:** if the arm is bent and Juji Gatame is proving difficult, hug the opponent's elbow and drive forward for very strong elbow pressure.
- **Wrist lock:** control the joint above the wrist (the elbow) first. Put your hand over the opponent's knuckle line, hold the opposite wrist, and slap the wrist down and turned. Close your own elbow over his elbow and go hand over hand for the break — this is a different mechanism from a figure-four wrist lock.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Extension is everything: opponent's elbow overhead exposes the throat; opponent's elbow toward his belly protects it and wrecks your leverage.
- Build the position with a 2-on-1 grip and suicide-zone-to-suicide-zone wrist contact — this puts the opponent's arm in a mechanically weak, Americana-like position before the triangle is even locked.
- Lock the legs into position (kinked, pointing the same direction) before fully closing the triangle — this alone blocks both turning in and turning out.
- If the strangle doesn't land, you don't need to reset: the same control chains directly into Juji Gatame, Kimura, Americana, or a wrist lock.
- Avoid convoluted finishing attempts (crunching forward, chasing legs) — they generate movement that lets the opponent escape. Always work back toward extension instead.
