# Overarm Side — Side Switching Method

## Overview

The wedging method assumes you can trap the arm before committing to the floor. But there will be times you fall into rear mount on the overarm side with the opponent already sitting on your bottom leg, unable to use it to trap the secondary arm in the usual way. This is where side switching becomes essential: using the other, unweighted leg to trap the *primary* defensive arm instead — with several necessary modifications, since this creates two new problems of its own that have to be solved in sequence.

---

## The situation

- Behind the training partner with hooks in — one arm over, one arm under — and the opponent falls to the overarm side.
- He's now sitting on top of the leg on that side, so the ordinary leg-hook method can't be used on his secondary arm.
- Solution: use the other (unweighted) leg to trap the *primary* defensive arm instead — but it takes real modification to make this work.

## Step 1 — Protect the bottom hook first

- Before anything else, post the rear mount or a body triangle — the bottom hook must always be defended. If it's vulnerable and easily passed over, nothing else matters.
- Keep the bottom hook as high as possible, or as reinforced as possible, before proceeding.

## Step 2 — Trap the primary defensive arm with the free leg

1. Take the opponent's primary defensive hand and push it down below the line of the chest.
2. As it drops below the chest line, unhook the legs and place your hamstring *over* the opponent's elbow. This has to be above the elbow — going in below the elbow won't form an effective catch.
3. Bring the heel of your hand inside your own thumb — trust proprioception (prior perception) here: even without being able to see the hand, the body knows where hand and foot need to go to connect.
4. Block over the top of the elbow so it can't move; touch the foot to your own hand, then "limp" the arm free.
5. Cross your feet to maximize tightness. Note: with feet crossed, the foot actually controlling the opponent's arm is the bottom foot.

## Step 3 — Fix the missing control hand

- After trapping the arm this way, there's no control hand left — an unstable position.
- Fix: no matter how tightly the opponent holds on, take a cross grip and pull his wrist; lift the elbow and slide your hand underneath to establish a cross-hand grip.

## Step 4 — Fix the new head/hand problem: the head pommel

- Establishing that cross-hand grip creates a new violation of the most fundamental rule of back control: the strangle arm and the head must always be on *opposite* sides of the opponent's head, or no strangle is possible at all.
- After the previous steps, the strangle hand and the head both end up on the same side of the opponent's head — no strangle is available yet.
- Fix: the **head pommel**. Grab the opponent's head — even if he drives it toward the floor to escape — and bring your elbow forward to pommel the head over to the other side, restoring the strangle arm and head to opposite sides (the "two-sided head trap").

## Step 5 — Finish

- With the secondary arm pinned, the primary arm pinned, and the head pommeled to the correct side, everything is drawn in close.
- Penetrate the neck: chin down, flat fist method, walk the arm all the way through and around.
- Finish with either a one-handed or a two-handed strangle.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Side switching solves the problem of already having fallen to the overarm side with your bottom leg pinned under the opponent, unable to trap the secondary arm the normal way.
- Use the *free* leg to trap the *primary* defensive arm instead, with the hamstring going over (never under) the elbow.
- Trapping the arm this way costs you your control hand — recover it with a cross grip: pull the wrist, lift the elbow, slide the hand underneath.
- That cross-hand grip puts your strangle hand and head on the same side — a fundamental violation. Fix it with a head pommel before attempting anything else.
- Only once the head and strangle arm are back on opposite sides, with both defensive arms pinned, do you proceed to penetrate and finish (one- or two-handed).
- Always defend the bottom hook first, before starting any of this sequence.
