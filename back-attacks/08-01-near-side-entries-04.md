# Near Side Entries 04

## Overview

Continues the near side entry sequence with a foot-first method of establishing the first hook, built off a tight waist breakdown from the turtle position. Establishes the philosophy governing the choice between knee entries and foot entries, then walks through the specific mechanics of a foot entry that exploits an opponent's own escape attempt.

---

## Knee Entries vs. Foot Entries

- General entry philosophy favors low-amplitude methods with maximal body contact and minimal opportunity for the opponent to stand out of the position.
- Knee entry: usually easier and safer for most athletes in most situations, but it's a two-part move — the knee goes in first, then the leg must be extended afterward to fully establish the hook.
- Foot entry: when the foot goes in first, the hook is complete immediately — no second step needed. Foot entries tend to be smoother and faster.
- Favor foot entries when operating with maximal body contact against a broken-down opponent.
- Favor knee entries in uncontrolled situations where the opponent isn't broken down and body contact would have to be sacrificed to attempt a foot entry.

## Tight Waist Breakdown (Setup)

- Starting from turtle position, establish the tight waist: elbow (or wrist — either works) glued into the opponent's hip.
- Transition the body across the opponent's, stepping the near knee from one hip to the other, pulling the opponent over.
- Once balance is felt to break, withdraw the knee and break the opponent down to a hip.
- From here, maintain strong chest-to-back contact but don't lock a full seat belt yet — instead keep the elbow glued to the opponent's hip.

## Forcing the Escape You Want

- If the opponent stays put, go straight into the entries already shown or straight into a stranglehold — no need for anything fancy.
- Against a live opponent trying to return to turtle, holding the elbow tight to the body removes every other escape option: the only mechanically viable way to climb back to turtle is to scissor the legs.
- Naive attempts to just turn or flip up are stopped dead by the elbow.
- The moment the opponent is forced to scissor the legs, that scissoring action creates exactly the space needed for the foot entry.

## Executing the Foot Entry

- As the opponent scissors up, drive the leg forward — the whole shin travels along the mat as it enters, keeping continuous body contact throughout.
- The hook is established as the opponent returns toward turtle, putting you in perfect position to take diagonal control (the second hook) immediately.
- Explicitly avoid picking a leg up and stepping a hook in — described as a disaster; the correct picture is the shin sliding the entire way in along the floor.
- Once the first hook is set, lock in diagonal control (both hooks) so that whichever way the opponent goes, you're already stuck to the back in a finishing position.

## Common Mistakes

- Picking the opponent's leg up and stepping the hook in, rather than sliding the shin in along the mat.
- Trying to enter with the foot against a settled, non-broken-down opponent — sacrifices body contact and invites getting thrown or scrambled off.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Knee entries are two-part (knee in, then extend); foot entries are one-part and immediately complete — but only safe with strong body contact against a broken-down opponent.
- The tight waist breakdown, using elbow-to-hip pressure, mechanically forces a scissoring opponent into the one escape path that creates the space for a foot entry.
- Slide the shin in along the mat — never pick a leg up and throw the hook in.
- Elite back-attackers move fluidly between knee and foot entries, reading the conditions for each.
