# Limitations Of The Straitjacket System

## Overview

Before teaching the Straitjacket System (SJS) in detail, the honest limitation of the system is named up front: it is a one-part-of-you-attacking-one-part-of-them tool, and that concentration is exactly what makes it beatable if it's the only tool in the box.

---

## The core limitation

- The SJS uses only one part of your body — your arms — to work into an attack on one part of the opponent's body: the neck.
- No matter how well the legs trap the opponent's arms, this is still a fairly small piece of "real estate" to be attacking.
- When a large set of the opponent's defenses (both hands, chin/jaw) are all focused on defending one small area, it becomes hard to get a breakthrough.

## Why this matters for system design

- Working behind an opponent, it's critical to be able to do more than have one part of your body attacking one part of theirs — attacks need to be distributed over the opponent's whole body so you become unpredictable in nature and scope.
- This is exactly the role of the auxiliary systems covered later in the series: as powerful and competition-proven as the SJS is, offering only a single-target system would be a real gap. The auxiliary systems let attacks spread over the length and breadth of the opponent's body, making the breakthrough far easier because the opponent can no longer pool all his defense on one target.
- The relationship between the SJS (the main, central focus of the back attack system) and the auxiliary systems (which distribute attacks over a much wider area) is a central theme of this whole video series.

---

## Key Takeaways

- SJS limitation: one attacking tool (the arms) against one small defensive target (the neck) — concentrated defense is easier for the opponent to mount.
- The fix is not a different single technique — it's a *system* that pairs SJS with auxiliary attacks spread across the opponent's whole body.
- Keep this main-system/auxiliary-system relationship in mind throughout the rest of the series; it's the organizing idea behind why the course is structured the way it is.
