# Technical Notes On Rear Naked Strangle

## Overview

A shift from pure strangle mechanics to the technical details that determine whether a rear naked strangle succeeds or stalls out. The rear naked strangle can come unstuck in a number of common, avoidable ways — usually right at the moment you transition from a control hand to a support hand. Getting behind an opponent, taking them down, getting hooks in, and then failing to finish is one of the most frustrating outcomes in the position; this chapter is about eliminating the two most common causes of that failure.

---

## Starting position and the critical transition

- When you first get behind an opponent: a control hand goes underneath the opponent's arm, and a strangle hand goes over the opponent's shoulder. Between the two elbows, you control the movement of the opponent's upper body.
- Once neck penetration is achieved and the strangle begins to close, the control hand has to be relinquished — it becomes a support hand, freeing the strangle hand to cross over into the finish (rotational or contracting, depending on preference).
- Managing this transition — losing a control hand, gaining a support hand — is where a large share of failed strangles go wrong.

## The two common mistakes

- **Mistake 1 — strangle hand to bicep.** An extremely common error, seen even at surprisingly high levels. Grabbing your own bicep effectively offers your support hand to the opponent, who grabs it and yanks it down the body. Result: no control hand left, and the opponent can start turning inside you. Disaster.
- **Mistake 2 — poor support-hand placement.** Commonly, people place the support hand on top of the opponent's skull. The opponent can easily strip this hand down, again leaving no control hand in place and allowing the opponent to turn inside.

## The correct method

- Don't relinquish the control hand too early.
- **The hidden hand principle**: the strangle hand always hides the thumb line — specifically, hide the thumb line behind the opponent's neck. If the opponent gets access to the thumb line, he can always stop the strangle.
- Use the head to compensate for the loss of the control hand. If the head is back when the control hand is released, there's a large gap for the opponent's head to turn and move out through. Instead, drive the head in and pressure with it — close the circle around the opponent's neck by putting the chin over your own wrist, and use the head to push against the direction of the opponent's escape (the strangle hand's fingers point the direction he'd escape toward).
- Never go hand-to-bicep. Instead, go **wrist over wrist**: when withdrawing the control hand, bring your wrist over your own wrist — cross wrists, never cross arms.
- From the cross-wrist position, the elbow goes in front. It doesn't matter if the elbow is surrendered to the opponent — what matters is not surrendering the wrist, "the end of the lever."
- Punch across so the hands land in the correct final position: the strangle hand goes to the upper bicep, and — checked from the camera/onlooker's viewpoint in front of you — the fingernails of the support hand should be visible. If the fingernails can't be seen, penetration is insufficient and the arm needs to go further through.
- From here, the chin goes over the wrist of the support hand, looking in the direction of the strangle. Rotational finishes bring the elbow over the shoulder; contracting finishes bring the elbows together going forward — either works, this is a matter of personal preference (rotational is favored here).

## Full sequence recap

1. Get behind the opponent and penetrate.
2. Hide the thumb line.
3. Relinquish the control hand while the head compensates for the loss (drive in, pressure against the escape direction).
4. Go wrist over wrist, elbow over.
5. Everything falls into place for the finish — rotational preferred.

## Applies from hooks-in position and on both sides

- The same sequence was demonstrated from a genuine hooks-in position (not just an artificial kneeling setup): penetrate, withdraw the control hand while the head locks against the escape direction, wrist over wrist, elbow over shoulder, fingernails visible on the support side, rotational finish — even when the opponent grabs both hands to the forearm to block.
- The same mechanics were also shown on the so-called "weak side" (falling to the underside): the identical wrist-over-wrist, elbow-over-shoulder sequence applies regardless of which side you fell to.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Most failed rear naked strangles fail at the control-hand-to-support-hand transition, not at the strangle itself.
- Never take the strangle hand to your own bicep, and never place the support hand on top of the skull — both offer your support hand to the opponent for free.
- Hide the thumb line behind the opponent's neck; use head pressure (driven against the opponent's escape direction) to compensate the instant the control hand is released.
- Transition with wrist over wrist, never crossing arms — surrender the elbow if you must, never the wrist.
- Confirm depth of penetration visually: the support hand's fingernails should be visible from in front of you.
- The same sequence works from a kneeling setup, from real hooks-in position, and on either side (over or underside).
