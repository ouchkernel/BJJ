# Rolling Entries 01

## Overview

Introduces rolling entries as a distinct, broader alternative to knee and foot entries — using the momentum of a roll, rather than a discrete leg movement, to separate an opponent from a tight turtle and land in a back-control position. Covers the two seat belt–based rolling entries: the shoulder-over-head roll and the double-underhook/body-lock roll, plus the correction needed when a seat belt can't be locked in the first place.

---

## Rolling Entries as a Category

- Knee and foot entries account for the large majority of the best entries seen in modern competition, but they aren't the only methods.
- Rolling entries are probably the broadest category of alternatives — several distinct ways to roll an opponent into hooks without a discrete knee or foot entry first.
- Rolling entries intentionally use higher amplitude and more momentum than the low-amplitude philosophy favored elsewhere — more moving parts and more chance of slippage or separation in principle, but effective when executed correctly.

## Seat Belt Roll: Shoulder Over the Head

- Starting from a strong seat belt on a tightly compacted turtle opponent, instead of keeping chest and head away from the opponent, raise the shoulder and drive it over the top of the opponent's head.
- Put your own head down on the mat as the knees come up off the floor; in one motion, lift the rear leg and turn the body through, rolling the opponent over.
- The momentum of the roll is what separates the opponent's knees from the chest, opening the door to throw a foot across the body into hooks, a body triangle, or a cross-body ride.
- Variant grip: lock the seat belt on the near side of the neck (bringing the far-side arm all the way through) rather than the traditional far-side lock — this sets up the shoulder-over-head roll more cleanly. Move the shoulder to dominate the crown of the head, head down on the mat, and the roll creates a whiplash effect that carries the opponent through into a high tripod finish.

## Crab Ride Adaptation (Disciplined Opponents)

- More experienced opponents know that knees away from the chest is an invitation to hooks, and will keep the knees tucked in tight even while being rolled through.
- If the roll lands with the knees still tucked, hooks can't go straight in — transition to a crab ride instead: get the feet in behind the opponent and use your foot to lengthen one of the opponent's contracted legs.
- Once one leg is lengthened, pass your foot to the inside and fall across to the opposite side, landing in the same attacking sequences used elsewhere in the system.
- Alternate version: with feet in close behind the opponent, place a foot in behind the opponent's leg as a hook and extend it out — your leg can always out-leverage a tucked leg from this position.

## Seat Belt Roll: Double Underhooks / Body Lock

- A different momentum roll done from double underhooks or an open-handed body lock (rather than a seat belt), with the grip set high underneath the opponent's chest.
- Because the roll is lined up directly with the opponent's body rather than out to one side, it generates tremendous momentum, throwing the opponent through into position for hooks, a cross-body ride, or other back attacks.

## When the Seat Belt Can't Be Locked

- If the opponent hand-fights and blocks the seat belt from locking in front, release and go to the one area that can't be hand-fought — behind the opponent's arms and armpits.
- Lock the hands there palm-to-palm (no thumbs).
- Drive body weight forward into a high tripod: head down on the mat, feet leaving the mat, body rising up and over the opponent.
- The key mechanical point: throw your own body first and let the opponent's body follow as a consequence — create separation between the two bodies so one goes first and pulls the other, rather than trying to yank the opponent over while both bodies stay locked together.
- The common mistake here is simply hooking and pulling with no separation — described as leading to a disaster where the entry stalls or collapses.
- Done correctly, throwing the body through breaks the opponent down to the mat, opening the fight for the seat belt, hooks, and the rest of the winning sequences.

## Common Mistakes

- Trying to muscle the opponent over by hooking and pulling without first creating body separation and going yourself first.
- Insufficient momentum on the shoulder-over-head roll, leaving the opponent's knees tucked with no clean hook lane (though this is manageable via the crab ride adaptation).

---

## Key Takeaways

- Rolling entries trade the low-amplitude philosophy for momentum — a legitimate, high-value alternative to knee/foot entries, especially valuable out of scrambles.
- The shoulder-over-head seat belt roll uses momentum to separate the opponent's knees from the chest, opening a foot-across-the-body entry.
- If the opponent keeps knees tucked through the roll, don't force it — switch to a crab ride and lengthen a leg to get the hook in after the fact.
- When a seat belt can't be locked, go behind the arms, get a palm-to-palm grip, and throw your own body first — never just hook and pull.
