# Alignment And Deficit Problem

## Overview

Having covered the preliminaries (left/right control, control hand vs. strangle hand, the head trap), this chapter names the two central problems that the rest of the Straitjacket System exists to solve: the **Alignment Problem** and the **Deficit Problem**. Both are stated conceptually here — the detailed solutions come later in the series. Critically, the two problems are not sequential: in live rolling, they have to be solved simultaneously.

---

## Building a system means naming its central problems

- Building any system starts with identifying the central problems of a given position, then building a set of solutions that, at sufficient complexity, becomes a system.
- There are two central problems associated with the rear-mounted position. Overcoming them is essentially the whole story of the Straitjacket System.

## Problem 1: The Alignment Problem

- Once you get behind an opponent in rear mount, your two spines tend to line up in a straight line. Hooks and upper-body grips are designed to keep you roughly lined up with the opponent.
- Almost all of the opponent's escapes are attempts to break this alignment.
- The head trap **is** the basis of alignment: when a strangle hand is kept on one side and the head on the other, you stay lined up with the opponent no matter which way you fall together.
- The opponent's goal is to get a hold of the dangerous strangle hand and, from there, break alignment — e.g., as you fall to one side and he works his body over your feet, the alignment starts to break. Critically, this always starts with the head trap.
- As the opponent works further away, your chest separates from his back — that separation (chest-to-back connection) is the fundamental measure of alignment. As the angle between you increases, the chance of successfully getting through to a strangle keeps diminishing.
- The overall goal behind an opponent is simple to state, even if hard to execute: **stay aligned.**

## Problem 2: The Deficit Problem

- Possibly the single most important problem addressed by the whole back attack system.
- Rear mount is repeatedly described as the best position in submission grappling — and yet, once you actually get there, something puzzling shows up: count the offensive and defensive instruments available to each athlete.
- **Your offense:** most of the time, one arm is tied up underneath the opponent's arm as a control hand, leaving only one arm as a strangle arm — one offensive instrument. With a body triangle locking the lower body, you could be more liberal with the control arm and potentially work with two strangle arms at once — two offensive instruments at most.
- **Their defense:** the opponent has up to three defensive instruments — a left hand, a right hand, and the jaw/chin. Even when you're controlling one of his arms, he still has one free defensive hand plus the jaw against your single offensive instrument.
- The count, no matter how you configure your arms, always favors the defense: 2 offensive instruments vs. 3 defensive instruments (both hands + jaw free), or 1 offensive instrument vs. 2 defensive instruments (one hand + jaw, if you're controlling his other arm). You are always outnumbered.
- This is presented as genuinely surprising and troubling given that back position is supposed to be the ultimate attacking position — and yet in the context of a match, you're outnumbered every single time you use it. A large part of the rest of the back system is dedicated to overcoming this deficit.

## The two problems run concurrently

- The alignment problem and the deficit problem sit right next to each other and have to be solved at the same time — this is not a sequential "solve one, then the other" process.
- In live combat, the opponent is trying to escape while you're simultaneously trying to hold him and strangle him. You can learn the two problems separately for the sake of instruction, but in live training and competition you have to run solutions to both side by side.
- The series proceeds by looking at the alignment problem first, then coming back to the deficit problem in later chapters.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Alignment Problem:** keeping your chest connected to the opponent's back and preserving the head trap as the basis of staying lined up; the opponent's whole escape strategy is built around breaking this alignment.
- **Deficit Problem:** you almost always have fewer offensive instruments (1–2 strangle arms) than the opponent has defensive instruments (up to 3: two hands + jaw) — back position, despite being the best position in the sport, leaves you numerically outnumbered every time.
- These two problems must be solved simultaneously in live rolling, not one after the other — the rest of the Straitjacket System is essentially the set of solutions to both, starting with alignment.
