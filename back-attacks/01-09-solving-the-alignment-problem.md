# Solving The Alignment Problem — Understanding

## Overview

Before offering any fixes, this chapter builds a precise picture of exactly how an opponent breaks alignment from the back. The escape is not random — it is a fixed, sequential, four-step process. Understanding the steps in order is what makes the later solutions (protecting the bottom hook, through movement, switching arms) make sense: each solution exists to interrupt one specific step, and the earlier you interrupt it, the less ground you give up.

---

## What alignment actually means

- In rear mount, the attacker's spine and the opponent's spine are roughly lined up in a straight line. Hooks and upper-body grips exist largely to preserve that line.
- The **head trap** — strangle arm on one side of the opponent's head, attacker's own head on the other side, opponent's head caught between them — is the practical basis of alignment. As long as the head trap holds, a rear naked strangle stays viable no matter which way the pair falls.
- Every escape the opponent attempts has one ultimate goal: get the attacker's chest off their back and, critically, get the strangle arm and the attacker's head onto the *same* side of their own head. The instant that happens, the strangle threat is dead and the attacker has to scramble to something else.

## The opponent's opening choice: which side to fall to

- When the pair falls to one side, the opponent wants to fall toward the **open side** — wherever there is a gap between the control hand and the rest of the attacker's body. That gap is the route the opponent's head can travel through to break the head trap.
- Falling the other way (into the arm) does nothing for the opponent — he simply falls into the arm and the head trap is never threatened.

## The predictable four-step escape sequence

1. **Beat the bottom hook.** Falling to a side converts the two symmetric hooks into a bottom hook (low, near the mat) and a top hook. With conventional hooks, even a small movement is enough for the opponent to slide over the low one. This is always step one — nothing else in the sequence can happen until this step succeeds.
2. **Beat the bottom knee.** The knees, not the feet, are the real locus of lower-body control — as long as the opponent is between the knees, the position can be recovered with minimal effort. Once the hook is beaten, the opponent's next move is always to slide across and beyond the knee.
3. **Beat the head.** With the hook and knee gone, the head trap is still technically intact (strangle arm and head still on opposite sides) — so there's still a thread of a strangle. The opponent's next job is to bring his head forward and swing it across to the other side, which finally breaks the head trap.
4. **Beat the inside shoulder.** The last step: bring the elbow up between the two bodies and drop the inside shoulder to the mat. Once that shoulder touches down, the attacker has only "the appearance" of back control — there's no workable angle left for a strangle, and it's time to abandon the position and reset rather than fight for it.

## Why the sequence matters

- The steps are strictly ordered and cumulative — the opponent cannot skip ahead. If he never beats the bottom hook, he can never reach the knee; if he never beats the knee, he can never reach the head.
- This gives the attacker a clear hierarchy of priorities: stopping the sequence at step 1 is far cheaper than trying to recover after step 3. The earlier the interruption, the less has to be rebuilt.
- Knowing the exact procedure in advance — rather than reacting to an opponent's escape as if it were unpredictable — is what makes shutting it down dramatically easier. This understanding is the foundation for every solution that follows.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Alignment = chest connected to the opponent's back + head trap intact (strangle arm and attacker's head on opposite sides of the opponent's head).
- The opponent always falls toward the open side — the gap next to the control hand — because that's the only route to break the head trap.
- The escape is a fixed four-step sequence: beat the bottom hook → beat the bottom knee → beat the head → beat the inside shoulder (game over).
- Each step is a prerequisite for the next — stopping the sequence as early as possible (ideally at step 1) is always cheaper than recovering later.
